# UNIRA V6.2 — France: sector labour hours, output and the 30-hour week

**Status:** empirically anchored *accounting arithmetic*: a sector model for France, plus a 30-country labour-reserve comparison. It is not a forecast, not a general-equilibrium model, and not evidence for or against UNIRA's overall feasibility. All numbers below are generated by `run_all.py`; see `outputs/REPORT_TABLES.md`.
**Base year:** 2023 (latest non-provisional national-accounts year at retrieval, 2026-11-03). 2024 (provisional) is used as a robustness check.

## 1. Question

V6.1 left two open problems. It had no hours data covering all workers, so it ran 1,400/1,500/1,600 h sensitivity values. It also applied a full-time-only benchmark (1,656 h) to all 30.5 m employed people, which its own caveats prohibited (see `audit/reproducibility_audit.md`). V6.2 asks:

> Using official, year-matched statistics covering **all** employed persons, how many hours does France work by sector? If usual weekly hours are capped at 30 (the UNIRA rule), how many fewer hours is that? What hourly-productivity increase, additional workforce or output reduction would offset the difference?

## 2. Data (all retrieved from the Eurostat API; hashes in `data/raw/raw_manifest.csv`)

- **Hours and persons, all employed, by NACE section:** `nama_10_a64_e` (national accounts, domestic concept, transmitted by INSEE). France 2023: **45.60 bn hours**, **30.38 m persons**, i.e. 1,501 h/person. Employees: 37.79 bn h / 27.05 m. Self-employed: 7.81 bn h / 3.32 m.
- **Value added:** `nama_10_a64`, current prices and chain-linked 2020 volumes.
- **Weekly hours by full-time/part-time and status:** EU-LFS `lfsa_ewhun2` (main job, usual hours).
- **Validation:** `lfsa_epgan2` (part-time persons) and `lfsi_sla_a` (labour-market slack).
- **INSEE cross-check (audit/source_verification.csv):** INSEE's 2025 LFS figures for full-time employees are confirmed: 1,656 h/yr and 38.9 h/week. The same page reports **966 h/yr for part-time employees and 1,903 h/yr for self-employed**. INSEE publishes no all-worker annual average.

## 3. Method (`src/unira_v62/model.py`)

For each sector *s* and group *g* (employees; self-employed incl. family workers):

1. LFS gives mean usual weekly hours for all (uT), full-time (uF) and part-time (uP) workers. The part-time share follows from the identity uT = q·uP + (1−q)·uF. **Validation:** the inferred q matches Eurostat's direct part-time counts to within 0.6 percentage points in every sector (check `inferred_part_time_share_matches_lfsa_epgan2`).
2. Cap usual hours at c = 30: retained weekly hours R = q·min(uP,c) + (1−q)·min(uF,c).
3. **Primary annual method ("constant absence"):** H′ = H·R/uT. Annual hours fall in proportion to weekly hours, and leave, holidays and sickness are unchanged. The method uses only weekly *ratios*, so it is unaffected by the ~10% level gap between national-accounts and LFS annual hours (§5.3).
4. Required hourly-productivity gain at fixed output and fixed persons = H/H′ − 1. Additional persons with the same hours profile = Σ_g N_g(H_g/H′_g − 1). Break-even avoidable output = 1 − H′/H.
5. Historical benchmark: CAGR of real VA per hour (CLV20 / hours), 2015–2019 and 2015–2023. Years needed = ln(1+gain)/ln(1+trend).

**Bias direction, with its limits.** min(·,30) is concave, so applying it to group means overstates retained hours (proved in a test). The reduction is therefore a lower bound **only with respect to hours dispersion within the full-time and part-time groups**. Other simplifications push the other way:
- full compliance with the cap is assumed (French full-timers report 38.9 usual hours under a 35 h legal week);
- RTT absences are kept (the 2-week sensitivity lowers the result by 4.1 points);
- second-job hours are scaled by the main-job ratio.

The net direction of bias of the headline is **unknown** (independent code review, `audit/v62_code_review.md`).

## 4. Results (2023)

| scenario | hours after | required hourly productivity gain | extra persons (same profile) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Observed | 45.60 bn | 0 | 0 |
| **Primary:** cap 30 h for employees, self-employed unchanged | 37.97 bn | **20.1%** (LFS rounding bounds 20.0–20.2%) | 6.8 m |
| Cap 30 h for all workers incl. self-employed | 35.33 bn | 29.1% | 8.5 m |
| Primary + full-timers regain 2 weeks (RTT days lapse; synthetic magnitude) | 39.31 bn | 16.0% | 5.4 m |
| V6.1 convention (FT annual hours capped at 30×46), employees — **not a 30 h cap**: with ≈38.7 observed effective weeks it permits ≈35.7 h per week actually worked | 42.60 bn | 7.0% | 2.3 m |
| Sensitivity: 35 h cap, all workers | 40.40 bn | 12.9% | 3.6 m |

2024 (provisional) gives 19.9% for the primary scenario and 28.8% for the all-workers scenario: the result is stable across years.

**Sector detail (primary).** The required gain ranges from 7.7% (agriculture, where most hours are self-employed and uncapped in this scenario) to 30.4% (electricity/gas). Sectors with many part-timers need less: Q health and social work 16.9%, S other services 14.3%. Additional persons are concentrated in trade (0.81 m), health and social work (0.78 m), manufacturing (0.73 m), public administration (0.66 m), professional services (0.56 m), administrative support (0.53 m) and education (0.43 m). Essential public services O+P+Q alone need **1.87 m** additional persons.

**Against history (both windows reported).** Economy-wide real VA per hour grew 0.57%/yr over 2015–19 and only 0.06%/yr over 2015–23. The second window includes France's post-COVID productivity slowdown. A 20.1% gain therefore takes about **32 years** at the 2015–19 trend and about **301 years** at the 2015–23 trend. Neither trend is a forecast. Both show that ordinary productivity growth cannot deliver the change quickly. Eight sections (A, D, H, I, L, N, S, T) had non-positive measured trends in 2015–19, so trend growth never closes their gap. O, P and Q are marked `nonmarket_input_based`: their volume output is largely measured from inputs, so their "productivity trend" says almost nothing about feasible efficiency.

**Against labour slack (same-profile framing).** In 2023 France had 2.30 m unemployed (15–74) and 4.80 m in total LFS slack. That total includes 1.25 m underemployed part-timers who are *already employed*. The primary scenario's 6.8 m additional "same-profile" persons exceeds the whole slack. That framing is too pessimistic, though: new entrants can work 30 h, and underemployed part-timers can move up to 30 h. §4b models this properly.

**With a behavioural offset.** If a share θ of lost hours were recovered through higher hourly productivity, the residual requirement in the primary scenario would be 17.7% (θ=0.1), 15.5% (θ=0.2), 13.3% (θ=0.3) or 9.1% (θ=0.5). θ is **not estimated here**. The evidence review is in `audit/hours_productivity_evidence.md`.

## 4b. Labour reserve: people who could work in a 30-hour economy (EU27 + IS/NO/CH)

**Rationale (project direction, 2026-11-03).** A 30 h week is not only a cut in hours. It may make work accessible to adults excluded today: the unemployed, people available but not seeking, involuntary part-timers, older workers, carers. `src/unira_v62/labour_reserve.py` quantifies this with Eurostat data for 30 countries (persons aged 20–64, 2023).

**Pools.** Each country's 30 h hours gap (same constant-absence method; France top-down 20.2% vs sector bottom-up 20.1%, checked) is compared with:
- T1, the unemployed;
- T2, the extended labour force (seeking-but-not-available, available-but-not-seeking);
- T3, underemployed part-timers raised to 30 h.

Two framings are tested, and they are **alternatives, never summed** (checked):
- **slack** = T1+T2 (+T3 hours);
- **benchmark** = max(employment-rate convergence to the mean of the three best performers, T1+T2) (+T3 hours). In 2023 the best performers were IS, NL and CH, at 84.0%.

Entrants work 30 h/week. Effective weeks cancel out of the coverage share (proved in a test), so the result does not depend on the NA–LFS level gap.

| | required gain, no mobilisation | share of gap closed: slack | share closed: benchmark | residual gain (benchmark) | residual, entrants at 75% productivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| France | 20.2% | 51% | 59% | **7.5%** | 10.4% |
| EU27 | 21.4% | 43% | 50% | **9.8%** | 12.5% |

(Full 30-country table: `outputs/REPORT_TABLES.md` §7.)

**What this shows**
1. **Mobilising today's non-workers could plausibly cover around half of France's 30 h gap.** The required productivity gain falls from about 20% to about 7.5–10%. At the 2015–19 trend that is roughly 13–17 years rather than 32, and it is in the range of what a θ offset or modest output re-composition could cover. **This makes the 30 h rule much less implausible than §4 alone suggests.**
2. **The reserve is very unevenly distributed.** Spain, Italy and Greece hold large reserves: 55–126% of their gaps under the benchmark framing. Countries that already employ most adults (Germany 25%, Czechia 22%, Iceland 19%, Sweden 35%) have little left to mobilise. In those countries the gap must be covered by productivity, output choices or migration. For a *worldwide* federation, this is an argument for international coordination of labour and production, with all the equity issues that raises (handover/07, test 9).
3. **Netherlands evidence.** The NL has the shortest average usual week (31.3 h) and one of the highest employment rates (83.5%; 75.0% at ages 55–64). Its own 30 h gap is the smallest in the sample (12.5%). Across 30 countries, shorter average hours correlate moderately with higher employment (r = −0.36). That is descriptive only: part-time prevalence, demography, institutions and income confound it.

**Why these are upper bounds**
- Every person in the pool is assumed to take a 30 h job in the right sector and place, with the right skills. Pools include people with health limitations, care duties and long-term unemployment.
- Entrant productivity equal to incumbents is optimistic; the 75% variant is illustrative (SYNTHETIC).
- Childcare, eldercare and training for new entrants also need hours. Those hours are part of the gap, not free.
- Benchmark convergence took years where it happened. No transition path is modelled.
- Inactivity reasons (care, own illness; `lfsa_igar`) are reported as context only. They are *not* added to the supply, because their overlap with T2 is unknown.

## 4c. Evidence-graded pathway for France (behavioural offset + mobilisation)

The literature review (`audit/hours_productivity_evidence.md`, 22 sources, 2 marked unverified) gives these ranges for θ, the share of lost hours recovered by higher hourly productivity:
- **Market sectors, short run:** 0.10–0.35.
- **Health, care and education:** 0–0.15.
- **No support for θ > 0.5** in an economy-wide cut to 30 h. All credible evaluations cover cuts of ≤10% from ≥39 h, and below about 35 h output looks close to proportional to hours (Pencavel; Collewet & Sauermann).

The review also finds that **hours reductions alone did not create net jobs** without subsidies: France's 35 h jobs were tied to payroll-tax relief, and Chile, Japan, Portugal and later French studies find none. Mobilising the labour reserve is therefore *not* automatic. It requires deliberate institutions (training, childcare and eldercare, accessible jobs, regional matching), and its take-up is a SYNTHETIC parameter until estimated.

| case | θ market / care | take-up of reserve | entrant productivity | gap closed by θ | by mobilisation | **residual required gain** | years of 2015–19 trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| low | 0.10 / 0 | 25% | 75% | 8% | 11% | **15.6%** | 26 |
| central | 0.20 / 0.05 | 50% | 75% | 18% | 22% | **11.2%** | 19 |
| high | 0.35 / 0.15 | 100% | 100% | 32% | 59% | **1.6%** | 3 |

**Reading.** Under central assumptions, a 30 h week in France at constant output still needs about 11% higher output per hour: roughly two decades of 2015–19 trend growth. Alternatively, genuinely avoidable output of similar size would have to be identified (V6.3). The high case almost closes the gap, but only with full mobilisation of the entire benchmark reserve. That would be historically unprecedented, but it is not physically impossible. Health and care stay the binding sector: θ is near zero there, and Iceland's national rollout required new hiring, mostly in healthcare.

## 4d. Alternative rule: an enforced 35-hour week (question raised 2026-11-03)

**Definition matters.** France's *legal* reference week is already 35 h, but full-time employees report 38.9 usual hours (overtime, "forfait-jours" managers, RTT compensation). "Keeping 35 h" therefore has two meanings:
- **Keeping today's rules:** no change in hours, no requirement. This is the observed baseline A.
- **Enforcing 35 h as an actual cap** on usual hours, like the UNIRA 30 h rule. This is the scenario modelled here, with the same method, data and checks.

| France 2023, employees capped | 30 h | 35 h |
|---|---|---|
| hours lost | 7.63 bn (−16.7%) | 3.30 bn (−7.2%) |
| required productivity gain, no offsets | 20.1% | **7.8%** |
| years of 2015–19 trend / 2015–23 trend | 32 / 301 | 13 / 124 |
| extra persons, same profile | 6.8 m | 2.6 m (health/social work 0.27 m) |
| labour reserve, share of gap (slack / benchmark) | 51% / 59% | **142% / 161%** |
| evidence-graded residual: low / central / high | 15.6% / 11.2% / 1.6% | **3.9% / 0.2% / 0%** |
| EU27, reserve share of gap (slack / benchmark) | 43% / 50% | 106% / 123% |

θ cases for 35 h use the range from the reforms that were actually evaluated: 39→35 h gives θ 0.2–0.6 (French Aubry laws, Portugal 1996). The upper end comes from subsidised volunteer firms. So the 35 h estimates rest on **stronger evidence** than the 30 h ones, which extrapolate beyond any evaluated reform.

**Reading.**
- An enforced 35 h week is **within reach on current evidence.** France's existing labour reserve is about 1.4–1.6 times the hours it would remove. Under central assumptions (θ 0.4 market / 0.05 care, half the reserve mobilised at 75% productivity), output is maintained without additional productivity growth.
- The surplus reserve could go to *more* output: care, renovation, the energy transition.
- A 30 h week remains about 2.5 times harder, and it depends on mobilisation at a scale never observed.
- **Health and care are still the constraint at 35 h:** about 0.27 m extra persons, with θ near zero.
- The mobilisation caveat from §4c is unchanged. Cutting hours does not create jobs by itself.

**Design decision (user, 2026-11-03): UNIRA keeps 30 h from the start.** The staged 35 h → 30 h path is rejected. The 35 h results are retained only as a comparator showing how much harder the 30 h rule is.

Consequences for the research programme:
- The 30 h residual (central about 11%, range 1.6–15.6%) must be closed **in the first years**, not over a transition.
- That puts the burden on four things:
  - (i) mobilisation at high take-up from day one;
  - (ii) avoidable output (V6.3);
  - (iii) health and care staffing, where θ is about 0;
  - (iv) transparent acceptance of any temporary output shortfall, with essential needs protected first.
- Falsification test 1 (handover/07) is evaluated against **30 h from year 1**.

## 5. What this does and does not show

### 5.1 Findings (appropriately qualified)
1. **The V6.1 annual convention is not a 30 h rule, and its 20% figure was right only by coincidence.** Assuming 46 working weeks implies a 1,380 h target. Observed effective weeks for employees are about 39 (national accounts) or about 42.6 (INSEE LFS, 1,656/38.9). At the national-accounts level, 1,380 h allows about 35.7 h per week actually worked, and the convention gives 7%. At the LFS level it gives 20%. Because of that instability, V6.2 uses the level-invariant ratio method.
2. **Sector averages mislead.** In O, P and Q, average annual hours per worker (1,140–1,297) are already *below* 1,380 because of part-time work and school calendars. An average-based model would wrongly report no requirement. Separating full-time from part-time is essential.
3. **Without mobilising new workers, a 30 h week at constant output needs roughly +16% to +29% output per hour in France**, depending on treatment of the self-employed and of RTT days. That is decades of recent trend growth. **Bringing today's unemployed and excluded adults into 30 h jobs could cover roughly half of it (§4b)**, leaving about 7.5–10% for employees, under optimistic take-up assumptions.
4. **Combined evidence-graded pathway (§4c):** with literature-based θ and partial mobilisation, the residual requirement is about 11% (central; range 1.6–15.6%).
5. **This does not falsify UNIRA's 30 h rule.** It quantifies what must be true for the rule to hold. Some combination of the following must close the gap:
   - (a) genuinely avoidable output, which needs physical and needs evidence (V6.3–6.5);
   - (b) productivity responses to shorter hours (θ);
   - (c) labour-force mobilisation (§4b: up to ~50–60% of the gap in France, 43–50% EU-wide), plus migration;
   - (d) investment and automation, with lead times;
   - (e) accepting lower measured output.

   Each is now a quantified, testable requirement instead of an implicit assumption.
6. **A/B/C counterfactuals:** in labour arithmetic alone, a reformed market economy adopting a 30 h week (B) and UNIRA (C) face *identical* requirements. Any UNIRA advantage must come from (a)–(e) and must be shown with matched resources, per the master specification.

### 5.2 Limitations
- **Usual hours, not distributions.** LFS gives group means only, so the reduction is a lower bound with respect to dispersion. The `pt_tail` sensitivity (15% of part-timers at 32 h) adds just 0.2 points.
- **Main job versus all jobs.** LFS hours refer to the main job, while NA hours include secondary jobs. The weekly ratio is applied to all NA hours.
- **Self-employed inconsistency.** NA hours per self-employed person (≈2,300–2,450 h/yr) exceed INSEE's LFS figure (1,903 h) by about 21%. One plausible cause is secondary-job self-employment: hours are counted, but persons are classified by main job. This is unverified. Because of it, self-employed results are less reliable, and the primary scenario leaves them uncapped. UNIRA has not yet decided how the 30 h rule applies to the self-employed (open decision).
- **Domestic versus national concept** (cross-border workers), and LFS sampling error, are not propagated beyond publication rounding.
- **Sector T (households as employers)** has implausible implied effective weeks (128). NA and LFS count multi-employer domestic workers differently. T is small (0.27 bn h).
- **"Additional persons"** assumes recruits have the same hours profile and productivity as incumbents. Skills, training lead times (e.g. nursing), geography and health are not modelled. No transition costs are computed.
- **VA at risk** (€448 bn at current prices, primary) is a market-price benchmark only. It is not a welfare loss and not a UNIRA physical cost.
- **Section level only** (NACE A*21). Hospital versus social-care (Q86 vs Q87–88) and occupation-level staffing need A64 and occupational data.
- **Unpaid domestic and care work** is outside national-accounts hours. A 30 h week may shift time into it, which is not counted here.

### 5.3 NA–LFS level reconciliation (unresolved, documented)
NA hours per employee × LFS weekly ratios imply 1,501 h for full-time employees in 2025, against INSEE's 1,656 (ratio 0.906). For part-time employees the figures are 897 h against 966 (0.929). The full-time/part-time *ratios* agree within 2.5% (1.67 against 1.71), which supports the ratio method. The level gap reflects definitional differences between national accounts and survey hours and is not resolved in V6.2.

## 6. Falsification register (handover/07, test 1)
- **Pass/fail criterion proposed for V6.3+:** the 30 h rule is *physically feasible in France* only if, in the matched comparison, the sum of (a)+(b)+(c)+(d) closes the 16–29% gap without unmet essential needs, within an explicitly stated transition period. Each component must rest on evidence classes other than SYNTHETIC_ASSUMPTION. Component (c) now has an evidence-based upper bound of about 51–59% (§4b). V6.3 must replace it with realistic take-up rates.
- **Essential-service sub-test:** health and social work needs about 0.78–0.97 m additional persons (primary / all-workers). V6.3 must compare this with training capacity and vacancy data.
