## 1. German monetary union (1 July 1990) and the Treuhand
- **Conversion.** Wages, prices and basic savings were converted 1:1. The 1:1 savings limit was 4,000 M for adults, 2,000 M for children up to 14, and 6,000 M for people aged 60+. Larger savings, company debts and housing loans were converted at 2:1. "Speculative money" was converted at 3:1. *(Wikipedia, verified.)* The claim that 3:1 applied to non-residents was **not** verified.
- **Shock.** Within a few months, industrial output fell to about **half its 1989 level**. Only **8%** of workers were in firms that could cover variable costs at the post-union wage and world prices (Akerlof et al., BPEA 1991). More than **3 million jobs (about 1/3)** were lost. Real GDP fell to **2/3** of its 1989 level within two years (IAB 1998).
- **Treuhand.** It handled about 8,500 firms with more than 4 million employees, and 2.5 million were laid off. It closed with a **DM 256.4 bn deficit** against about DM 67 bn in revenue, after more than 15,000 privatisations. It collected pledges of DM 211 bn in investment and 1.5 m jobs, and 20–30% of the promised jobs never appeared (GHDI). Other sources give DM 200–270 bn.
- **Property.** On 15 June 1990 the two German states agreed on **"restitution before compensation"**. Expropriations of 1945–49 were not reversed. Property whose use had changed, and property bought in good faith, led to compensation instead of return (GHDI, verified). Later "investment priority" overrides and the claim counts (often quoted as over 1 m or about 2.2 m) were **not verified**.

## 2. EU enlargement and convergence
- **Growth of membership.** The EU grew from 6 members in 1958 to 27. Accessions came in 1973, 1981, 1986, 1995, 2004 (+10), 2007 and 2013. Membership peaked at 28 until the UK left in 2020. Norway voted no twice, so joining was voluntary and exit was possible. Entry required the Copenhagen criteria.
- **Transfers.** In 2014–20, cohesion support averaged **0.4% of GDP** across the EU-27 and **more than 2%** for some 2004 entrants (EC). Net EU transfers in 2010–17 were **2.7% of GDP (PL), 4.0% (HU), 1.9% (CZ) and 2.2% (SK)**. Donors paid only **0.3–0.4% of GDP**. Profit outflows to foreign owners were larger, at 4.2–7.6% of GDP (PIE 2019).
- **Convergence.** The 2004 entrants rose from **60% to 80%** of the EU average (EC). Poland went from **44% (1995)** to **~51% (2004)**, **80% (latest)** and **81% (2025)** of the EU average per capita in PPS. Cohesion policy raised GDP in less developed regions by **2.4%** in 2023 (model estimate).
- **Labour mobility.** A deferral of up to 7 years (2+3+2) applies only to search snippets (`no`). The fetched Wikipedia text confirms that most old members restricted access temporarily while the UK opened immediately. For Bulgaria and Romania, restrictions lasted until 2014.

## 3. Euro changeover
Eurostat put the cash changeover's contribution to euro-area HICP in 2002 at **0.12–0.29 pp** (ECB Monthly Bulletin, May 2007). An earlier estimate gave 0.0–0.16 pp of a 0.8% quarterly rise. **Perceived** inflation rose far more, because frequently bought items (cafés, groceries) rose. This effect explains up to half the gap between perceived and measured inflation (NBS 2007).

## 4. Debt relief
- **HIPC and MDRI.** HIPC cost **USD 76.2 bn** and MDRI **USD 43.3 bn** (end-2017 PV), so **about USD 119.5 bn** combined (derived). 36 of 39 eligible countries reached completion. Poverty-reducing spending reached **7.3% of GDP** against **1.9%** for debt service in 2017. Before relief, these countries spent more on debt service than on health and education combined. After relief, they spent about 5× more on social services (IMF).
- **Sovereign haircuts.** The mean haircut is **37%**, from 180 cases in 68 countries over 1970–2010. Half the cases are below 23% or above 53%. Larger haircuts mean higher spreads: +150 bp in year 1 and +70 bp in years 4–5 per 1 SD of haircut. A 1 SD larger haircut also gives a 50% lower annual chance of regaining market access (Cruces & Trebesch). A 200-year average of about 45% is unverified.

## 5. Land reform and compensation norms
- **Japan, 1947–50.** Tenants and part-tenants fell from **43.5% to 11.8%** of farmers. Landlords were paid **under 1,000 yen per tan** while land was worth **about 20,000 yen per tan** by 1950. Inflation turned nominal compensation into near-confiscation, and landlords complained widely (ILO 1971).
- **Taiwan, 1953.** Landlords were paid **70% in land bonds and 30% in stock of government enterprises**, and could keep up to 7.5 acres. The source's summary is flagged as AI-extracted, so treat it with care.
- **South Korea, 1950.** Land above **3 ha** and land of absentee owners was bought with bills paid over **5 annual payments**. Counties with 10 pp more tenancy saw rice-yield growth 5 pp higher (Kim & Lee 2025).
- **International law.** The norms are "prompt, adequate and effective" (Hull, 1938) and "appropriate compensation" (UNGA Res 1803, 1962). This is snippet-level only (`no`) and should be checked against the UN text.

## 6. Wealth taxes and caps
- **France, ISF.** It raised **EUR 4.42 bn** in 2007, about 1.5% of tax receipts. In 2006, **843** people left because of the tax, a net loss of **EUR 2.8 bn** (Marini). It was abolished in 2017–18. A 10-year net loss of 4,578 taxpayers and EUR 23.8 bn is unverified.
- **Norway, 2022.** Wealth tax and dividend tax went up. **At least 30** billionaires and millionaires left in 2022, more than in the previous 13 years combined. Those who moved to Switzerland held **NOK 29 bn** (Swissinfo). About **80** business owners left by 2024 (Brussels Report). Revenue-loss claims are unverified.
- **Concentration (WIR 2026).** The top 10% hold **75%** of wealth and the bottom 50% hold **2%**. The top 0.001% hold more than **6%**. A 3% tax on fewer than 100k centimillionaires and billionaires would raise about **USD 750 bn/yr**, before behavioural response. The top 1% share of about 37% is unverified.

## 7. Household debt
- Global household debt was **USD 64.6 tn** (Q4 2025), out of **USD 348.3 tn** of total debt (IIF). The share of household debt in GDP (about 57%) is derived from an unverified 308% total-debt-to-GDP ratio.
- **Iceland, 2014.** The plan was about **ISK 150 bn** for up to **100,000** households, capped at **ISK 4 m per household**, cutting the average mortgage by about 20%. It reached **56,000** households, and **2,500** of them moved to positive equity. It was funded by state contributions of 40 + 20 + 20 bn ISK.
- **Hungary, 2014–15.** FX mortgages were converted by law at **CHF/HUF 256.47** and **EUR/HUF 308.97**. More than **400,000** borrowers took part, and the central bank provided **EUR 9 bn** from reserves. The rate was locked just before the January 2015 franc shock.

## Lessons for UNIRA's transition

**What worked**
1. **Voluntary, staged accession with clear entry rules.** The EU grew from 6 to 27 over 55 years and two Norwegian "no" votes were respected. Entry was conditional (Copenhagen criteria), and new members got phase-in periods (up to 7 years for labour mobility). → UNIRA: let regions join in cohorts, with published criteria and transition windows. Keep exit possible.
2. **Modest transfers produced large convergence.** Donors paid 0.3–0.4% of GDP. Recipients got about 2–4% of GDP a year, and the 2004 entrants rose from 60% to 80% of the EU average in about 20 years (Poland: 51% to 81%). → Needs-based redistribution at a few % of the recipient's output is historically absorbable. Full equalisation is a multi-decade path, not a year-1 event.
3. **Protecting small savings at par.** East Germany converted savings 1:1 up to 2,000–6,000 M by age and at 2:1 above that, and debts were halved alongside. → UNIRA's conversion into U can protect a needs-based floor of savings at par, taper above it, and convert debts on the same terms as assets.
4. **Targeted, capped debt relief.** HIPC/MDRI (USD 119.5 bn) moved poverty-reducing spending to about 4× debt service (7.3% vs 1.9% of GDP). Iceland capped relief at ISK 4 m per household. → Relieve debt by cap and rule, not case by case.
5. **Compensation in real or productive assets.** Taiwan paid landlords 70% in bonds and 30% in state-enterprise equity, which steered landlords into industry. → If UNIRA ever buys out holdings above the 2-home cap, pay in indexed or real-value claims, not fixed nominal sums.

**What failed**
1. **Converting the whole economy at once, at a politically chosen rate.** Wages converted 1:1 left only 8% of East German workers in viable firms. Output halved, about 1/3 of jobs disappeared, and the Treuhand ran a DM 256 bn deficit. → If wages in U are set high relative to a region's productivity on day 1 (5,000–25,000 U range), there is a risk of the same "competitiveness shock". UNIRA's planned (non-market) production sidesteps price competition, but the physical-productivity gap remains. Model it explicitly.
2. **Restitution in kind blocked investment.** Germany's restitution-before-compensation rule, plus large numbers of claims, created title uncertainty. It was later overridden by investment-priority rules (abstract-level evidence only). → UNIRA's "no seizure of homes" rule is the right direction. Settle any claims by compensation, quickly, with clear title.
3. **Nominal compensation eaten by inflation.** Japanese landlords got under 5% of 1950 market value, and Korean bills eroded in war-time inflation. → Any UNIRA compensation must be indexed (in physical or real terms), or it becomes confiscation in disguise.
4. **Perception beats statistics.** The euro changeover added only 0.12–0.29 pp to measured inflation, but perceived inflation jumped. → UNIRA's fixed essential prices help. Publish dual prices, ban rounding up, and monitor everyday items.
5. **Wealth taxes on mobile capital leak while exit is open.** In France, 843 people left in 2006 alone (net EUR 2.8 bn). In Norway, more than 30 left in 2022, more than in the previous 13 years combined, holding NOK 29 bn. → A personal money cap in a *voluntary* federation faces the same exit option. Phase it in, apply it at the federation level, and pair it with exit rules (an exit tax is the Norwegian response). Losses come from relocation by a few hundred very wealthy households, not from mass flight.
