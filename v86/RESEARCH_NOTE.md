# UNIRA V8.6 — automation and model B (guaranteed income plus rewards for work)

**Author's question:** if machines one day do 90–100% of today's work, who receives an income, who owns the machines, and how do people get what they need? **Author's choice (rule 59):** model B, a guaranteed income plus rewards for work, introduced gradually as automation grows.

**Model** (`automation/model.py`, illustrative, all parameters synthetic):
- automation follows an S-curve to 90% of today's tasks (slow, medium and fast scenarios);
- 10% of tasks stay human (care, teaching, research, governance, maintaining the machines);
- real needs per adult grow 0.5% a year (better quality within ecological limits, not endless growth);
- tools make the remaining human tasks 1% a year more productive;
- hours needed are shared among everyone able to work (rule 16);
- with fixed prices, total income equals the value of what is produced for people;
- under model B, the **guaranteed income takes the share of output not paid as hours worked**, funded by the community share of automated production (Article 16), and **pay for work takes the human share**.

## Findings

1. **UNIRA already turns automation into free time and shared income.** Productivity becomes shorter hours (rule 16), and enterprise surpluses become the community share (Article 16). Over decades this leads naturally to model B: the guaranteed part of income grows from almost nothing today to most of income when most work is automated. No new ownership revolution is needed, because machines' returns already belong to everyone.
2. **Human work never disappears.** About 10% of today's tasks stay human, an average of about 2 hours a week per adult. In practice that is a minority working longer by choice (about 6% of adults full-time), attracted by the salary multipliers (up to 5×), recognition and meaning. This is why model B keeps rewards for work.
3. **Automated systems can fail.** If 20% of automated capacity is lost for a year (cyber-attack, breakdown, energy failure), people must work about 3–4 extra hours a week. That is possible only if enough people keep the skills. A **skills reserve** is therefore needed: regular training and rotation, about 2 hours a week, which covers such a failure in every scenario.
4. **Concentration of control is the main risk** (author's point): one supplier, one network, one operator. Proposed Article 14c.6 requires public oversight, human control and several independent suppliers for critical systems.

## Proposed additions to Article 14c (still for research and debate)

- the guaranteed income is funded by the community share of automated production;
- a skills reserve keeps people trained to run essential systems by hand;
- a minority of essential human work is rewarded by multipliers rather than compelled.

## Limitations

- Illustrative and region-level.
- The automation pace is unknown.
- Machines' energy and material needs are not included (they must fit Article 10c and the ecological limits).
- Distribution between people, and motivation in a mostly voluntary economy, are untested and need the pilot and later evidence.
