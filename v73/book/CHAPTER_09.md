# Chapter 9 — Security, not scarcity

*Second draft, 3 November 2026, updated with V7.9. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.4, V6.6, V7.1, V7.9 and the draft constitution) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

It is a Saturday in March 2020. A shopper pushes a trolley down a supermarket aisle. The shelf where the toilet roll should be is bare. So is the pasta shelf. Next to them, another shopper loads a trolley with twelve packets of rice.

Neither of them is greedy. They have heard that shops may close. They do not know when food will come back. So they buy what they can, while they can. And by buying, they help empty the shelves for everyone else.

This scene is an imagined composite, but the shelves were real. In Ireland, in the week to 15 March 2020, sales of toilet roll were **181%** higher than a year earlier. Pasta was up **199%**, and rice **123%**.

Now imagine the same shopper in a world where they know, without doubt, that food will always be there. The price will not jump. The shop will be stocked next week, and next year. Would they still fill the trolley?

That is the question of this chapter. It sounds like a question about shopping. In fact it reaches much further. It decides how UNIRA prices energy, how large its emergency stores must be, and whether a comfortable life for everyone fits inside the climate limits of Part III.

## The idea: people stop hoarding when they feel safe

UNIRA's twentieth design rule is a hypothesis, not a promise. It says that when people know they can always afford and access food and essentials, they stop over-buying, hoarding and over-eating.

The author of UNIRA offers an everyday analogy. When mobile calls became unlimited for a fixed price, nobody started calling 24 hours a day.

It is a good idea. It is also exactly the kind of idea that should be tested, not assumed. So the research looked for evidence, good by good. It asked one plain question each time: when access becomes secure, cheap or unlimited, does use go up, go down, or level off?

### What "security" means here

Before the evidence, it helps to be clear about what UNIRA promises. Security in this chapter has three parts.

- **Guaranteed rights.** Article 6 of the draft constitution gives every person food and clean water, "sufficient and secure, with access guaranteed". It also guarantees "energy, transport and digital access sufficient for a comfortable life".
- **Fixed prices.** Prices of essentials never change (Article 13). A family need not fear that bread will double next month.
- **Real reserves.** Every region keeps "studied, rotated reserves against catastrophes" (Article 11).

The hypothesis is that these three together change behaviour. The rest of the chapter tests that, piece by piece.

## What happened to phone calls

Start with the analogy itself. In France, the average mobile customer spoke for about **2 hours 15 minutes** a month in early 2011. Unlimited plans then spread. By early 2013, the figure was **2 hours 51 minutes**. The French telecoms regulator, ARCEP, attributed the rise to unlimited plans.

Then the rise slowed almost to a stop. In early 2015 it was **3 hours 3 minutes**. In 2023 it was about **3 hours 28 minutes** a month, or about **7 minutes a day**.

So the analogy holds, but with a twist. Use did not fall when calls became unlimited. It **rose first**, by about **36%** within three years, and then levelled off. Why? Because there are only so many hours in a day, and only so many people to call. Calls have a natural ceiling.

### A closer look: how the phone figures were measured

The regulator's figures are minutes of calls per customer per month. They are an average over all customers, heavy and light users together.

Two details matter. First, the growth rate. In early 2013, minutes were **15.5%** higher than a year before. In early 2015, they were only **1.6%** higher. That is the levelling-off, seen directly.

Second, the basis changed. The 2023 figure counts people who hold a mobile device. The figures for 2011 to 2015 count customers. So the 2023 number is not perfectly comparable with the earlier ones. The research notes this openly. It does not change the shape of the story: a step up, then a plateau.

### Paying for peace of mind

There is a second lesson from phones. A study by Lambrecht and Skiera (2006) found that up to **46.4%** of customers chose a flat rate that cost them more than paying per use would have.

Why would anyone do that? Not to use as much as possible. Their own usage was low enough that paying per minute would have been cheaper. They paid extra for the comfort of never worrying about the bill. In the researchers' terms, they bought insurance.

That is the heart of rule 20. People value security for its own sake. Given it, many do not try to squeeze out every last unit.

## Where security helps

The evidence supports the idea for several kinds of goods.

### Hoarding

The Irish figures show that scarcity, or the fear of it, drives stockpiling. In that same week of March 2020, total grocery sales were **23%** higher than a year earlier. The goods people feared would run out rose far more: toilet roll **181%**, pasta **199%**, rice **123%**.

**A closer look.** These are "year-on-year" figures. The research compares sales in one week of 2020 with the same week of 2019. That removes normal seasonal swings. What remains is the panic. Notice the pattern: the whole basket rose by about a quarter, but storable staples nearly tripled. People were not eating three times more pasta. They were filling cupboards.

Remove the fear, and much of the panic goes with it. Chapter 4 showed the other side of this coin: in shortage economies, the fear of empty shelves fed itself.

### Alcohol and tobacco

A common fear about giving people money is that they will spend it on drink and cigarettes. The evidence says otherwise. Evans and Popova gathered **44 estimates from 19 studies** of cash transfers. The results were almost all zero or negative. More secure income did not mean more spending on these goods.

**A closer look.** Each "estimate" measures how spending on alcohol or tobacco changed when households received cash. A zero result means no measurable change. A negative result means spending on these goods went *down*. The review also quotes an average effect size, but that exact figure was not confirmed in the text the research fetched. So the chapter relies only on the direction: almost all zero or negative.

### Calories

In India, Deaton and Dreze (2009) found that people ate fewer calories as their real incomes rose. A review by Nettle and colleagues (2017) of **125 studies** found food insecurity linked to obesity only in one group: adult women in high-income countries. Bodies, like days, have limits.

But security is not the whole story for food. In a controlled trial by Hall (2019), people offered ultra-processed food freely ate about **508 more kilocalories** a day.

**A closer look.** A randomised controlled trial is the strongest kind of test. It changes one thing and holds the rest steady. Here the thing that changed was the kind of food on offer, not income or worry. So the extra calories point to the food itself. The lesson is clear: **what kind of food is on offer matters more than whether people feel secure.** Chapter 11 returns to diet.

### What the review chose not to use

Good research also says what it left out. The review deliberately did not use the well-known buffet studies by Wansink, because of retractions. It also flagged figures it had seen only in search results, not in the original texts. Those were never used as central values. This care matters. A hopeful hypothesis attracts hopeful evidence, and that is exactly when checking should be strictest.

## Where it helps only a little: food waste

Does security reduce food waste at home? The evidence here is weak.

Household food waste is similar across rich and poor countries. In the UN's 2021 food waste index, it was about **74 kg** per person per year. The spread between country income groups was only about **7 kg**.

The pattern is not a straight line. Waste rises at middle incomes, partly from buying extra "to avoid running out". That is an inverted U: waste rises from low to middle incomes, then falls again.

One more data point cuts the other way. During the 2020 lockdown in the United Kingdom, people reported wasting **34%** less. That happened at a time of insecurity, not security. It is self-reported, so people may have remembered their habits kindly. But it is a warning against assuming that comfort, by itself, cuts waste.

The research tested what security might do. It tried a cut in household waste of 0%, 5% (the central value) and 15%. That moves the world's comfortable-diet supply only from **1.105** to **1.11–1.12** times the need.

So Chapter 1's lesson stands. **Halving waste is the main lever**, and it needs deliberate effort: better storage, better planning, no overproduction. Security alone will not do it. Chapter 11 looks at how much waste there is, and where.

## Where security fails: goods with no ceiling

Now the other side. Some goods have no natural limit. Nobody's stomach fills up with data, travel or warm rooms. For these goods, making use cheap or unlimited pushes use up, and keeps pushing.

| Good | What the evidence shows | Natural ceiling? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mobile calls | Rose by about a third, then levelled off | Yes |
| Toilet roll, pasta, rice | Panic buying up to **+181–199%** when scarcity is feared | Yes, once fear is gone |
| Alcohol and tobacco | No increase from cash transfers (44 estimates, 19 studies) | Yes |
| Food calories | Fell as incomes rose in India | Yes, but food type matters |
| Household food waste | Only a weak link to security | Weak |
| Mobile data | **0.5 GB → 14 GB** a month, 2015–2023; unlimited plans +20% use | **No** |
| Heating | Flat-rate heat uses up to about **30%** more than metered heat | **No** |
| Public transport | Free transit: **+14%** (Tallinn) to **+70%** (Dunkirk) trips | **No** |

### Data

Data is the clearest case. Mobile data use rose from about **529 megabytes** a month in 2015 to **14 gigabytes** in 2023. That is about **2 GB** more every year. In high-income countries, users on unlimited plans used about **20%** more than users on large capped plans. There is no sign of a ceiling.

Why the difference from calls? A call needs a person at the other end and your own time. Data needs neither, so nothing in the day stops it growing.

### Heat

For home energy, two findings stand out. A review by Sorrell (2009) puts the direct rebound under **30%**. And homes billed a flat rate for heat use "often" up to about **30%** more than homes that pay for what they use. A flat bill removes the reason to turn the radiator down when you open the window.

### Travel

Travel tells the same story. When public transport became free in Tallinn, its use rose by **14%**. In Dunkirk, weekday trips rose by **70%**, and weekend trips by **140%**.

Free transport may be good for a city. But it does not show that people use less when access is secure. It shows the opposite.

Even then, free transport did not empty the roads. In Luxembourg, the third free-transit case in the review, the share of people commuting by car went only from **72.3%** to **69.3%** between 2011 and 2021. Use went up, but most car commuters stayed in their cars.

## The pattern: two kinds of goods

Put the evidence side by side and a simple pattern appears.

Some goods have a **natural ceiling**. There are only so many hours to talk, so much food a body can hold, so much space in a cupboard. Once people feel safe, use levels off. Sometimes it rises first, as calls did. But it stops.

Other goods have **no ceiling**. Data, travel, heated space and status can always grow. More is always possible, and often tempting. For these goods, cheap or unlimited access raises use without limit.

The author's hypothesis is therefore half right. That is not a failure. It is what testing is for. It turns a hopeful belief into a precise rule.

## Why this matters for the planet

This may sound like a detail. It is not. It decides whether UNIRA stays inside the climate limits described in Part III.

Chapter 10 shows that a comfortable life for everyone can fit a 1.5 °C pathway. In 2050, it needs about **386 exajoules** of final energy a year. That is about **6–13% less** than the world uses today: 409 EJ in the UNIRA model, 442 EJ according to the International Energy Agency. That works only if rich regions use much less energy than they do now.

Now add the rebound. When energy becomes cheaper or easier, people use more of it.

**A simple illustration.** The numbers here are round and imagined, to show the arithmetic. Suppose a rich region insulates its homes and fits heat pumps. On paper, that saves 100 units of energy. With a 20% rebound, people keep rooms a little warmer and leave lights on a little longer. They use back 20 units. The real saving is 80.

Now scale that up. The research tested rebound on the savings of every rich region. Suppose people use back **20–30%** of those savings. Then peak world energy rises to **424–436 exajoules**. That is above today's **409** in the model and outside the 1.5 °C range.

With only a **10%** rebound, peak use would be about **413 exajoules**, just above the line. The difference between those numbers is the difference between keeping the climate promise and breaking it.

| Rebound on rich-region savings | Peak world final energy | Inside the 1.5 °C envelope? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None (the plan) | at or below **409 EJ** | Yes |
| 10% | about **413 EJ** | Just outside |
| 20–30% | **424–436 EJ** | **No** |

## The answer: meter what has no ceiling

So the research proposed a clear rule, now Article 19 of the draft constitution:

> "Energy, travel and data are always metered and paid per unit, never at a flat rate."

This does not mean these goods are scarce or expensive. Article 6 guarantees everyone "energy, transport and digital access sufficient for a comfortable life". Under UNIRA, the price per unit is fixed and never jumps. What is ruled out is the "all you can use" offer.

There is an important difference between a **fixed price** and a **zero price**. A fixed price is still a price. Each extra kilowatt-hour or kilometre still costs something, so people still think before they use it. That limits rebound. Free or flat-rate access removes the signal.

There is a second trap, found later. UNIRA prices goods by the hours of work they take. Priced that way alone, fuels would become about **79%** cheaper relative to other goods, and electricity about **65%** cheaper, because today's prices for them pay mostly for nature, not work. That would undo the meter. So Article 13.6 adds a **resource surcharge**: energy, fuels and air travel never become cheaper, relative to other goods, than they were when a region joined. Chapter 6 explains the rule.

The rule therefore has two halves:

- **Goods with a natural ceiling** (food, basic goods, calls) can be offered with secure, guaranteed access.
- **Goods without one** (energy, travel, data, space) stay metered and priced per unit.

### An imagined evening under the rule

Picture a family in a UNIRA region on a winter evening. The scene is invented. Their fridge is full, and they never worry about the next shop. Their phone calls are part of a simple plan. None of that is metered by the minute.

Their heating, their train tickets and their mobile data are different. A small screen shows what the evening has used and what each unit costs. The price is the same as last year, and it will be the same next year. Nobody is short. But when the teenager leaves a window open with the heating on, the screen shows it.

That is all the rule asks. Not less comfort, but a visible cost for goods that would otherwise grow without end.

## Reserves against catastrophe

Security does not come from a promise alone. It must be real, or people will stop believing it, and the trolleys will fill again.

UNIRA's thirteenth design rule asks every region to keep **fixed, studied catastrophe reserves** of goods. Article 11 of the draft constitution writes this down: each region keeps "studied, rotated reserves against catastrophes". Article 17a says the One Earth Fund pays for them.

Three words in that rule do a lot of work.

- **Studied.** Reserves are sized from how often shocks have actually happened. The research notes this can be tested against records of how much harvests vary from year to year.
- **Rotated.** Stocks are eaten or used and replaced before they spoil. That keeps the principle of no waste.
- **Fixed.** Reserves are not an excuse to overproduce. Production equals what people consume, plus replacing the reserves, plus investment. Nothing more.

Article 3a puts the same idea in one line: "We produce what we need, for all regions", planned "from measured needs and population in every region, plus the studied reserves".

### How security and reserves work together

Here security and reserves reinforce each other. The research estimates that a credible guarantee avoids about **25–75%** of panic-buying spikes, with a central value of **50%**.

That estimate has conditions. It needs the reserves themselves. It needs limits on purchases during a shock, so that one trolley cannot empty a shelf. And it needs people to believe the guarantee.

The payoff is practical. Reserves can be sized for real **supply shocks**, such as a failed harvest. They do not have to cover demand panics on top. In normal times, the effect of security on buying is small, close to nothing. Its value shows up in a crisis.

**A closer look.** The 25–75% is a model parameter, not a measurement. No region has yet run guaranteed reserves through a pandemic and counted the panic it avoided. The research sets the range wide on purpose, and uses the middle value only as a central case.

## Fixed prices: no more price shocks

Under UNIRA, prices of essentials are fixed. Rule 8 says they should not change, "even after 1,000 years". Article 13 of the draft constitution sets them from the human work and resources each good embodies.

The author gives a homely example. One kilogram of tomatoes costs **1 U**. It costs 1 U this year, next year, and when today's children are old.

That removes a large source of fear. A family need not worry that bread will double in price next month. A retired person's income, set by need to cover food, daily life and one holiday a year, buys the same basket for life.

The research found one more benefit. Fixed prices end household price shocks such as the energy-price crisis of 2022.

But history gives a warning. The Soviet Union froze the prices of staple goods for decades. The economist Kornai studied the result in *Economics of Shortage* (1980). The price held, but the goods did not. There were queues, rationing and informal markets. A fixed price on an empty shelf is not security. Chapter 4 tells that story in full.

So in UNIRA, the burden of balance moves to **physical supply**. Production is planned to needs. Reserves cover shocks. And the money people hold must never be larger than the goods available at the fixed price.

## The money cap keeps prices honest

That last condition is where the money cap comes in. Chapter 6 introduced it, and Chapter 7 showed its role in housing. Here is why it matters for security.

At fixed prices, unspent money is a claim on goods. The research simulated 6,000 adults over 60 years in a France-sized region. Without a cap, unspent money grew without limit: **152 months** of consumption after 60 years, and still rising. If even part of it were spent at once, the shelves would empty. That is the Soviet trap again.

With a cap and zero-interest credit for homes, unspent money settles at about **12 months** of consumption. Money above the cap goes to the One Earth Fund, about a fifth of the region's consumption value each year. The Fund turns it into homes, energy and care where they are needed.

| 60-year simulation, 6,000 adults | No cap | Moderate cap of 1.5 years' base salary, with protected accounts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unspent money, months of consumption | **152**, still rising | about **13** free, plus about **12** in protected accounts |
| Extra demand if a quarter of free money is spent in one year | **+316%** of a year's goods | **+26%** |
| Flow to the One Earth Fund | about 0% | about **22%** a year |
| Adults at the cap | — | **40%** |

The middle row is the one that matters for security. It asks what happens if fear strikes and people rush to spend. Without a cap, the rush would be more than three years' worth of goods: empty shelves are certain. With the moderate cap, it is about a quarter of a year's goods, which reserves can be built to cover. Money in the protected home and retirement accounts cannot be spent in a rush at all. A higher cap of 5 years would raise the rush to **+78%**. That is one reason the author chose 1.5 years (Chapter 6).

The cap also adjusts itself. A yearly thermostat, run by rule and limited to **10%** up or down, kept unspent money within about **10–14 months** of consumption through a simulated disaster, a wave of fearful saving, a baby boom and fast automation.

Pay is guarded too. If average pay drifted up by 0.5% a year with no matching rise in productivity, demand would exceed supply by **13%** after 30 years. So Article 14 says that average pay may rise only as fast as productivity.

When productivity rises faster, Article 13 turns the gain into shorter working hours, not higher prices. With productivity growing 1% a year, the simulated week falls from **30 to about 23 hours** after 30 years.

Each of these rules has the same aim. People can trust that what their money buys today, it will buy tomorrow. That trust is what lets the shopper leave the rice on the shelf.

## Who gains most from stable prices

Security matters most to people who have the least of it today.

The research looked inside rich regions, using official income data for 2024. A worker on the lowest UNIRA salary who saves the maximum, half their pay, can still buy about **86%** of what an average French person consumes today. By that measure, **half the population** of France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the EU as a whole is already better off. The poorest tenth gains **146% to 258%**.

Free health care, education and guaranteed housing come on top, and are not counted in those figures. Chapter 15 returns to who gains and who gives something up.

## Honest answers to hard questions

A careful reader will have objections. Here are five, with the best answers the evidence allows.

**"Metering energy is just rationing with a nicer name."**
No. Rationing caps how much you may have. Metering only shows the cost of each unit. Article 6 guarantees energy, transport and digital access sufficient for a comfortable life. Chapter 10 shows that a comfortable level of energy for all fits the planet. The meter limits waste and rebound, not comfort.

**"Free public transport is good for the climate. Why rule it out?"**
The evidence says free transport mostly adds trips. In Dunkirk, weekend trips more than doubled. In Luxembourg, the share of car commuters fell only three points in ten years. A fixed price per trip, which never jumps, keeps public transport within reach while still giving each trip a cost. What the rule forbids is the unlimited pass.

**"The phone analogy failed, so the whole idea fails."**
It did not fail. It was refined. Calls rose about a third, then stopped. That is exactly what a natural ceiling looks like. The analogy simply does not carry over to goods without one. Rule 20 now says so.

**"Won't people just hoard money instead of goods?"**
They might try. That is what the money cap is for. Money above the cap goes to the One Earth Fund, and returns as resources. The research is honest that this is untested. It names a clear test that would prove the money rules wrong. If people at the cap spend rather than save, and demand at fixed prices outruns what reserves can absorb, the design fails.

**"If food is guaranteed, won't people overeat?"**
The calorie evidence says no, in general: people in India ate fewer calories as incomes rose. But the Hall trial shows that ultra-processed food changes the picture. Security is not the risk. The kind of food is. That is a question of what is produced, which Chapter 11 takes up.

## What the evidence says

- Security reduces over-consumption for goods with a **natural ceiling**. Phone calls rose by about a third, then levelled off at about **7 minutes a day**. Up to **46%** of customers pay extra for a flat rate, for peace of mind.
- Cash transfers did not raise spending on alcohol or tobacco across **44 estimates** from 19 studies.
- Scarcity drives hoarding: toilet roll **+181%** in Ireland in March 2020. A credible guarantee could avoid about **25–75%** of such spikes, if backed by real reserves and purchase limits during shocks.
- Security alone does little for food waste. Halving waste remains the main lever for a comfortable diet.
- For goods **without a ceiling**, cheap or unlimited access raises use: data **0.5 → 14 GB** a month, free transit **+14–70%** trips, flat-rate heating up to about **30%** more.
- A **20–30%** energy rebound would push world energy to **424–436 EJ**, outside the 1.5 °C range. That is why energy, travel and data are always metered (Article 19).
- Fixed prices are stable only if money stays matched to goods. The money cap holds unspent money at about **12 months** of consumption, against **152** without it. At the chosen cap of 1.5 years, a sudden spending rush adds about **26%** to a year's demand, against **316%** with no cap.

## What we still don't know

- No society has yet lived with permanently guaranteed access to essentials. The evidence comes from phones, cash transfers and short crises, not from a whole economy.
- Some figures in the review were seen only in search results and were not used as central values. They include an average effect size for cash transfers and some car-use figures for Tallinn and Dunkirk.
- Studies of basic income in Finland and of the Mincome experiment reported no results on how much people bought or stockpiled.
- The 25–75% avoided panic is a model range, not a measurement.
- The size of the reserves, and how often they must be rotated, still has to be studied region by region.
- How people behave once they reach the money cap is unknown. If they spend rather than save, demand at fixed prices rises.
- A fixed price limits rebound better than a free good, but how well it works over decades is untested. The 10–30% rebound values come from today's economies.
- The phone figures changed basis in 2023, from customers to device holders, so the long series is not perfectly comparable.
