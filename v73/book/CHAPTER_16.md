# Chapter 16 — One humanity, one future

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.2–V7.5) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine a child born today, anywhere on Earth. In 2050 that child will be about twenty-four, one of about **9.7 billion** people.

What kind of world will they live in? One where billions still live in regions without enough health workers, where homes are crowded, where wars still take people and money, and where the climate keeps getting harsher? Or one where every person has enough food, a comfortable home, care when they are ill, and time with the people they love?

The child is imagined. The choice is not.

This book has asked a narrower question than "what should the world be like?" It has asked: **is the second world physically possible?** Do we have enough food, energy, land, materials and working hands for everyone to live comfortably, within the limits of the planet? And could a world organised as one federation of equal regions, with UNIRA's rules, get there?

This last chapter gathers the answer. It sets out what we still do not know, how the work could be proven wrong, and how every reader can check it for themselves.

## What the research shows

The answer from the evidence is a qualified **yes**.

**There is enough.** The world already produces enough food for a decent diet for everyone. It has enough for a comfortable diet if consumer waste is halved. On energy, a decent life needs about **15.3 GJ** of final energy per person a year, and a comfortable one about **40 GJ**. The world uses about **55 GJ** per person today. So there is enough for comfort, if it is shared. There is not enough for everyone to live like today's Europeans or Americans.

**Work can be shared.** A 30-hour week is affordable if almost everyone able to work does work. In France, an employment rate of about **88%** keeps today's output from the first year. Technology can then shorten the week further: towards **23 hours** after 30 years, if productivity grows 1% a year.

**The money can work.** A single currency with fixed prices, salaries from 5,000 to 25,000 U, and a cap on personal money keeps goods and money in balance. Without the cap, unspent money piles up without limit. The money above the cap flows to the One Earth Fund, which pays for what every region receives.

**The planet can carry it.** A comfortable level of energy for everyone, **40 GJ** per person, adds up in 2050 to about **6–13% less** energy than the world uses today. The range depends on the estimate of today's use. Material use per person must fall about **2.7 times**, and homes must be built with low-carbon materials.

**Needs-first allocation works better.** Given the same resources, a reformed market economy lets the gaps between rich and poor regions widen. UNIRA closes them: comfortable energy for every region by about year **27**, and European-level health care for all by about year **30**.

**Homes take longest.** Comfortable space for everyone, 27 m² per person, means about **65 billion m²** of new homes. Building about **4.7 billion m²** a year, without a break, closes that backlog by year 27. But the population grows, and old homes need upgrading. Counting both, about **109 billion m²** must be built or upgraded. That needs building in regions of need to grow **5–10%** a year, to a peak of about **6–7 billion m²** a year.

**Safeguards make the difference.** Real institutions make mistakes and lose resources. With strong safeguards, needs-first allocation keeps about **85%** of its advantage. Without them, it keeps less, but still more than the best market world.

**Most people gain.** Compared with a reformed market world after 30 years, about **86%** of humanity gains materially by joining. In the richest regions, *average* energy use per person is about **6–12%** lower. That reduction falls on the heaviest users, not on the poor, and nobody falls below the comfortable level. In return they gain peace, security, time and a stable climate. And inside them, half of the people of France, Germany, Italy and Spain would be better off even at the lowest UNIRA salary.

## The six conditions

The research says yes, but only if six things happen together:
1. **Universal work:** about 88% of adults aged 20–64 in work, about 30 hours a week.
2. **Less waste:** consumer food waste is halved and diets shift towards what the planet can carry.
3. **Clean energy, fast:** about **1.2–1.8 TW** of new clean power a year. That is solar and wind built **1.7–1.9 times** faster than the 2025 record. Meanwhile, regions above 40 GJ cut their use about **3–4%** a year.
4. **Less material:** about **2.7 times** less material per person, through repair, reuse and building from what exists.
5. **Many more carers:** a 20–30-year world programme to train health workers. The world has **47.4 million** and needs about **107 million** for EU-level care. Former soldiers and healthier lives help.
6. **Many more homes:** building that grows **5–10%** a year to about **6–7 billion m²** a year, with low-carbon materials and about **250 million** builders at the peak. Most of it is in Africa and South Asia.

None of these is easy. Some go further than anything done so far. No country has reached 88% employment, and solar and wind must be built faster than the record year. That is why each condition is also a test.

## A closer look: what the six conditions ask of people

Numbers like "1.2 TW" or "6–7 billion m²" can feel abstract. Here is what some of them mean in hands and hours.

**Builders.** Each square metre of home takes about **33–64 hours** of work. The world has about **294 million** construction workers. Only about **116 million** of them are in South Asia and Africa, where most of the need is. Universal work and training must roughly double the builders in those regions.

**Carers.** In France, a general practitioner takes **10 years** to train, and a nurse **3 years**. Schools and teachers are the bottleneck, not willingness. No allocation rule can shorten that.

**Workers.** In France alone, 88% employment means workplaces for about **5 million** more people. It also means childcare and eldercare, so that parents and carers can take those jobs.

These are not reasons to give up. They are the size of the task, stated honestly.

## What we have not shown

An honest book must say clearly where its evidence ends.

- **People's behaviour.** No UNIRA economy exists, so we do not know how people would save, spend, work or move under its rules. The models use stated assumptions, and some of them are optimistic.
- **Building the institutions.** The research shows what needs-first allocation would achieve if it worked. It cannot show that a world federation can be built, or how quickly. Its own institutions also need work hours that are not yet counted.
- **Politics.** Whether rich regions would choose peace, security and time over part of their energy use is a choice for their people. No model can make it for them.
- **Averages.** Most numbers are national or regional averages. Inside every region, people live very differently.
- **Planning at scale.** The comparison with markets covered energy, health workers and homes. Planning millions of products for billions of people is a much larger problem. It is still open.
- **Some data are missing.** World figures for new medical graduates, home building by income group and military energy use do not exist in usable form. Floor area per person comes from a regional model, because no official country data exist.

## How UNIRA could be proven wrong

Good science says in advance what would prove it wrong. The research names its own tests:
1. **Labour:** employment rates cannot be raised above about **85%** within a few years. Or output per hour barely responds to a 30-hour week.
2. **Money:** under the cap, people spend rather than save, and demand exceeds supply at fixed prices by more than reserve stocks can absorb.
3. **Health:** the world's capacity to train health workers cannot grow beyond about **1.5 times** today's. EU-level care for all would then be more than 50 years away.
4. **Ecology:** clean-energy building stays below the 2025 record, or material use cannot be at least halved while homes and infrastructure are built.
5. **Housing:** the world cannot build about **65 billion m²** of homes within a generation.
6. **Comparison:** a reformed market economy with the same resources reaches the same outcomes. UNIRA would then add nothing beyond its assumptions.

The allocation test was later sharpened. **If One Earth cannot keep leakage well below about 45%, and data lags under about 5 years, needs-first allocation is no better than a cooperative market.**

The draft constitution writes that test into law. If leakage passes **25%**, or needs data are more than 2 years old, the allocation system must be reviewed within a year. If leakage passes **45%**, or data are older than 5 years, for three years running, it must be reformed by law. A good system should know how to notice its own failure.

## The strongest objections, in one place

Each chapter of this book has faced its own critics. Here are the hardest objections together, with where the book answers them.

**"Markets are more efficient than any planner."** Chapter 13 gave UNIRA no efficiency bonus and markets none either. The comparison changed only institutions. But it covered only three kinds of new capacity, and it did not count the work of running UNIRA's own institutions. That objection is not settled.

**"A world authority will be captured or corrupted."** Chapter 14 measured how real allocators fail and put those failures into the model. With typical failures and no safeguards, needs-first allocation still beat the best market world. Under the worst governance ever observed, it did not.

**"Taking money above the cap is confiscation."** Chapter 15 does not hide it. The research calls the direct transfer "legally and politically harder" than bonds. The cap phases in over ten years, homes are never taken, and anyone may leave with their money up to the cap.

**"If things are free, people will waste them."** Chapter 9 found this is true for some goods and false for others. Use of food and basic goods levels off once needs are met. Use of energy, travel and data does not. So those three are always metered and paid per unit.

**"People will not work if their needs are met."** The research cannot answer this from observation. No UNIRA economy exists. The work rule, the salary scale and the money cap are designed to keep people working, but behaviour is an assumption. It sits behind the labour and money tests below.

**"Rich regions will never agree."** Perhaps not. That is why UNIRA can start without them. And inside those regions, half the people would be better off even at the lowest UNIRA income.

## An answer to the oldest objection

The oldest objection to any plan like this is short. "It is utopian."

The research does not claim that UNIRA is certain, or even likely. It claims something more modest and more testable. The world has the physical means. The institutions that would share them can be described in detail. Their weak points can be named and measured.

A utopia hides its weak points. This book has tried to list them: leakage, data lags, a 50% audit rate that is only an assumption, the risk of an East German shock, the very rich leaving before a vote, an employment rate no country has reached. If those weak points prove fatal, the research has said in advance how we would know.

## How you can check everything

This book does not ask you to trust it. It asks you to check it. Here is how.

### Open data

Every data source behind the research is official and public. Eurostat, INSEE, the World Bank, FAO, WHO, the ILO and the UN's population projections supply most of it.

Each source is frozen as it was retrieved. Its retrieval time is recorded. So is a SHA-256 hash, a kind of digital fingerprint. If a single figure in a file changes, the fingerprint changes too. So anyone can confirm they are using exactly the same data.

Every number also carries a label saying what kind of number it is:

| Label | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| VERIFIED_OFFICIAL | Taken from a named official table, with its fingerprint |
| REPORTED_NEEDS_RECHECK | Reported by a source, but still to be checked against the original |
| MODEL_DERIVED | Calculated by the research models |
| SYNTHETIC_ASSUMPTION | An assumption, stated openly |
| NORMATIVE_TARGET | One of the author's design rules, a goal rather than a fact |

The evidence reviews go one step further. For each value, they record its source and whether the number was actually seen in fetched text. Numbers that could not be seen are marked and not used as facts.

### Open code

The calculations are open too. They run on an ordinary computer, with Python 3 and nothing else beyond its standard library. One command reruns the whole chain.

When the research is rerun from a clean copy, the outputs come out byte for byte the same. All **151** automated tests pass.

### An illustration: checking one number

Imagine a reader who doubts the claim that **86%** of humanity gains by joining. They are a schoolteacher, curious and a little suspicious.

The notes for Chapter 15 point them to one output file. They open it in any text editor. They find a line giving the gainers' share of world population: **0.859**. They find the number of gaining regions: **146**. They find that each region is compared with a reformed market world in year 30, not with today.

They do not have to stop there. They can open the model, change the assumption they distrust, and run it again. If the answer changes a great deal, they have found something important. They should say so.

The teacher is imagined. The path they follow is real, and anyone can take it.

### Independent audits

The research has been checked by others, not only by its author. Four separate reviews were run, each given the code and sources rather than the conclusions:
- an archive check found **125 of 125** files intact and **90 of 90** runnable tests passing;
- a check of primary sources found **0** contradictions in **23** recorded figures;
- an adversarial code review found **5** major problems, all fixed or documented;
- a review of the evidence on working hours and productivity set the ranges used in the labour model.

A later round checked **11** more figures against primary sources.

### A record of corrections

The most useful sign of honest work is not that it was right first time. It is that it changed when the evidence said so. Here are some of the corrections the checks forced:
- two earlier money models had been described as fully balanced in their accounts. They were not, and the description was withdrawn;
- an earlier "stress case" turned out to be the same as the baseline;
- an earlier working-time rule turned out not to be a 30-hour week at all;
- a self-check in the labour model was found to test nothing, and rewriting it exposed a real bug, which was fixed;
- the development-aid figure was updated from 0.33% to **0.26%** of donor income;
- the training time for a French general practitioner was corrected to **10 years**;
- the world's yearly growth in housing, about **3.4 billion m²**, was relabelled as a net increase, not total building.

Some of these made UNIRA's case harder, not easier. They were made anyway.

### A planned public website and simulator

A free public website is planned for the research. It is meant to bring the data, the code, the evidence tables and the draft constitution together in one place, open to anyone.

It is also meant to host an interactive simulator. The idea is simple. Instead of reading that UNIRA closes 60% of the energy gap by year 15, a reader could move the levers themselves. They could try a lower employment rate, slower clean-energy building or higher leakage, and watch what happens. The website and simulator are plans, not yet finished tools.

### Public discussion

Checking is only half the work. The other half is talking about what the checks find.

The draft constitution builds this habit into its own rules. Every decision must publish its data, its methods and its reasons, with their uncertainty, so anyone can check them. An independent Scientific Council, drawn from all regions, reviews the methods. A decision that ignores its advice must say why. When new evidence shows a rule is wrong, the rule is reviewed.

The research asks the same of itself. Its stated next steps include outside review by economists, demographers, health-workforce experts and climate scientists. They also include piloting the safeguards, measuring leakage in building projects, and finding evidence on how many people take up guaranteed work.

If you find an error, an assumption you disagree with, or better evidence, the research should change. That is how this work was done from the beginning. Every claim was tested, and several were corrected when the evidence said so.

## What would come next

If the research holds up under that scrutiny, what follows is not a world government overnight. Chapter 15 showed why.

It would begin with a few willing regions. They would vote. They would build the safeguards first, publish every allocation, and let their results be judged in public. A union of only the regions that gain would still close the world's gaps faster than the best market world, at about half the speed of the whole world together.

If it worked, others could join. If it failed, the built-in tests would say so. And any region could leave by vote.

## An invitation

This book does not ask you to believe in UNIRA. It asks you to consider that a better world is not a fantasy of abundance. It is a question of organisation, sharing and care. And the numbers are closer to our reach than we are usually told.

Every region keeps its faith, its language, its traditions and its borders. Every person keeps their freedom. What changes is that no one is left without enough, no region rules another, and no war takes the future from a child born today.

One humanity. One future.

## What the evidence says

- The world has enough food and energy for a comfortable life for everyone, though not for everyone to consume like today's richest regions.
- A 30-hour week, fixed prices and a money cap can work together as one consistent system, if almost everyone able to work does work.
- Needs-first allocation closes the gaps between regions that a market economy, with the same resources, lets widen. With strong safeguards it keeps about **85%** of its advantage.
- Compared with a reformed market world after 30 years, about **86%** of humanity gains materially by joining.
- Six conditions must hold together: universal work, less waste, fast clean energy, less material, many more carers and many more homes.
- Every number can be traced to frozen public data and open code, and rerun on an ordinary computer.

## What we still don't know

- Whether people and institutions would behave as the models assume.
- How a world federation could be built in practice, and how fast.
- Whether the rich regions of the world would choose to join.
- Whether planning can work beyond the three kinds of capacity tested here.
- How much leakage there would be in building homes and power plants, rather than in cash and food programmes.
- What outside experts will find when they review the work.
