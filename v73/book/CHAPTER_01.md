# Chapter 1 — Enough for everyone

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.5–V6.6) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine two kitchens on the same evening.

In the first, in a suburb of Lyon, a family clears the table. Half a baguette goes into the bin, and so does a bowl of salad nobody finished. The fridge holds more than the family will eat this week, and some of it will be thrown away on Sunday. Nobody in this kitchen thinks of themselves as wasteful. This is simply what normal looks like when food is never in doubt.

In the second kitchen, in a town in northern Nigeria, a mother divides a pot of millet porridge among five children. Tonight there is enough for everyone to eat something. Tomorrow depends on the price at the market, on whether her husband finds work, on whether the rains came on time.

These two kitchens are not invented to make you feel guilty. They are composites, and they stand for two facts that the numbers in this chapter will make precise. The first is that the world, taken as a whole, already produces enough food for everyone. The second is that it does not reach everyone. This book is about the distance between those two facts, and about whether a different way of organising the world could close it.

This chapter is a stocktaking. It asks a plain question: if all the food, energy, health workers, homes and working hours that exist today were counted against what every person needs, would there be enough? It does not yet ask how to share them. That is the work of the rest of the book.

## How to count "enough"

Before the numbers, a word on method, because everything that follows rests on it.

The UNIRA research compares two quantities for each basic need.

The first is **supply**: what the world actually has today. It is measured in physical units. Food is counted in kilocalories, energy in gigajoules, health workers in people, homes in square metres. No money enters the count. A tonne of wheat is a tonne of wheat, whatever it sells for.

The second is **need**: what every person requires for a given standard of living, multiplied by the number of people. The research calls this *needs × population*. It is also the rule UNIRA proposes for sharing: each region receives according to what its people need and how many people live there.

The research uses two standards.

- **Decent** is a floor. It is the minimum for a dignified life: enough food, a safe home, clean water, basic health care, schooling and a way to get around. It comes from a body of research called the *Decent Living Standards*.
- **Comfortable** is UNIRA's aim. It sits above the floor but well below what the richest people use today. The research's own phrase for it is "comfortable, not castles".

Divide world supply by world need, and you get one number. If it is above 1, the world has enough. If it is below 1, it does not. A result of **1.16** means that for every 100 units the world needs, it has about 116.

The data cover **217 countries** and more than 99% of the world's people, about **8.14 billion** in 2024. They come from the World Bank, the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Health Organization, the International Labour Organization and other official sources.

## The world's pantry

Start with food, the most basic need of all.

Every year, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) adds up how much food each country produces, imports, exports, feeds to animals and loses before it reaches a shop. What remains is called the *dietary energy supply*: the calories available, on average, to each person each day.

For the world as a whole, that figure is about **3,017 kilocalories per person per day**.

How much does a person need? Researchers who study what a "decent life" requires put the food a person actually eats at around **2,000 to 2,150 kilocalories a day**, averaged across children and adults, active and less active people. Some food is always lost between the shop and the plate, and consumers waste about **19%** of what reaches them. Allowing for that, the world needs to supply about **2,590 kilocalories per person per day** for everyone to eat decently.

The world supplies 3,017. That is **1.16 times** what is needed.

There is enough. Not in a few rich countries, and not in theory: there is enough today, counted over all 8.1 billion people alive.

And yet about **1.6 billion people** live in countries where the average supply falls below that decent level. Inside those countries, and inside rich ones too, many people get less than the average.

How much food would have to move from where there is more than enough to where there is too little, for everyone to reach the decent level? About **7%** of the world's food supply. Not half, not a third: seven parts in a hundred.

### A closer look: from the field to the plate

The word "supply" hides a long journey, and it matters where along that journey the counting happens.

Think of a sack of rice. Some grain is lost before it ever reaches a shop, somewhere between the field and the shelf. The FAO measures this loss between harvest and retail at **13.3%** of food in 2023.

What survives reaches shops, markets, restaurants and homes. There, a second share is thrown away: food that goes off in the fridge, plates left half eaten, bread past its date. The UN Environment Programme measures this at **19%** of the food available to consumers. In 2022 that was **1.05 billion tonnes**, or about **132 kilograms** for every person on Earth.

The FAO's dietary energy supply is counted *after* most of the first loss. So the research applies only the second one, the 19% wasted by shops, restaurants and households. Count both together, and the world loses about **775 kilocalories per person per day**. Growing that lost food uses roughly **20%** of the world's farmland.

So the arithmetic runs like this. Of every 100 kilocalories that reach a shop or a home, about 81 are eaten. To put 2,100 kilocalories on each plate, about 2,590 must be supplied. The world supplies 3,017.

The FAO also publishes a minimum dietary energy requirement for the world: **1,836 kilocalories** a day in 2024. It is a cut-off set for a sedentary person, a floor rather than a target.

## Enough for comfort, if we waste less

"Decent" is a minimum. UNIRA aims higher: a *comfortable* life, where food is varied, plentiful and enjoyed, not just sufficient. Researchers put a comfortable diet about 15% above the decent level, around **2,470 kilocalories** actually eaten each day.

At today's level of waste, the world's food supply covers a comfortable diet for everyone almost exactly: **0.99 times** the need. One percent short.

Now halve consumer waste, from 19% to about 9.5%. This is not a fantasy: much household waste comes from buying too much, storing badly and throwing away food that is still good. Then the world's supply covers a comfortable diet for everyone **1.11 times** over.

Halving waste helps the decent standard too. The number of people living in countries below the decent level falls from about **1.57 billion** to about **0.48 billion**, before any food is moved at all.

| Food standard | Today's waste | Half the waste |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decent (2,100 kcal eaten) | **1.16** | **1.30** |
| Comfortable (2,470 kcal eaten) | **0.99** | **1.11** |
| Share of food that would need to move | about 7% | about 7% |

This is the first lesson of this book, and it will come back again and again. **Abundance for everyone is not mainly a question of producing more. It is a question of wasting less and sharing better.**

### An honest complication about waste

The two kitchens at the start of this chapter suggest that waste is a habit of the rich. The evidence is more awkward than that.

When researchers compare household food waste across countries grouped by income, the difference is small: about **7 kilograms** per person per year between groups. People in poorer countries throw food away too. In middle-income countries, waste can even rise as people overbuy "to avoid running out".

That matters for UNIRA. Halving waste is not simply asking rich households to be more careful. It is also about habits, storage, and the confidence that tomorrow's food will be there. Chapter 9 looks at that last point, and finds that security alone helps only a little with waste. Rules, storage and habits matter more.

### What about protein, and the planet?

Calories are not the whole of a diet. A person also needs protein. The World Health Organization puts the safe level at **0.83 grams per kilogram** of body weight each day, about **50 to 55 grams** for an adult. The world supplies about **92 grams** per person per day. Here too, there is enough on average.

But today's food is grown with today's methods, on today's land. Feeding about **9.7 billion people** in 2050 comfortably is a different question. The research finds that it fits within Earth's limits only if waste is halved *and* diets shift towards what is called a planetary-health diet, with less ruminant meat. Chapter 11 tells that part of the story.

## A decent life is more than food

Food is the easiest need to count. The same researchers have set floors for the other parts of a decent life too. They are worth seeing side by side, because they show how modest "decent" is, and how far "comfortable" sits above it.

| Need | Decent (minimum) | Comfortable (reference) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food eaten | 2,000–2,150 kcal a day | about 2,300–2,470 kcal |
| Final energy | **15.3 GJ** a year | up to about **40 GJ** |
| Home | about 15 m² per person, shared space included | about **27 m²** per person |
| Health workers | **44.5** per 10,000 people (WHO) | about **131** per 10,000 (EU) |
| Schooling | 9 years | 12 years |
| Domestic water | 50 litres a day (20 litres a basic emergency floor) | about 100 litres |
| Travel | 5,000–15,000 passenger-km a year | — |

Two points about this table matter for everything that follows.

**First, these are references, not commandments.** The researchers who set the decent floors say they "should be decided at a local level". A cold country needs more heating; a hot one, more water and cooling. An ageing population needs more care. The comfortable column is a choice UNIRA must make openly, not a scientific finding.

**Second, "comfortable" is not luxury.** For comparison, the average German has about **49.5 m²** of home. A UK study of a "good life", measured as a full consumption footprint, puts its energy at about **73 GJ** a year per person. UNIRA's comfortable level sits between the floor and today's rich-country habits.

## Energy: three times enough for a decent life

Food keeps us alive. Energy keeps the modern world running: heating and cooling homes, cooking, light, hot water, transport, hospitals, schools, factories.

Researchers have calculated how much energy a decent life needs if it is delivered with good, modern technology. It includes a home kept at a healthy temperature, a fridge, a washing machine, cooking, lighting, internet, public and private transport, and a share of schools, hospitals and food production. The answer is about **15.3 gigajoules of final energy per person per year**.

A gigajoule is a unit of energy. To give a feel for the scale: the decent-living study found that, depending on climate and population, countries need between **13 and 18.4** gigajoules for the same decent life. Another study, using a different method, found a range from about **9** in South Asia to about **36** in North America. Climate and the make-up of the population explain much of the difference.

Today the world uses close to **80 gigajoules of primary energy per person** each year. Converted to the energy people actually use at the point of use (final energy), that is about 51 (the International Energy Agency's estimate is nearer 55). The world already has **3.3 times** the energy a decent life requires.

What about comfort? A comfortable life, with larger homes, more travel and more appliances, but without excess, needs up to about **40 gigajoules** of final energy per person. The world has **1.27 times** that.

What the world cannot do is give everyone the energy an average European uses today: about **82 gigajoules** of final energy per person. That would need 1.6 times today's world supply. An American level is further out of reach still.

| Energy standard (final, per person per year) | World supply ÷ need | People in countries below it today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decent, 15.3 GJ | **3.33** | 2.07 billion |
| Comfortable, 40 GJ | **1.27** | 4.45 billion |
| Today's EU level, 82 GJ | **0.62** | 5.49 billion |

### A closer look: primary and final energy

Two words in this section need explaining, because confusing them leads to wrong answers.

*Primary energy* is energy as it is found: coal in the ground, oil in a well, the electricity a wind turbine produces. *Final energy* is what reaches the user: petrol in the tank, electricity at the plug, gas at the boiler. Between the two, much is lost, mostly as heat in power stations and engines.

All the decent-living studies count final energy. World statistics usually count primary energy. So the research had to convert one into the other. It used the European Union's ratio for 2024: final energy was about **0.66** of the energy available, or about two-thirds. Applied to the world, close to 80 gigajoules of primary energy becomes about 51 of final energy.

This is an approximation, and the research says so. The ratio differs between countries. It also changes as the world moves to solar and wind, which lose far less on the way to the user. That is one reason a clean energy system can deliver a comfortable life with less total energy than today.

### What equal shares would mean for one person

So here is the second lesson, and it is not always comfortable for readers in rich countries. **A comfortable life for everyone is possible. Today's rich-country consumption for everyone is not.**

If the world's energy were shared equally, a French person would use about 43% less energy than today: 51 instead of 90 gigajoules, still above the comfortable level. An American would use about 72% less. A person in India would have 2.7 times more; a person in Nigeria, about 9 times more.

It helps to picture what that means. Imagine a retired teacher in Lille and a young farmer near Kano. The teacher heats a family house, drives to see grandchildren and flies on holiday once a year. The farmer cooks over wood, has no fridge and walks to the market. These two lives are imagined, but the gap between them is the gap in the numbers. An equal share would mean the teacher's home is better insulated and heated by a heat pump, and the car trip is sometimes a train. It would mean the farmer has electric light, a fridge, a phone that charges at home, and a bus to town.

Neither would be living a castle life. Both would be living comfortably.

Energy, unlike grain, cannot be put in a ship and sent across the ocean. About **40%** of the world's final energy would have to "move" for everyone to reach the comfortable level. In practice, moving energy means building solar panels, wind turbines, grids and efficient homes where people live. That takes years, which is why Part III of this book looks at how fast it can be done, and within what climate limits. Chapter 10 shows that 40 gigajoules for everyone in 2050 adds up to about **6–13% less** energy than the world uses today.

## Health workers: the real shortage

If food and energy are a story of enough, health care is not.

The world has about **47 million** doctors, nurses and midwives, about **58 for every 10,000 people**. The World Health Organization considers **44.5** per 10,000 a minimum below which basic health goals cannot be met. On average the world is above that line. But health workers cannot be shipped like food. About **4.2 billion people** live in countries below the minimum. In those countries alone, about **7.7 million** health workers are missing.

The WHO's line is a low bar. It matches only middling progress on twelve basic health indicators that the WHO tracks. It marks a shortage, not a good service.

And the minimum is not comfort. The countries of the European Union have about **131** doctors, nurses and midwives per 10,000 people, about three times the WHO line. To give everyone on Earth that level of care, the world would need about **59 million more** health workers: more than double today's number. As the population grows towards 2050, the total need at that level comes to about **107 million**.

| Health-worker standard | World supply ÷ need | People in countries below it today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WHO minimum, 44.5 per 10,000 | 1.32 | 4.21 billion |
| EU level, 131 per 10,000 | **0.45** | 7.27 billion |

The first row looks reassuring: 1.32 is above 1. It is misleading. It would be true only if nurses could be moved around the world like sacks of grain. They cannot, and UNIRA rejects forced relocation. So the honest reading is the second column: billions of people live where there are too few.

This is the hardest number in the whole of the UNIRA research. Food can be redistributed this year. Energy systems can be built in a decade or two. A nurse takes three years to train, and a doctor ten. Chapter 8 shows how long it would take, and what disarmament and healthier lives can contribute.

### Why rich regions must not simply give up their nurses

Strict equal shares would be a mistake here, and the research says so plainly.

If health workers were shared equally, France would fall from about **134** health workers per 10,000 people to about **59**. That is still above the WHO minimum. But it would be a collapse in care for millions of French patients, and it would do little for Nigeria, because nurses cannot be moved by decision.

So for health, "equal shares" must mean something different. It means **building up** the regions that have too few, by training health workers where people live. It does not mean taking from the regions that have enough. This is one of the clearest places where UNIRA's rule of needs × population has to be applied with judgement, not mechanically.

## Homes: room to live

The fourth basic need is a home. Here, too, the world average hides enormous differences.

Across the world, people have on average about **26 square metres** of home per person. In Ethiopia the figure is about 7. In India, about 10.5. In France, about 45. In the United States, about 55.

To picture 7 square metres per person: a family of five would share about 35 square metres for sleeping, cooking, washing and living.

Researchers put a decent minimum at about 15 square metres per person, shared space included. Another study sets a floor of **30 m²** for a household, plus **10 m²** for each person beyond three. A comfortable level is about **27 m²** per person.

| Housing standard | World supply ÷ need | People in countries below it | New floor area to build |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decent, 15 m² per person | 1.70 | 3.5 billion | about **13 billion m²** |
| Comfortable, 27 m² per person | 0.94 | 5.0 billion | about **65 billion m²** |

Homes, like nurses, cannot be moved, so they must be built where people live, most of them in Africa and South Asia. The world's population is also still growing, which adds about another 40 billion square metres of need by the end of a 30-year plan.

Building on this scale has a cost in energy. Making the materials for a home takes about **2 to 4 gigajoules** per square metre. Spread over 30 years, 65 billion square metres comes to about **6.5 exajoules** a year. That is affordable in energy. It is heavy in materials and in workers. Chapter 7 shows that it can be done in about a generation, and why the materials matter as much as the speed.

One caution applies to every housing number. **No official world statistics on floor area exist.** These figures come from a regional model, not from counting homes. They are the best available estimate, and they are uncertain.

## Hands and hours

Everything above depends on one more resource: human work.

Today about **3.5 billion people** have a job, and they work on average about **41 hours a week**. UNIRA proposes a **30-hour week**, six hours a day, five days a week, so that families have time together. Would there be enough work to go round?

If 80% of adults of working age had a job, which is UNIRA's principle that everyone able to work does work, and each worked 30 hours, the world would have **88%** of the working hours it has today. The gap is 12%. That is about **4.2 billion** people at work, against 3.5 billion today.

The world's gap is smaller than France's. A 30-hour week in France, with nothing else changing, would remove about **17%** of its hours. The world has a larger reserve of people who could work but do not have a job today, so more of the gap can be filled by bringing them in.

How much work does a decent life really require? One study traced all the labour behind what people consume in the United Kingdom, including the supply chains abroad. It found that a decent standard of living for everyone took the equivalent of about **17.5 to 18 hours** of work a week. A "good life" took about **45.5 to 46 hours**. These are footprints under today's technology, measured in a different way from the rest of this chapter. But they suggest that the 30-hour week is not an extreme demand.

Chapter 3 shows how the 12% gap can be closed: by bringing into work the people who are excluded today, by stopping work that serves selling rather than needs, and by the higher productivity that comes with shorter, less exhausting days.

## Three questions a reader might ask

**"If there is enough food, why are people hungry?"** Because food is not shared by need today. The research is clear that the binding problem is distribution and waste, not production. The numbers in this chapter do not say that anyone is cruel. They say that the problem is how food is shared, not how much exists.

**"Won't rich countries simply lose?"** In energy, yes, somewhat. Under strict equal shares, French final energy would fall by about 43%, but still stay above the comfortable level. For food, French supply would fall from about **3,490** to **3,017** kilocalories per person per day, still above a comfortable diet. For health care and homes, rich regions would not give anything up; the poor regions would be built up. Chapter 15 shows that, in a full UNIRA, the richest regions would give up about 6–12% of their energy use, and gain peace, time and a stable climate.

**"Are these standards just Western ideas of a good life?"** Partly, and that is a fair worry. The decent-living floors were built to be universal: food, shelter, health, schooling, water and mobility. But the researchers themselves say the exact values should be set locally. The comfortable column draws on European levels. UNIRA's constitution leaves each region free to follow its own culture and traditions. What it shares across the world is the floor below which no one may fall.

## Back to the two kitchens

Return to the evening in Lyon and the evening in northern Nigeria.

Nothing in this chapter asks the family in Lyon to go hungry. Their supply would stay above a comfortable diet. What it asks is that less of their food ends up in the bin, and that the food the world already grows reaches the second kitchen too. Moving about seven parts in a hundred would do it.

The mother in the second kitchen would gain more than food. Her town would gain electricity, a clinic with enough nurses, and room to live. Those cannot be shipped. They have to be built and trained where she lives, over years.

That is the shape of the whole book. Some things can be shared quickly. Others take a generation. And the world, taken as one, already has more than most people think.

## What the evidence says

- The world already produces enough food for a decent diet for every person: **1.16 times** the need. With half the waste, it covers a comfortable diet: **1.11 times**. Moving about **7%** of food supply would reach everyone.
- About **19%** of food reaching consumers is wasted, and another **13.3%** is lost between harvest and shop. Together that is about **775 kilocalories** per person per day.
- The world has **3.3 times** the energy a decent life needs and **1.27 times** what a comfortable life needs. It does not have enough for everyone to live like today's Europeans or Americans.
- Health workers are the real shortage: **59 million more** are needed for everyone to have European-level care. For health, equal shares must mean building up, not taking away.
- A comfortable home for everyone means building about **65 billion square metres**, mostly in Africa and South Asia.
- A 30-hour week with universal work gives the world **88%** of today's working hours.

## What we still don't know

- These are national averages. Inside every country, rich and poor live very differently, and the averages hide that.
- The food-waste figures are reported by the UN Environment Programme. They are carefully estimated but uncertain.
- "Decent" and "comfortable" are defined by researchers. Different cultures and climates may need different amounts. A cold country needs more heating; an old population needs more care. The thresholds used here are not adjusted for climate, age or region.
- Converting primary to final energy uses the EU's ratio for the whole world. That is an approximation.
- There are no official statistics on floor area for most countries. The housing figures are model estimates.
- Working hours are missing for some countries, including China.
- The numbers show what is *physically* possible. They do not show that the world can organise itself to do it. That is what the rest of this book examines.
