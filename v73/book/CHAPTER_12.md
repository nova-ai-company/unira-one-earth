# Chapter 12 — No more wars

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.6–V7.6) and the draft Constitution of One Earth, and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine a corporal called Amina, on her last morning in uniform.

She hands in her rifle at the depot. A clerk checks the serial number against a list and signs a form. Outside, a bus waits to take her and thirty others to a nursing school in the next town. Her income does not stop. Her pay continues while she trains, and in three years she will be a nurse.

Amina is not a real person. She is a composite, and her morning has not happened anywhere yet. But each part of it comes from UNIRA's rules: the arms handed over, the full income, the offer of training in health. This chapter asks what the world gains when its armies stand down, and what could go wrong.

## What war costs today

Start with what the world spends on being ready for war.

In 2020 the world's armed forces counted about **27.4 million** people. That is the latest year with a complete count.

In 2025 the world spent about **USD 2,887 billion** on its militaries. That is **2.5%** of everything the world produces in a year. Spread over every person alive, it is about **USD 352** each.

Armies also burn fuel and need factories. Military activity, including the supply chains that make weapons and equipment, causes about **5.5%** of the world's greenhouse gas emissions. The estimate is uncertain: the range runs from 3.3% to 7.0%. Operations alone, without the supply chains, cause about 1.0%.

| What the world's militaries use | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| People in armed forces (2020) | **27.4 million** |
| Spending (2025) | **USD 2,887 billion** |
| Share of world output | **2.5%** |
| Spending per person on Earth | USD 352 |
| Spending per member of the armed forces | about USD 105,000 a year |
| Share of global emissions, with supply chains | **5.5%** (range 3.3–7.0%) |

None of this feeds anyone, houses anyone or heals anyone. It is the price of preparing for war.

### A closer look: how these numbers were counted

Each figure comes from a different source, and each has its limits.

The headcount comes from the World Bank. It is **27,406,000** people in 2020. Later years have no complete figure yet, so 2020 is the latest usable one.

The spending figure comes from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI), in its fact sheet of April 2026. The research checked it against the original document.

The emissions share comes from a 2022 study by Scientists for Global Responsibility and the Conflict and Environment Observatory. It puts military emissions at about **2,750 million tonnes** of CO₂-equivalent a year. Most of it comes not from operations, which cause about 1.0%, but from the supply chains behind them.

One more figure is missing entirely. No one publishes a global total for military *energy* use. The research looked and found none. That gap matters later in this chapter.

## What is freed

UNIRA's Constitution says it plainly: **war is abolished**. No region may keep armed forces or weapons of war, or make them. Existing armies are dissolved within **5 years** of a region becoming a full member.

What happens to the soldiers? Every one of them is offered work, training and full income. The Constitution names the fields: health, construction, civil protection, or service in the One Earth Peace Force. Nobody is thrown out of work. And nobody is forced into a job: Article 7 guarantees free choice of occupation.

### People

The 27.4 million soldiers are about **0.79%** of the world's labour force. That sounds small. But they are people already trained, organised and paid, who could do other work.

Chapter 8 showed that health workers are the world's hardest shortage. The research tested what happens if a quarter of today's soldiers volunteer to train in health. In the ambitious programme, half do.

The effect is largest early, when the shortage hurts most. By year 10 of the plan, volunteers lift the world to about **71 health workers per 10,000 people**, against **64** without them.

Over the full plan the effect is smaller. With central training capacity, expanded 7% a year, a quarter of soldiers volunteering brings EU-level care for everyone about a year sooner: **36** years instead of **37**. Schools and teachers are the real bottleneck, and former soldiers cannot train faster than schools can teach them. Disarmament helps. It does not replace building training capacity.

Other former soldiers can build homes, run logistics or work in civil protection. Building is a large need. Chapter 7 showed that a generation of comfortable homes needs about **240 to 265 million** construction workers at its peak. The world has about **294 million**, of whom only about **116 million** are in South Asia and Africa. Every pair of trained hands counts.

### Energy and climate

Chapter 10 explained that rich regions must use less energy while poorer regions catch up. The question is how fast.

To keep world energy at or below today's level, regions above the comfortable level must cut their energy use by about **4% a year**. Without weapons production and military fuel, about **3% a year** is enough.

That difference matters. A 3% yearly cut is hard. A 4% cut is harder still. In the model, the "no wars" case lets rich regions take the gentler path. World energy in year 30 is then about **400 EJ**, against **390 EJ** in the central case. Both stay below the model's **409 EJ** for today.

The research is clear about what this does and does not do. Freeing military energy matters mainly for the climate. It does not make poorer regions reach a comfortable level any faster. That still depends on how fast clean energy is built and shared.

One honest caveat. The 5.5% is a share of *emissions*, and the research uses it as a stand-in for energy. That is an assumption, stated openly.

### Money

Without armies, the world's 2.5% of output is mostly freed. Not all of it, because One Earth still needs a small armed force, as the next section explains. With that force in place, about **2.45%** of world output is freed.

That is a gross figure. It does not yet subtract the costs of the change itself. These include retraining and full income for 27 million former soldiers. They also include destroying weapons of mass destruction, and more police where policing is thin.

**A closer look at scale.** Two comparisons help. UN peacekeeping costs about **USD 5.6 billion** a year. That is about **0.2%** of what the world spends on its militaries. And rich countries' development aid in 2025 was about **USD 174.3 billion**, only **0.26%** of their national income, down from **0.34%** in 2024, against a target of **0.7%**. Set that beside the **USD 2,887 billion** spent on militaries. The world spends more than sixteen times as much preparing for war as it gives in aid.

## One Earth owns the arms

Abolishing war on paper is easy. Keeping the peace is not.

Disputes between regions still happen. Under UNIRA they go to the Court of One Earth, whose rulings bind. But a ruling needs to be enforceable. People whose rights are attacked need protection. Disasters need fast, organised help.

So the Constitution keeps one armed force: the **One Earth Peace Force**. **All arms belong to One Earth.** Nuclear, chemical and biological weapons are destroyed under independent checks and never rebuilt.

This creates an obvious danger. Whoever controls the only armed force could dominate everyone. Article 5a of the Constitution is written against that danger:
- **Purpose.** The Force enforces Court rulings, protects people whose rights are attacked, and helps in disasters. It is never used against peaceful protest, a lawful election or a region's democratic institutions.
- **Two keys.** It may be deployed only with a ruling of the Court **and** a double majority of the regions. If lives are under immediate threat, it may act for at most **72 hours** before it needs both.
- **No region controls it.** No region provides more than **5%** of its members or commanders. Commanders rotate every **3 years**. Members never serve in their own region.
- **A size limit.** The Force may never exceed **1 million** members, about 12 per 100,000 people. Its budget may never exceed **0.15%** of world output. Size, budget and every deployment are published each year.
- **Oversight.** An Inspector-General, appointed by the Court, reports publicly. Anyone harmed by the Force can take their case to the Court.
- **A counterweight.** Regions keep civilian police for everyday crime, under regional control, equipped only for policing.

Chapter 14 sets these controls beside UNIRA's other safeguards against power. Here the focus is on two practical questions: how big the Force should be, and whether such forces work.

## How big should the Peace Force be?

The closest real-world example is UN peacekeeping.

### A closer look: UN peacekeeping in numbers

In September 2025, UN peacekeeping had **59,376** uniformed personnel. They broke down like this:

| UN peacekeeping, 30 September 2025 | People |
| --- | --- |
| Troops | 50,963 |
| Police | 5,852 |
| Staff officers | 1,627 |
| Military observers | 934 |
| **Uniformed total** | **59,376** |
| All personnel, including civilians, in 11 operations | 67,783 |

They came from **121** countries. That already answers one doubt: a force drawn from many nations can work together.

The budget for 2024–25 was **USD 5.6 billion**. That is about **USD 94,000** per uniformed peacekeeper. The UN reimburses countries about **USD 1,428** per soldier per month.

Peacekeeping was larger a decade ago. In March 2015 it had **106,595** uniformed personnel in 16 operations, and a budget of **USD 8.47 billion**. Its peak, in April 2015, was about 108,000.

### A worked example: building up to 500,000

The research built its estimate in three steps.

1. **Start from the largest deployment ever sustained.** UN peacekeeping peaked at about **107,000** people deployed at once.
2. **Allow for rotation.** A force cannot keep every member deployed all the time. People rotate home, train and rest. The research multiplies by about **3**, which gives about **320,000**. That multiplier is a planning assumption, not a measured fact.
3. **Add a reserve for disasters and special tasks.** Enough for two or three Haiti-sized disasters at once, about **20,000** people each. Then add airlift, logistics, and checking the destruction of weapons of mass destruction.

The result is about **500,000** people. At a cost of about **USD 120,000** per member, that is about **USD 60 billion** a year.

| | Low | **Planned** | Constitutional ceiling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Members | 250,000 | **500,000** | 1,000,000 |
| Per 100,000 people | 3.1 | **6.1** | 12.3 |
| Share of today's armed forces | 0.9% | **1.8%** | 3.6% |
| Times the 2015 UN peak | 2.3 | **4.7** | 9.4 |
| Cost per member | USD 100,000 | **USD 120,000** | USD 150,000 |
| Budget | USD 25 bn | **USD 60 bn** | USD 150 bn |
| Share of world output | 0.02% | **0.05%** | 0.15% at most |
| People freed from armies | 27.16 m | **26.9 m** | 26.4 m |
| Share of world output freed (gross) | 2.48% | **2.45%** | 2.37% |

So the planned Peace Force is less than **2%** of today's armies. It would cost about **0.05%** of world output, against 2.5% today.

The research proposed writing the ceiling as a share of world population: **0.0125%**, about 1 million people. That way the limit moves with the world's population.

## What about disasters?

The largest disasters in recent memory needed tens of thousands of military personnel, not hundreds of thousands.

- **Haiti, 2010.** After the earthquake, the United States sent **21,493** armed-forces personnel within two weeks, with **25** ships and **79** helicopters.
- **The 2004 tsunami.** The United States had more than **13,000** military personnel in the region by 7 January 2005. A later figure of about 16,000 could not be checked, so the research does not use it.
- **Türkiye, 2023.** After the earthquake, about **5,000** international rescuers came in **90** teams.

Civilian systems already do much of this work. The European Union's Civil Protection Mechanism links the EU states and 10 other countries. It was activated **64** times in 2025, and more than **900** times since 2001. Its budget for 2021–27 is about **EUR 3.32 billion**, or roughly **EUR 0.47 billion** a year. Worldwide, about **632,000** people work in humanitarian crisis response, **90%** of them nationals of the country they serve. That last figure is reported secondhand.

So most disaster work can stay with civilians. Under the Constitution, the Peace Force responds "together with the Protection Service", UNIRA's civilian disaster body. How large that service should be is still to be studied.

## Does peacekeeping work?

A fair critic will ask whether soldiers in blue helmets actually keep anyone safe.

The research looked at two well-known studies. The first, by Hultman, Kathman and Shannon (2013), examined African civil wars from 1991 to 2008. It found that when more UN troops and police were deployed, fewer civilians were targeted. Several thousand troops with several hundred police made a large difference.

The second, by Walter, Howard and Fortna (2021), reviewed the numerical studies of peacekeeping as a whole. Its authors describe "an extraordinary relationship between peacekeepers and peace".

**A closer look.** Both claims were checked against the papers' own summaries. The first is a single study of one region and period. The second is a review of many studies, which is why its conclusion carries more weight. Neither is an experiment: they observe real missions in real wars.

Peacekeeping is not perfect, and these studies measure today's missions, not a world force. But the evidence points the same way: a neutral force, deployed in enough numbers, saves lives.

## The most dangerous moment

When is a world without armies most at risk? Not when it is finished, but while it is being built.

Research on military coups finds a clear pattern. A coup is an open attempt by the military or other elites to remove a government.

### A closer look: how coups are counted

The main dataset, by Powell and Thyne (2011), sets precise rules. A coup counts as *successful* if its leaders hold power for at least **7 days**. By that count, there were **7** coups between 2008 and 2010, and almost three dozen in that decade. Attempts have become less frequent since the end of the Cold War.

A study by Belkin and Schofer (2003) asked what makes coups likely. Risk is higher where civil society is weak, where government lacks legitimacy, and where coups have happened before.

### The warning for UNIRA

One study, by De Bruin (2018), has a warning that matters directly for UNIRA. Creating a **new** armed force raises the chance of a coup attempt the following year. Having several independent forces reduces the chance that a coup *succeeds*, but not the chance that one is *tried*.

So the transition is the period of highest risk. Old armies are dissolving. A new Peace Force is forming. Loyalties are uncertain.

The Constitution responds in three ways:
- the Force is built up **gradually**, with members from all regions from the first day;
- the Inspector-General is in place **before the first unit is formed**;
- regional police, under regional control, act as a counterweight.

The research also proposes keeping arms depots and supplies under separate control from combat command. That idea has not been tested by evidence. Another study, which found that measures to "coup-proof" a government deter coups, was seen only in a search summary. The research does not rely on it.

## Disarmament, step by step

How would standing down actually unfold in one region? The rules give the order of events. The sketch below is imagined, but every step comes from the draft constitution.

**First, the vote.** A region joins by referendum. It then passes through three stages: observer, associate and full member (Article 40). As an observer, it builds the safeguard institutions. Nothing about its army changes yet.

**Second, the guarantee.** At full membership, One Earth takes on that region's security. The region's armed forces must be dissolved within **five years** (T-5).

**Third, the offer.** Every member of the old army is offered work, training and full income. Some, like the imagined Amina, train as nurses, which takes three years. Others train as builders, or join civil protection. A few join the Peace Force, but they never serve in their own region.

**Fourth, the weapons.** Arms pass to One Earth. Nuclear, chemical and biological weapons are destroyed under independent checks and never rebuilt (Article 5).

**Throughout, the watchers.** The Peace Force grows gradually, with members from all regions from the first day. Its Inspector-General is in place before the first unit forms. Regional police keep everyday order.

The order matters. In the rules, dissolving an army follows full membership, and with it One Earth's guarantee. It does not come first.

## Countries that already live without armies

A world without armies sounds utopian. A few countries already live that way.

**Costa Rica** abolished its army in its 1949 Constitution. Article 12 reads: "The Army as a permanent institution is abolished." It keeps police for public order, and any military force must answer to civilian power. It has had no coup since. Whether the abolition deserves the credit is disputed.

**Iceland** has no standing army. It is the only member of NATO without one. Yet it was a founding member of NATO in 1949. A United States defence force was stationed there from 1951 to 2006. Its Coast Guard has about 280 staff.

**Panama** also has no army. Search results say it abolished its military in 1990 and banned it in its constitution in 1994, but the research could not verify those dates.

The World Bank records **0%** of output spent on the military in all three countries from 2020 to 2024.

But there is a lesson in the details. The World Bank still counts about 10,000 armed personnel in Costa Rica and 28,000 in Panama. These are police and border units that carry weapons.

"No army", in practice, means two things. It means armed police for public order. And it means someone outside guarantees the country's security. Under UNIRA, One Earth gives that guarantee to every region.

### How many police does a region need?

In functioning states today, there are roughly **130 to 350** police per 100,000 people.

| Police per 100,000 people | |
| --- | --- |
| Finland | 132 |
| India | 155 |
| Iceland | 184 |
| United States | 242 |
| Japan | 255 |
| Costa Rica | 297 |
| Panama | 315 |
| Germany | 349 |

A UN analysis from 2006, reported secondhand, put the middle value at about **300**. A figure often quoted as a "UN recommendation", 222 per 100,000, could not be found in any UN document, so the research does not use it.

At 300 per 100,000, the world would have about **24 million** police: roughly **50 times** the planned Peace Force. Everyday safety stays local, in the hands of each region.

## When wars end, people go home

War does not only kill. It drives people from their homes.

At the end of 2025, **117.8 million** people were forcibly displaced. That is about one person in every 70 on Earth. It was down from **123.2 million** a year earlier, the first fall in a decade. They included:
- **41.6 million** refugees;
- **9 million** asylum-seekers;
- **68.7 million** people displaced inside their own country by conflict.

By the UN refugee agency's own definition, every one of them fled persecution, conflict, violence or human rights abuse. These are the causes UNIRA sets out to remove. **70%** had been in exile for five years or more. Most did not go far: **65%** live in a neighbouring country, and **68%** in low- or middle-income countries.

The flows continue. In 2025, **5.4 million** people crossed a border to flee. In 2024, conflict forced **20.1 million** new moves inside countries.

Do people go back when wars end? History says many do.

| After the conflict | Who returned |
| --- | --- |
| Mozambique, peace accord of 1992 | about **1.7 million** refugees by mid-1995; 75% of 3.7 million internally displaced people resettled by mid-1994 |
| Syria, after December 2024 | more than 500,000 refugees by mid-May 2025, with 1.2 million internally displaced; **1.3 million** refugees during 2025 |
| Whole world, 2025 | **14.7 million** people: 4.4 million refugees and 10.3 million internally displaced |

In Mozambique, the yearly records show the wave: **604,387** refugees returned in 1993 and **804,376** in 1994. Worldwide, 2025 was the second-highest year for refugee returns since 1965.

### A closer look: what the research assumes

The research does not claim everyone goes home. Its working assumption is that about **50–85%** of people displaced by conflict return within roughly ten years. The rest settle where they are. New flows from war fall to about zero. That return share is an assumption, drawn from cases like Mozambique and Syria, not a measured world rate.

Returns are not automatic. The UN refugee agency says many of 2025's returns happened in hard conditions, including forced returns. Long exile, children born abroad and destroyed homes keep some people where they are. Those who stay should be treated as settled residents, not as refugees.

UNIRA also keeps a protection procedure for anyone threatened. Under Article 4b, protection comes first in the person's own region, through rebuilding and needs-first allocation. Where that is impossible, other regions share temporary shelter, paid for through the One Earth Fund. People return home when it is safe again.

### Peace does not stop the weather

In 2024, floods, storms and other disasters displaced **45.8 million** people, more than twice as many new moves as conflict caused that year. About **99%** were weather-related, and almost all stayed inside their own country. That is a separate problem, and Chapter 5 explains how UNIRA handles it.

## Honest answers to hard questions

**"What stops a region from secretly rearming?"**
The Constitution forbids any region to keep or produce weapons of war. Disputes go to the Court, and the Peace Force exists to enforce its rulings. But the evidence reviewed for this chapter does not test how hidden rearmament would be detected. That is a real gap, and it needs its own study.

**"Won't a single world force become a world tyrant?"**
That is the most serious objection, and the Constitution treats it as such. Two keys to deploy it. No region above 5%. Rotating command. A hard ceiling on size and budget. An Inspector-General who reports publicly. Regional police as a counterweight. None of this has been tested at world scale. The research says plainly that the build-up years are the riskiest.

**"Isn't disarming naive while others stay armed?"**
UNIRA does not ask a region to disarm and stand alone. Armies are dissolved within five years of *full* membership, and One Earth then guarantees each region's security, much as an outside guarantee long did for Iceland. How a union of only some regions protects itself while others remain armed is not answered by the evidence in this chapter. It belongs to the study of the transition in Chapter 15.

**"Will soldiers really want to become nurses and builders?"**
Nobody knows. The research tests a quarter and a half of soldiers volunteering for health, but these are assumptions. What UNIRA can promise is full income, training and free choice. Article 7 forbids forcing anyone into a particular job.

**"Without armies, won't crime fill the gap?"**
The draft constitution is careful here. It aims to remove the main causes of crime: poverty, insecurity, inequality and war. But it notes that the evidence does not support a claim that crime disappears. So it keeps independent courts and regional police. Abolishing war is not the same as abolishing wrongdoing.

## What the evidence says

- Today's militaries take **27.4 million** people, **USD 2,887 billion** a year (**2.5%** of world output) and cause about **5.5%** of global emissions, mostly through their supply chains.
- Disarmament frees people for health and building. Volunteers lift the world to about **71** health workers per 10,000 by year 10, against **64** without them.
- Without military energy, rich regions can cut energy use by about **3%** a year instead of **4%** and still keep the world within its climate limits. This helps the climate, not the speed of convergence.
- A Peace Force of about **500,000**, under a ceiling of **1 million** and **0.15%** of world output, frees about **26.9 million** people and **2.45%** of world output.
- UN peacekeeping, at **59,376** uniformed personnel from **121** countries and **USD 5.6 billion**, is found to reduce violence against civilians.
- The largest recent disasters needed tens of thousands of military personnel, not hundreds of thousands.
- New armed forces are most at risk of coup attempts while being built, which shapes how the Peace Force grows.
- All **117.8 million** forcibly displaced people fled causes UNIRA abolishes, and large returns follow peace: **14.7 million** people went home in 2025.

## What we still don't know

- No global statistic for military energy use exists. The 5.5% is an emissions share used as a proxy.
- The Peace Force size rests on assumptions: a threefold allowance for rotation, training and reserve, and a cost of USD 100,000–150,000 per member.
- The freed 2.45% is gross. Retraining soldiers, destroying weapons and expanding police all cost something first.
- How many soldiers would actually choose to train in health is unknown.
- The build-up of a new armed force is when coups are most likely. Whether Article 5a's safeguards are enough has never been tested at world scale.
- Separating arms depots from combat command is a proposal, not a tested safeguard.
- How hidden rearmament would be detected, and how a partial union stays secure, are not covered by this evidence.
- Some figures were seen only in search summaries or reported secondhand: Panama's dates, the UN police median, the humanitarian workforce. They are marked as such.
- The size of the civilian Protection Service is still to be studied.
- How many displaced people would return home is an assumption. Return rates vary widely.
