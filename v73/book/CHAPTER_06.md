# Chapter 6 — The U: money that serves people

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.4, V6.7, V7.2–V7.5) and the draft Constitution of One Earth, and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

A woman in Marseille buys a kilo of tomatoes. It costs **1 U**. It cost 1 U last year, and it will cost 1 U in ten years and in a hundred. She does not check the price, because it never changes.

She pays with her card. The payment goes straight to the cooperative that grew the tomatoes, with an invoice attached. Later that day the cooperative pays its workers' salaries. At the end of the year, whatever it does not need goes to the region.

This scene is imagined. The price of tomatoes is the author's own example, not a measured price. But the rules behind the scene are UNIRA's.

This chapter explains how the U works. It covers what people earn, what things cost, what happens to savings, and why the whole system stays stable. It also shows where the research found a serious flaw in the first design, and how the flaw was fixed.

## What the U is

The U is the single currency of One Earth. The draft constitution describes it in a few lines.

It is **digital**, with no physical cash. Every commercial transaction carries a verifiable invoice through a global transaction network. Personal gifts need no invoice.

A digital-only currency raises fair worries. What about privacy? What about people without a phone, or places without a signal? The constitution answers each one. The system must guarantee:
- **privacy**;
- **access without a smartphone**;
- **offline operation**;
- **independent oversight**;
- **the right to appeal**.

Why digital at all? One reason is honesty. In the research on leakage, direct digital payments cut losses in public programmes in India by **19–24%** (Chapter 14). A currency that leaves a trail is harder to steal.

## What people earn

Every adult who works earns a salary in U.
- The **base salary** is **5,000 U a month**.
- Salaries rise with **experience, knowledge and the complexity of the work**: 1.2 times the base, 1.3 times, and so on.
- No salary may exceed **5 times** the base, **25,000 U** a month.

The limit applies to everyone. That includes the owners and managers of enterprises. Their profit counts as salary, within the same limit.

So pay can differ by up to five times. But the basics of life do not depend on pay. Resources are shared between regions by need. Health care and education are free. A home is guaranteed to anyone without a salary. Pay decides how comfortable a life is, not whether it is decent.

### People who do not earn a salary

The rules protect everyone who cannot work for pay.
- An adult **unable to work for health reasons** receives **5,000 U a month**.
- A **retired person** receives an income for life, set by need. It covers food, daily life and one holiday a year. It is lower if they own their home. Couples receive an amount suited to two.
- Each **child** brings **1,000 U a month**. The limit is that adult income plus child allowances may not exceed **15,000 U**.
- **Health care and education are free**, for everyone.

Illness, disability, caring for others, study and retirement all exempt a person from the duty to work. They keep their income and their rights.

## Five households: a worked example

Rules are easier to grasp in a real budget. The five households below are imagined. Every amount follows from the rules and the research.

The retiree amounts are the research model's own proposal: **2,500 U** a month for a single person, **1.5 times** that for a couple, and **500 U** more for tenants.

| Household (imagined) | Adult income | Child allowance | Total per month |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sam**, single, base salary | 5,000 | — | **5,000 U** |
| **Ana and Joe**, two children; Ana at base, Joe at 1.3× | 5,000 + 6,500 = 11,500 | 2 × 1,000 = 2,000 | **13,500 U** |
| **Lea and Tom**, two children; both at 1.4× | 7,000 + 7,000 = 14,000 | limited to 1,000 | **15,000 U** |
| **Rosa**, unable to work, one child | 5,000 (guarantee) | 1,000 | **6,000 U** |
| **Mia and Raj**, retired couple, renting | 2,500 × 1.5 = 3,750 | — | 3,750 + 500 = **4,250 U** |

Three details are worth noticing.

**The child limit.** Ana and Joe earn 11,500 U. With two children, their total is 13,500 U, below the limit. So they receive the full allowance. Lea and Tom earn 14,000 U. Two full allowances would take them to 16,000 U, above the limit. So they receive 1,000 U, which brings them to exactly 15,000 U.

**The disability guarantee.** Rosa's 5,000 U counts as adult income for the child limit. But the constitution says the guarantee itself is never reduced by that limit.

**Retirement.** Mia and Raj receive less than a working couple. Their income is set by need, not by past pay. If they owned their home, they would receive 3,750 U, because they would pay no rent.

### Where the money goes

Now follow Ana and Joe's month. They have no home yet, so they save the most the rules allow, half their salaries.

| Ana and Joe, one month | U |
| --- | --- |
| Salaries | 11,500 |
| Child allowances | 2,000 |
| Saved (50% of salaries) | **5,750** |
| Left to spend | **7,750** |

Is 7,750 U a month enough? The research gives one anchor. A worker on the base salary who saves half spends **2,500 U** a month. That buys about **86%** of what an average French person consumes today.

Ana and Joe spend a little over three times that amount, for a family of four. Their health care and their children's schooling cost nothing. Saving half is not forever, either. It is meant to last mainly until the first home is paid. After that, a much lower rate is enough.

## What things cost

Prices of essentials are **fixed, permanently**. They are not set by bargaining, or by supply and demand. They are set by the human work and resources each good really takes.

### A closer look: how the fixed price was found

The research built a model of the whole France region. It contains every flow of U: salaries, guarantees, retiree incomes, child allowances, spending, saving and the purchase of homes. Every U has a sender and a receiver, and **11** accounting checks confirm that nothing is lost.

The model measures goods in **hours of embodied work**. That means all the work behind a product, including the supply chain. France's households use goods that embody about **16.4 billion** hours of work a year.

The fixed price was set **once**. It was chosen so that household spending buys exactly the goods available: no shortage, and no overproduction. The answer was about **146 U per hour of embodied work**. It never changes afterwards.

Two numbers follow from it.
- A base worker spending **2,500 U** a month buys goods embodying about **205 hours** of work a year. An average French person today consumes about **238**. That is the **86%**.
- A worker at the average multiplier buys about **1.6 times** what an average French person consumes today.

What about the tomatoes? At 146 U an hour, 1 U stands for about **25 seconds** of embodied work. That is simple arithmetic, not a measured price for tomatoes. It shows the idea: a price says how much human time a thing takes.

### Who earns what in the model

The model needed salary multipliers to work. It used illustrative values by occupation group, from **1.1** to **3.5** times the base. The average came to about **1.9**.

These values are placeholders. The research stresses that the real multipliers are for people to decide democratically.

## Following the U around the circuit

Where does each U go after it is spent? The model divides the economy into four parts:
- **households**, who earn, spend and save;
- **enterprises**, which produce goods and services;
- the **regional fund**, which pays public-service wages and guarantees;
- **One Earth**, which issues U and holds deposits.

Here is one year in the France region, in the first version of the rules.

| France region, one year | Billion U |
| --- | --- |
| Salaries, all workers | 3,822 |
| Disability guarantees | 69 |
| Retiree incomes | 387 |
| Child allowances | 64 |
| **Household income** | **4,342** |
| Saved (50% of adult income) | 1,945 |
| Spent on goods and services | 2,396 |
| Spent on new and renovated homes | 335 |
| **Saved but never spent** | **1,610** |

The model had about **33.5 million** workers. That is 88% of adults, following UNIRA's rule that everyone able to work does work.

Follow the circuit once. Households spend at enterprises. Enterprises pay salaries and keep what they need for production, maintenance and investment. Their surplus goes to the region. The region pays teachers, nurses, guarantees, pensions and child allowances. Whatever the region does not need goes to the One Earth Fund.

In a single year, everything balances. The research found UNIRA's money design coherent as a system of accounts.

### A closer look: the regional fund

The regional fund is where public services and guarantees are paid. In the model's first year, it paid about **1,108 billion U** in public-service wages. Everyone working in public services is paid from here. It also paid about **520 billion U** to households as guarantees, retiree incomes and child allowances.

Enterprises paid about **2,714 billion U** in salaries. Their surplus after production, maintenance and investment was small, about **18 billion U**. That makes sense. In UNIRA, enterprises do not exist to make a profit. They exist to produce what is needed.

So where does the region's money come from? In this first version, it did not balance on its own. The region needed One Earth to finance the gap, a gap exactly equal to the money households saved but never spent. That was the first clue that something in the design was wrong.

### A closer look: how credit works

Zero-interest credit sounds like a gift. In the accounts, it is simpler than that.

When a household borrows to buy a home, new U is created, with a matching debt. Each month, the household repays from salary. Each repayment cancels part of that U again. When the loan is paid, the U it created is gone.

No interest is charged, so the household repays exactly what it borrowed. A 300,000 U home costs 300,000 U, whether bought with savings or with credit. Nobody earns interest from a family's need for a roof.

The design rule adds one more line: credit is repaid directly from salary. That keeps the system simple. It also explains why credit cannot help people without a salary. For them, the rules guarantee public housing instead (Chapter 7).

### Sam's month

Go back to Sam, the single worker on the base salary. Sam has no home yet and wants one.

Sam earns **5,000 U**. Saving the maximum, Sam puts **2,500 U** aside and spends **2,500 U**. That spending buys about 86% of what an average French person consumes today. Sam's doctor's visits cost nothing. An evening class costs nothing either.

What does "modest but comfortable" mean? The design rule gives the author's own picture: food, one holiday a year, and new clothes twice a year. Someone who wants more can spend more, and simply saves less.

Saving at the maximum, Sam could buy a **100,000 U** home in about **3.3 years**. With zero-interest credit, Sam can move in sooner and repay from salary.

Sam is imagined. The amounts are the rules'.

But look at the last line of the table.

## What enterprises do with the U

If nobody competes for profit, what is an enterprise for? The draft constitution gives three short answers.

**Enterprises are free to organise and innovate.** There is no commercial competition for profit. But ideas and methods may be compared openly. A cooperative that finds a better way to grow tomatoes can show it, and others can copy it.

**Enterprises pay for what production needs first.** That means materials, wages, maintenance and investment in new equipment.

**Their surplus goes to the region**, and the region's surplus to the One Earth Fund.

The owners and managers of an enterprise are paid a salary, like everyone else. It sits on the same scale, with the same ceiling of 25,000 U a month. Nobody becomes very rich by owning a firm, because there is no profit to own.

This also changes what work is for. Chapter 2 showed how many hours today go into advertising, speculative finance and similar activities. In an economy without commercial competition, part of that work is no longer needed. Chapter 3 showed how those hours help pay for a 30-hour week.

## The trap of too much money

In the first design, households saved half their income every year. Of the **1,945 billion U** saved each year, only about **335 billion U** could become new homes. Builders, materials and land are limited. The other **1,610 billion U** sat in accounts, unspent.

That happens every year, and it adds up. After **10 years**, the unspent money equals about 7 years of the region's total spending. After **30 years**, it equals about **21 years**.

At fixed prices, that pile is dangerous. Each U is a claim on goods. If people ever tried to spend it, shelves would empty. This is what happened in economies that froze prices without balancing goods and money: queues, shortages and black markets. Chapter 4 tells that story.

### Could people just save less?

The research tested that too. At a saving rate of about **14%**, the region balances, with no pile of money. But then a single base earner needs **36 years** to buy a 300,000 U home. That is no better than today.

So the research looked for another answer.

## The money cap

UNIRA's answer is the **money cap**. Each person may hold money up to a limit. The limit is set every year by a published coefficient. Its purpose is to keep people working to produce what is needed.

Money above the cap goes to the **One Earth Fund**. So do large inheritances above the cap.

**Homes are not money, and they are never taken.**

### A closer look: the 60-year simulation

How do you test a rule for a whole lifetime? The research built a simulation of **6,000** adults. Their ages follow France's real population in 2024. Each person:
- works, and saves between 0% and 50% of their income;
- buys a home with savings and zero-interest credit, when builders can supply one;
- retires;
- dies, and passes on an inheritance, capped like everything else.

The simulation ran for **60 years**, three times with different random draws. The results below are the averages.

It compared three rules.

| After 60 years | No cap | Cap above a home's price | **Low cap with zero-interest credit** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cap, in years of base salary | — | 5.0 | **about 1.4** |
| Unspent money, in months of consumption (year 10 / 30 / 60) | 33 / 83 / **152** | 25 / 35 / 38 | 23 / **12** / **12** |
| Adults at the cap, year 60 | — | 28% | **57%** |
| Flow to the One Earth Fund, share of the region's consumption | about 0% | 20% | **23%** |

Without a cap, unspent money grows without limit. By year 60 it equals **152 months** of consumption, nearly **13 years**. And it is still rising.

A cap higher than the price of a home does not solve it. Money still piles up, more slowly.

A **low cap**, combined with zero-interest credit for homes, works. Unspent money settles at **12 months** of consumption, and stays there.

### What a low cap means for a person

The working cap is about **1.4 years** of base salary. A year of base salary is 60,000 U. So the cap is roughly **84,000 U** per person.

That is less than the price of a home. How can anyone buy a home, then? With **zero-interest credit**. People do not need to save the whole price first. They move in, and repay from their salary. Chapter 7 shows how this works for a family.

### Two effects at once

The cap does two jobs.

**It keeps money and goods in balance**, so fixed prices can last.

**It funds sharing.** About a fifth of the region's consumption value flows to the One Earth Fund each year. The Fund pays for new energy systems, homes and infrastructure in every region. It pays for the training of health workers and teachers. It covers guarantees wherever a region's own means fall short. It holds the catastrophe reserves.

Everything the Fund receives and spends is published in the Public Allocation Register. **Money above the cap returns to people, as resources.**

### An imagined case

Imagine a nurse called Ines, in her fifties. Her home is paid off. Her children have left. She earns above the base salary and spends modestly, so her savings grow.

One year her balance reaches the cap. From then on, what she does not spend flows to the Fund. Somewhere, it helps pay for a training school for nurses.

Does Ines stop working? The research's answer is careful. Her salary still pays for everything she buys, so she still has a reason to work. What she loses is the point of saving beyond the cap. She might spend more instead. Or she might choose to work fewer hours, if her work allows it.

Ines is imagined. The research has no evidence on how real people would behave at a cap. That is one of the biggest unknowns in this chapter.

## Rules that keep prices fixed for ever

The design rule says prices of essentials should not change "even after 1,000 years". That can hold only if a few other rules hold too.

**Average pay may rise only as fast as productivity.** Experience raises pay. If average pay crept up by **0.5%** a year without more goods, demand would exceed supply by **13%** after 30 years. At **1%** a year, the gap would be **28%**. So Article 14 links average pay to productivity.

**Productivity gains become free time, not cheaper goods.** The research also tested the opposite drift. If productivity rose **0.5%** a year while pay stayed fixed, goods would go unsold: demand would fall **14%** short of supply after 30 years. Under "no overproduction", the gain must become shorter hours.

The lifecycle simulation measured how much shorter.

| Productivity growth | Working week after 30 years | After 60 years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1% a year | **about 23 hours** | about 17 hours |
| 2% a year | about 17 hours | about 9 hours |

Fixed prices are kept, and nothing is overproduced. Machines and software become a source of free time, not of unemployment.

**Energy, travel and data are always paid per unit**, never at a flat rate. Chapter 9 explains why.

**Each region keeps studied reserves** of essentials, used and replaced before they spoil. A bad harvest or a disaster should never force prices up or empty the shelves.

## Who gains

Does all this leave ordinary people better or worse off? In rich regions, living standards are already high. The research compared today's income of each tenth of the population with what the **lowest** UNIRA salary buys.

| Region | People better off even at the lowest UNIRA salary | Gain for the poorest tenth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| France | **50%** | **+177%** |
| Germany | 50% | +153% |
| Italy | 50% | +244% |
| Spain | 50% | +258% |
| Poland | 40% | +146% |
| EU as a whole | 50% | +177% |

That is before counting free health care, education and guaranteed housing. It is also before counting everyone who earns above the base salary. Chapter 15 shows the full picture, tenth by tenth.

## Debts and the change itself

Today, according to UNCTAD, about **3.3 billion** people live in countries that spend more on interest than on health or education. Under UNIRA, **no region carries public debt**. Debts between member regions are cancelled. Bonds held by savers and pension funds count as their own protected savings.

How does a region move onto the U? Not overnight at a political rate. That was the mistake of 1990, when East Germany's output fell to about **half** its 1989 level within months (Chapter 4). Instead:
- wages move directly onto the UNIRA scale at full membership;
- savings convert so that they buy the same basket of essentials as before;
- the money cap phases in over **10 years**, applied to wealth held on the day of the joining vote;
- mortgages become zero-interest credit.

Chapter 15 sets out these transition rules in full.

## Questions a fair reader might ask

**"Without changing prices, how does anyone know what people want?"**
This is a real difficulty. In a market, prices rise when something is scarce. In UNIRA, the burden of balance moves to physical planning: measured needs, reserves, and incomes matched to the goods available. The research lists "coordination without prices" as a scientific challenge still to be tested.

**"How is the price of a new product set?"**
The research admits that new products need a price-setting rule. The principle is clear: the price follows the work and resources the product embodies. The detailed rule is not yet written.

**"Isn't the money cap just confiscation?"**
It changes property rights deeply, and the research says so. But three limits apply. Homes are never taken. The cap phases in over ten years. And money above the cap is not destroyed. It returns as homes, energy and care, published line by line.

**"With a cap, why would anyone work hard?"**
Salaries are still spent, so work still pays. What a cap removes is the reason to save beyond it. In the simulation, 57% of adults reached the cap. The research warns that this could weaken the motive to save. If people respond by spending more, demand rises, and the yearly coefficient must take that into account.

**"Doesn't a 5× pay range bring back inequality?"**
Pay differs, but rights do not. Food, a home, health care, education and an income are guaranteed to everyone. And the gap between the highest and lowest pay is capped at five times.

**"If saving causes so much trouble, why allow it at all?"**
Because people need it. Saving lets a family put money aside for a home, a project or a rainy day. The design rule keeps savings and protects them. What the research showed is that savings need a ceiling, not a ban. Below the cap, they are free and safe.

**"Won't a digital currency let someone watch everything I buy?"**
The risk is real, and the constitution names it. Privacy is one of five guarantees the payment system must give, with independent oversight and a right to appeal. Personal gifts need no invoice at all. The Public Allocation Register publishes amounts and places, never personal data. How well these promises hold in practice is untested.

**"What if I have no phone, or the network fails?"**
The system must work without a smartphone and must work offline. Both are written into the constitution, not left to a later choice.

**"What stops prices creeping up, as they do today?"**
Two rules. Prices of essentials are fixed by the constitution. And average pay may rise only as fast as productivity, so there is no extra money chasing the same goods.

## What the evidence says

- UNIRA's money rules balance as a system of accounts. A single fixed price, about **146 U per hour of embodied work**, matches spending to the goods available.
- A base worker saving half their income buys about **86%** of what an average French person consumes today. At the average multiplier, about **1.6 times**.
- If everyone saves half their income for life, unspent money piles up: **21 years** of consumption after 30 years in the first model, **152 months** after 60 in the lifecycle simulation.
- A low money cap, about **1.4 years** of base salary, with zero-interest credit for homes, holds unspent money at about **12 months** of consumption. About **20–23%** of a rich region's consumption value then flows to the One Earth Fund each year.
- Average pay must track productivity. A drift of **0.5%** a year opens a **13%** gap after 30 years.
- Half the people of France, Germany, Italy and Spain are better off even at the lowest UNIRA salary. The poorest tenth gains **146–258%**.

## What we still don't know

- How people really behave under a money cap. No such economy has ever been observed. If people spend instead of saving, demand rises and the coefficient must respond.
- How the yearly coefficient that sets the cap should be calculated. The constitution leaves this open.
- How prices are set for new products, and how preferences are read without market prices.
- The money cap is legally and politically hard. Some wealthy people may try to move money out before a vote.
- The salary multipliers and the share of people unable to work are illustrative values in the model.
- The model covers one region, France, one year at a time. Money flows between regions are not yet modelled.
- A home built today may embody about **3,600 hours** of work, roughly **525,000 U** at the fixed price. That is just above the 500,000 U ceiling (Chapter 7).
