# Chapter 7 — A home for every family

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.4–V7.2 and the draft constitution) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine a family of five in a city in India. They live in one room. At night the mattresses cover the whole floor. The eldest child does homework on the doorstep, because there is no table and no quiet. Their parents have work, and the home is not falling down. It is simply too small for five lives.

Now imagine a young couple in a French town. They both work. They have saved for years and still cannot buy a flat near their jobs. Each month, a large part of their pay goes on rent. They keep postponing a child until they have "a proper place".

Both scenes are imagined composites, not reports about real people. They stand for two different housing problems.

In the first, the world lacks space where people live. In the second, homes exist, but buying one takes much of a working life. This chapter shows how UNIRA tries to solve both. It uses clear rules for buying a home, and a building programme for the parts of the world where homes are missing.

## The housing rules

UNIRA's housing rules are short. Here they are in plain words.

- **A home costs between 100,000 and 500,000 U.** The price is fixed, and depends on the size and location of the home. The same rule covers buying, building and renovating.
- **Each adult may own at most two homes, and each family at most two in total.** Homes are comfortable, but they are not castles or very large.
- **People save to buy.** Saving is personal, up to a maximum of **50%** of salary.
- **Credit is free.** Zero-interest credit is available, repaid directly from salary.
- **An inherited extra home returns to the pool.** A home inherited beyond the heir's first home goes back to the regional pool, or the heir chooses which one to keep.
- **Public housing is guaranteed** for every adult without a salary.
- **New homes are built with low-carbon materials** where possible.
- **Homes are never seized.**

These rules are written into the draft constitution, in Article 18 ("Homes"). Article 6 adds a right. Every person has the right to "a safe, comfortable home of a decent size for their household, never seized and never subject to forced relocation".

## How long does it take to buy a home?

To understand the rules, it helps to put them in years of work. Chapter 6 introduced the salary scale. The base salary is **5,000 U** a month. Pay rises with experience, knowledge and the complexity of the work, up to five times the base. In the research's model of France, the average multiplier is about **1.9** times the base, an illustrative value.

The table shows how many years of saving it takes to buy a home, if a household saves half its income.

| Household | Home at 100,000 U | Home at 300,000 U | Home at 500,000 U |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One earner, base salary | **3.3 years** | 10.0 years | 16.7 years |
| Two earners, base salary | 1.7 years | 5.0 years | 8.3 years |
| One earner at 1.5× | 2.2 years | 6.7 years | 11.1 years |
| Two earners at 1.5× | 1.1 years | 3.3 years | 5.6 years |
| Two earners, average salary (1.9×) | **0.9 years** | **2.6 years** | 4.4 years |
| One earner at 3× | 1.1 years | 3.3 years | 5.6 years |
| One earner at the top, 5× | 0.7 years | 2.0 years | 3.3 years |

Two things stand out. Even the lowest-paid single worker can buy a modest home in a little over three years. And a couple on average pay can buy a mid-priced home in under three years.

Compare this with the French couple in the opening scene. Under UNIRA, credit carries no interest. The price of a home is fixed by its size and location. It does not rise because others are bidding for it.

## A worked example: buying with free credit

Saving first is only one way to buy. Zero-interest credit is the other. Here is how it works for an imagined couple, Nadia and Paul.

Both earn the base salary, **5,000 U** a month each. Together they earn **10,000 U**. They choose a home at **300,000 U**.

**Option 1: save first.** They save half their income, 5,000 U a month, or 60,000 U a year. After **5 years** they have 300,000 U, and they buy. That matches the table.

There is a catch. Chapter 6 showed that personal money is capped, at about **1.4 years** of base salary. That is about 84,000 U each, 168,000 U for the two of them. They cannot hold 300,000 U in savings. So in practice, they cannot save the whole price first.

**Option 2: borrow at zero interest.** They borrow 300,000 U and move in at once. Each month, 5,000 U is repaid directly from their salaries. After 60 months, **5 years**, the loan is paid.

| Nadia and Paul | Save first | Borrow at zero interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When they move in | after 5 years | **now** |
| Monthly amount set aside | 5,000 U | 5,000 U |
| Years until the home is theirs | 5 | 5 |
| Total paid for the home | 300,000 U | **300,000 U** |

The cost is the same. The difference is five years of living in their own home instead of waiting.

Once the loan is repaid, they no longer need to set half their pay aside. The research's recommended path is to save up to 50% mostly until the first home is paid, then at a much lower rate.

Nadia and Paul are imagined. The figures follow from the rules.

## A closer look: what is a home worth in hours?

A price in U is a price in human work. Chapter 6 showed that UNIRA's fixed price is about **146 U per hour of embodied work**, in the model of France.

So a **300,000 U** home pays for about **2,050 hours** of work. The full price range, 100,000 to 500,000 U, pays for roughly 680 to 3,400 hours.

Is that enough to build a comfortable home? The research found a warning sign. In France, construction embodies about **17.9 hours** of work for every €1,000 of output, counting the whole supply chain. So a home that costs about €200,000 to build today embodies about **3,600 hours** of work.

At UNIRA's fixed price, that is about **525,000 U**. It is just above the 500,000 U ceiling.

The euro figure is only a technical way to estimate hours. But the conclusion is in hours, and it matters. Either comfortable homes must be built with fewer hours, or the price range must be checked against the real work they contain. If homes were sold below their labour content, someone else would be paying the difference.

## Why saving half forever cannot work

The research found a problem with the original idea, and it is worth telling honestly.

If every household saved half its income every year, for its whole life, the money would pile up. In the France model, households would save about **1,945 billion U** a year. But only about **335 billion U** of that can become new or renovated homes each year. Builders, materials and land are limited. About **1,610 billion U** a year would be left unspent.

In the model, builders could deliver about **1.1 million** new or renovated homes a year. That figure assumes half of all construction investment goes to housing, an assumption the research marks as illustrative.

At fixed prices, the unspent money is a claim on goods that do not exist. After 30 years, it would equal **21 years** of total consumption. If people tried to spend it, shops would empty.

Could people simply save less? At about **14%** saving, the money balances. But then a single base earner needs **36 years** to buy a 300,000 U home. That is no better than today.

The answer combines three ideas:

1. **Save up to 50%, mostly until the first home is paid.** Saving is personal and voluntary. Fifty per cent is the maximum, for someone who lives modestly but comfortably.
2. **Use zero-interest credit** to bring the purchase forward. People move in early and repay from salary.
3. **Cap personal money.** Money above a yearly cap goes to the One Earth Fund, which spends it on homes, energy and care where they are needed.

Chapter 6 tells the money side of this story. Here is the housing side.

## What happens to homes over 60 years

The research simulated **6,000** adults over **60 years**. They work, save, buy homes, retire, die and pass on what they own. The simulation compared a world with no money cap and a world with a low cap and free credit.

| Housing result | No money cap | Low cap with zero-interest credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home ownership after 30 years | **92%** | **91%** |
| Households with a second home, after 60 years | **60%** | **38%** |

With the low cap and free credit, home ownership barely changes. Almost everyone who wants a home still gets one. But far fewer homes sit as second homes while others wait.

## Where second homes come from

The simulation found something surprising. Most second homes do not come from buying. They come from **inheritance**.

A person who already owns a home inherits their parents' home. Now they own two. Meanwhile, renters in the same region wait **15 to 19 years** for a home.

The research first tried an obvious rule: block second-home purchases while anyone is still waiting. It changed nothing. Purchases were not the main cause.

So UNIRA added a rule, known in the research as P1. A home inherited beyond the heir's first home returns to the regional pool. Or the heir chooses which home to keep. The two-home maximum stays.

### An imagined case

Imagine Omar, who owns a flat in the city where he works. His mother dies and leaves him her house in the village where he grew up.

Under P1, Omar now holds two homes, but only one is his "first". He has a choice. He can keep the flat and let the village house return to the regional pool. Or he can keep the village house and let the flat go.

Either way, he keeps a home, and the one he prefers. The other is offered to a family that has none.

This is not confiscation of a home someone lives in. It is a rule about what an heir can add on top of the home they already have.

## Who is still renting?

After 30 years in the simulation, most people own their home. Those still renting are mostly **adults without a salary**: people not employed, or unable to work. Credit cannot help them, because credit is repaid from salary.

That is why the research proposed a second rule, P3: **guaranteed public housing** for adults without a salary. It is now in Article 18 of the draft constitution. A person who cannot work still has a safe home, as Article 6 promises.

Picture an imagined man, Kofi, who cannot work after an illness. He receives the guaranteed **5,000 U** a month. He cannot take on credit repaid from a salary he does not have. Under the rules, he does not need to. A public home is guaranteed to him.

## Homes are never seized

When people hear "money cap", many worry about their home. The draft constitution answers clearly.

Article 17 says: "Homes are not money and are never taken." The transition rules add that, when a region joins, **homes are kept**. A household that owns more than two homes may sell the extra ones to the regional pool within **10 years**. Existing mortgages become zero-interest U credit.

Savings above the cap go to the One Earth Fund. Homes do not. The difference is deliberate. A home is where a family lives, not a store of wealth.

## Who gets the house by the sea?

Here is a question the rules do not yet answer.

Prices are fixed by size and location. But some places are more wanted than others: a flat near the centre, a house by the sea. At a fixed price, more people may want such a home than there are homes.

In a market, the price would rise until fewer people wanted it. In UNIRA, the price does not move. So something else must decide.

The research lists this as a task still open: how to allocate desirable locations at fixed prices, when demand exceeds supply. The rule that first homes come before second homes helps. But a full answer, fair and simple, has not been designed. It is one of the honest gaps in this chapter.

## The world's housing gap

So far this chapter has looked at rules. But rules cannot build a house. In much of the world, the problem is not the price of homes. It is that there is not enough space.

Across the world, people have on average about **26 square metres** of home per person. That average hides huge differences.

| Region | Floor area per person |
| --- | --- |
| Ethiopia | about **7 m²** |
| India | about **10.5 m²** |
| World average | about 26 m² |
| France | about **45 m²** |
| Germany (2025) | about 49.5 m² |
| United States | about **55 m²** |

Within the European Union alone, the range runs from about **19** to **64 m²** per person.

### A closer look: how much space is "decent"?

Researchers who study decent living give two common answers.

One defines a decent home as **30 m²** for a household, plus **10 m²** for each person above three. That works out at about **10 m²** per person. Another includes shared space and arrives at about **15 m²** per person.

UNIRA uses **15 m²** as decent and about **27 m²** as comfortable. The comfortable figure comes from a scenario with households of about three people. Researchers stress that such thresholds "should be decided at a local level". So the choice of 27 m² is a design decision, not a law of nature.

There is one more caution. No official statistics give floor area per person for every country. The figures above come from a regional model. They are estimates, not counts.

### How big is the gap?

| Standard | World floor area ÷ need | People in regions below it | New floor area to build |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decent, 15 m² per person | **1.70** | 3.5 billion | about **13 billion m²** |
| Comfortable, 27 m² per person | **0.94** | 5.0 billion | about **65 billion m²** |

At the decent level, the world as a whole has enough floor area. But it is in the wrong places. At the comfortable level, the world is slightly short overall. Most of the gap is in South Asia and Africa.

Homes cannot be moved. And UNIRA forbids forced relocation. So the homes must be built where people already live. Rich regions are already above the comfortable level. The two-home rule, the rule to use existing buildings first, and the inheritance rule keep them from growing further.

## Building a generation of homes

How fast can 65 billion square metres be built? At today's building capacity, the research found that only about **40%** of the gap closes after 30 years. That is not good enough.

How much does the world build today? The research found that the world's housing stock grew by about **3.4 billion m²** in 2024. That is a *net* figure, after old homes are removed, so gross building is higher. China alone built about **7.3 billion m²** of new floor area in 2024, counting all kinds of buildings.

The research then tested faster plans. Building capacity in regions of need grows each year, by 5% or 10%. But it never grows faster than the highest rate ever sustained.

That record belongs to China in 2011: about **1,468 m²** per 1,000 people in one year. South Korea may have come close in 1990. It built about **750,000** dwellings that year, about **17.5** per 1,000 people. How close depends on the size of each dwelling, which the research had to assume.

The world's population is also growing. Adding the backlog of 65 billion m² to the new homes needed for population growth, about **109 billion m²** must be built or upgraded in total.

| Plan over 30 years | Everyone at 27 m² by | Gap closed by year 15 | Embodied CO₂ | Peak construction workers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Today's capacity | not within 30 years | 15% | 29 Gt | 87 million |
| Capacity +5% a year | year 27 | 39% | 43 Gt | 253 million |
| Capacity +10% a year | year 27 | 70% | 43 Gt | 253 million |
| +10%, stock-first | year 27 | 83% | 41 Gt | 265 million |
| **+10%, stock-first, low-carbon** | **year 27** | **83%** | **16 Gt** | 265 million |
| +5%, stock-first, low-carbon | year 27 | 54% | 15 Gt | 236 million |

The headline is simple. **Comfortable homes for everyone within about 27 years are possible**, if building capacity in regions of need grows by 5–10% a year. At its peak, that means about 6–7 billion m² a year, at China's past rate per person.

### Stock-first: use what exists

Article 11 of the draft constitution sets a principle. Before producing new, maintain, repair, renovate, reuse and convert what exists. For housing, that means extending and upgrading existing homes first.

The evidence suggests this could meet about **40%** of the need, using about **40%** of the materials of new building. Reusing an existing building emits **50–75%** less than building new.

India shows why. In 2012, its urban housing shortage was about **18.8 million** homes.

| India's urban shortage, 2012 | Homes | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overcrowding | 15.0 million | **80%** |
| Obsolete or unsafe houses | 3.3 million | **17%** |
| Homeless | 0.5 million | about 3% |

Overcrowded homes can often be extended where they stand. In Chile, upgrading a slum in place cost about **two-thirds** as much per household as moving its residents. And only upgrading in place produced lasting gains in housing quality.

Stock-first speeds the early years: **83%** of the gap closed by year 15, against 70% without it. But it cannot replace building. Most of the need is missing space.

### What about empty homes?

Many people ask: why build, when so many homes stand empty? The research checked.

| Place | Empty homes |
| --- | --- |
| China, urban (2017) | about **65 million**, 21.4% |
| European Union (2021) | **46.6 million** of 236 million, 19.7%, including second homes |
| India, urban (2011) | **11.1 million**, about 12% |
| Japan (2023) | **9.0 million**, 13.8% |

These are large numbers. But they are mostly in the wrong places. The largest stocks are in China, Japan and the European Union, and a home cannot be shipped.

India's empty urban homes are in a region of need. Yet counted as floor area, they would meet only about **0.5%** of the world's need. Using them is worthwhile. It is not a solution.

### Low-carbon: the material matters

Building 109 billion m² with conventional concrete would release about **41–43 gigatonnes of CO₂**. The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is about **130 gigatonnes**, counted from 2025. Concrete homes alone would use about a third of it.

Low-carbon homes change that. They use bamboo, timber, vaults or low-cement structures. A conventional reinforced-concrete home releases about **400 kg of CO₂** per square metre as it is built. A low-carbon home releases about **150 kg**. Across the whole programme, emissions fall to about **15–16 gigatonnes**, roughly 12% of the 1.5 °C budget.

The savings come from the frame, the floors and the roof, not the walls. In Kenya, cement makes up **83–91%** of the footprint of earth blocks. Changing blocks alone saves little. In Ecuador and Colombia, a two-storey social house with a bamboo structure released **27%** of the emissions of a concrete frame. It also cost **36%** less.

Chapter 11 places these figures beside the world's other limits on materials and land.

### People: the real limit

The binding limit is not money or materials. It is people.

How many hours does a square metre take? The evidence gives a range.

| Source | Hours of work per m² |
| --- | --- |
| Industrial precast housing, Finland | about **13** |
| Estimate for China, 2010 | about **33** |
| India's rural housing programme | up to about **64** |

India's rural programme takes about **201** person-days per house, with a minimum size of 25 m². The research uses **48 hours** per m², the midpoint of 33 and 64.

At its peak, the programme needs about **240 to 265 million** construction workers. The world has about **294 million** today. Only about **116 million** are in South Asia and Africa, where most homes are needed: about **91.6 million** in Southern Asia and **24.8 million** in Africa.

So the builders in those regions must roughly double. This is where UNIRA's other rules matter. Everyone able to work, works (Chapter 3). Soldiers leaving disbanded armies are offered work and training, including in construction (Chapter 12). Even so, it is a very large workforce programme.

## Questions a fair reader might ask

**"Why only two homes?"**
Because the space and the builders are limited, and many families still have none. Two homes allow a family home and, for example, a place near work or family. More than that would keep homes empty while others wait.

**"Isn't the inheritance rule just taking people's property?"**
The heir always keeps a home, and chooses which one. What returns to the pool is a home *beyond* the one they already have. Homes people live in are never seized.

**"What if I lose my job and cannot repay my credit?"**
The rules guarantee an income to anyone unable to work, and public housing to anyone without a salary. Homes are never seized. How repayments work during a long gap in pay is not yet set out in detail.

**"Wouldn't concrete be cheaper for poor regions?"**
Not always. In Ecuador and Colombia, the bamboo-framed house cost **36%** less than the concrete one. And at the scale of 109 billion m², concrete would use about a third of the 1.5 °C carbon budget.

**"Will my home in a rich region shrink?"**
No. Rich regions are already above the comfortable level, and homes are never taken. The rules stop them growing further, through the two-home limit, stock-first and the inheritance rule. New building goes mostly where space is missing.

**"Can the poorest regions really double their builders?"**
It has never been done at this scale. The research says plainly that it is a very large workforce programme. It depends on universal work, training, and former soldiers choosing construction.

## What the evidence says

- UNIRA homes cost **100,000–500,000 U**. A single base earner saving half their pay buys a 100,000 U home in about **3.3 years**; a couple on average pay buys a 300,000 U home in about **2.6 years**.
- Zero-interest credit lets families move in at once, for the same total price.
- Saving half for life does not work at fixed prices. A low money cap with zero-interest credit keeps money stable, with home ownership around **91%**.
- Second homes come mainly from inheritance. With the low cap and free credit, they fall from **60%** to **38%** of households in the simulation.
- People still renting are mostly adults without a salary. Guaranteed public housing is their answer.
- Comfortable homes for everyone (27 m² each) mean building or upgrading about **109 billion m²**. It can be done in about **27 years** if building capacity grows 5–10% a year.
- Empty homes cover only about **0.5%** of the need in regions of need. Upgrading can meet about **40%**.
- Low-carbon construction cuts emissions from about **41–43 Gt** to **15–16 Gt** of CO₂.
- The real limit is workers: about **240–265 million** at the peak, against **294 million** in the world today.

## What we still don't know

- **Is the price range right?** A home embodying about 3,600 hours of work would be worth about **525,000 U** at UNIRA's fixed price, above the 500,000 U ceiling.
- **Who gets the most wanted locations** at fixed prices, when more people want them than there are homes.
- **How much work does a self-built home take?** No verified figure was found per square metre.
- **How much carbon do African and Indian concrete homes really emit?** No whole-house assessment was found.
- The share of homes that can be upgraded, and the materials it saves, are partly judgement.
- Building capacity was treated for the world as a whole, not region by region. No source splits construction by income group.
- The floor-area figures are model estimates. No official country statistics exist.
- The money simulation covers one region, France, with illustrative salary multipliers. How people behave when they reach the money cap is not known.
