# Chapter 10 — A comfortable life inside the climate budget

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.5–V7.1) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Picture two evenings in 2050.

In a flat in Lille, a retired teacher turns up the heat pump on a cold night. The flat was insulated twenty years ago. The electricity comes from wind farms in the North Sea and panels on the roofs of the street. A small screen by the door shows what the evening has used, and what each unit costs. The price has not changed in years.

In a village near Kano, in northern Nigeria, a young nurse comes home from a late shift. The lights are on. The fridge is cold, and so is the medicine stored in the clinic down the road. Twenty-five years earlier, the village had power for a few hours a day, when it had power at all. Now a solar field and a local grid run it day and night.

Neither person exists. They are composites, imagined to show one idea. The teacher in Lille uses less energy than their parents did. The nurse near Kano uses far more. Both live comfortably. And the world, taken together, uses less energy than it does today.

This chapter asks whether that picture fits inside the climate budget. The short answer is yes, under strict conditions. The longer answer is what this chapter is about.

## How much energy is "comfortable"?

Chapter 1 introduced the idea of a comfortable level of energy. It means a warm or cool home, hot water, cooking, appliances, light, internet, travel, and a fair share of schools, hospitals, farms and factories. It does not mean excess.

UNIRA sets that level at **40 gigajoules (GJ) of final energy per person per year**. Final energy is the energy people and machines actually use: electricity at the plug, heat in the radiator, fuel in the engine.

### A closer look: where 40 GJ comes from

The number is not a guess. It comes from a body of research on what a good life needs in energy terms. The main source is a 2020 study by Millward-Hopkins and colleagues.

That study built up energy needs from the bottom. It listed what people need, such as a home kept at a healthy temperature, a fridge, cooking, light and transport. It then asked how much energy each would take with the best available technology.

It found that a *decent* life needs about **15.3 GJ** of final energy per person per year. Across countries, the figure ranged from **13 to 18.4 GJ**. Climate and the age of the population drive that spread. A cold country needs more heat. The age of a population changes how much school and hospital space it needs.

The same study then added more comfort: larger homes, more travel, more appliances. Its higher scenarios reached **24 GJ** and then about **40 GJ** per person. UNIRA takes the top of that band.

Another study, by Kikstra and colleagues (2021), shows how much place matters. Its decent-living figure runs from about **9 GJ** in South Asia to about **36 GJ** in North America.

So 40 GJ sits well above what a decent life needs. It is a choice for comfort, not for bare survival. The research says plainly that choosing the level is a design decision, which UNIRA must make in the open.

### A closer look: final and primary energy

One trap catches many readers. Energy statistics come in two kinds.

*Primary* energy is what goes into the system: coal, gas, oil, wind, sunlight. *Final* energy is what arrives at the point of use. Some is lost in between.

In the European Union in 2024, final energy was about **0.66** of the energy available at the start. So primary energy was about one and a half times final energy. A system that runs mainly on solar and wind has a much smaller gap, because those sources are counted at their direct output.

Every figure in this chapter is *final* energy. Comparing 40 GJ of final energy with a figure for primary energy would make UNIRA's target look smaller than it is. Chapter 1 gives the world's primary figure, with the same warning.

### The arithmetic for the whole world

By 2050, the United Nations expects about **9.66 billion people** on Earth. Give each of them 40 GJ, and the world needs **386 exajoules (EJ)** a year. (An exajoule is a billion gigajoules.)

How does that compare with today? Here there are two answers, and it is worth seeing both.

- According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), the world used **442 EJ** of final energy in 2022, about **55 GJ** per person. Against that, 386 EJ is about **13% less**.
- UNIRA's own transition model estimates today's world final energy at **409 EJ**. Against that, 386 EJ is about **6% less**.

So a comfortable life for everyone in 2050 means using about **6–13% less** energy than the world uses now, with more than a billion extra people.

Why do the two estimates differ? Counting the world's energy is hard. The IPCC's own figure for 2020 is **419 EJ**, with a range of **367 to 458**. The research keeps both of its estimates in view, and its conclusions hold with either.

That 386 EJ figure sounds surprising. It is possible because today's energy is shared so unequally. Many people use far more than 40 GJ, and billions use far less.

## Where 40 GJ sits among the climate pathways

Climate scientists have built hundreds of pathways that keep warming within limits. Each one says how much energy the world can use in 2050. The table puts UNIRA's level beside them.

| World final energy in 2050 | Total (EJ) | Per person (GJ) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decent living, the bare essentials (Millward-Hopkins 2020) | 149 | 15.4 |
| Low energy demand scenario (Grubler 2018) | 245 | 25.4 |
| IEA Net Zero by 2050 (2023 update) | 343 | 35.2 |
| **UNIRA: 40 GJ for everyone** | **386** | **40** |
| IPCC 1.5 °C pathways, median (range) | 410 (325–540) | 42.4 |
| IPCC 2 °C pathways, median | 448 | 46.4 |
| Today (2022) | 442 | about 55 |

UNIRA's level sits above the IEA's net-zero scenario and below the median of the IPCC's 1.5 °C pathways. It is **inside the 1.5 °C range**. It fits **comfortably under 2 °C**.

### A closer look: what the IPCC range means

The IPCC's 1.5 °C figure is not one forecast. It summarises **97** separate pathways. Each keeps warming near 1.5 °C with at least an even chance, with little or no overshoot.

The middle pathway uses **410 EJ** in 2050. The range shown, **325 to 540 EJ**, leaves out the most extreme five percent at each end. So UNIRA's 386 EJ is not at the edge of what scientists consider workable. It is a little below the middle.

The low-demand scenario by Grubler and colleagues goes further. At **245 EJ**, it is "around 40% lower than today". The IPCC also notes that decent living for all needs only about **150 EJ**, or **40%** of today's final energy. UNIRA does not aim that low. It aims for comfort, and still fits.

An independent check agrees with UNIRA's own number. Millward-Hopkins and colleagues found that 40 GJ per person gives "just under 400 EJ" for the world. That matches 386 EJ.

So energy itself is not the hard limit. As the research puts it, the binding limits for a comfortable life are carbon timing, diet and materials. The next chapter deals with the last two.

## The carbon budget: almost spent

Here is the uncomfortable part.

Warming depends on the total amount of carbon dioxide (CO₂) humanity releases, added up over time. Scientists therefore speak of a *carbon budget*: how much more CO₂ the world can emit before it passes a given level of warming.

| Remaining CO₂ budget from the start of 2025 | 50% chance | 67% chance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stay below 1.5 °C | **130 Gt** | **80 Gt** |
| Stay below 1.7 °C | 490 Gt | 390 Gt |
| Stay below 2 °C | **1,050 Gt** | **870 Gt** |

*Source: Forster et al. 2025. Gt means billion tonnes.*

In 2025 the world emitted about **42.2 Gt** of CO₂. At that rate, the 1.5 °C budget is gone in about **3 to 4 years**.

Human activity had already warmed the planet by about **1.36 °C** in 2024. Warming is rising by about **0.27 °C** every ten years.

### A closer look: a budget that keeps shrinking

Carbon budgets are recalculated every few years. Each new estimate starts from a later date, so the years of emissions in between have already been spent. Watching the numbers fall shows how fast the window is closing.

| Estimate | Counted from | 1.5 °C budget, 50% chance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IPCC, 2021 | 1 January 2020 | 500 Gt |
| Lamboll et al., 2023 | January 2023 | 250 Gt |
| Forster et al., 2025 | start of 2025 | **130 Gt** |
| Global Carbon Budget, 2025 | start of 2026 | 170 Gt, about 4 years of emissions |

The estimates do not line up perfectly. They use different methods and starting dates. The Forster paper even gives two starting dates in different places, the start of 2024 in one table caption and the start of 2025 in its text. The research notes this inconsistency rather than hiding it.

The message is the same in every row. For 1.5 °C, only a few years of today's emissions remain.

A sense of scale helps. From 1850 to 2019, humanity emitted about **2,390 Gt** of CO₂. The whole remaining 1.5 °C budget is a small fraction of that history.

### What this means

This leads to a hard conclusion, and the research states it plainly. **No energy level, however low, now avoids at least a temporary overshoot of 1.5 °C.** Not UNIRA's, not anyone's. "Staying at 1.5 °C" now means going over it for a while, then pulling CO₂ back out of the air to come down again.

The 2 °C limit is different. It is still open. Suppose emissions fell in a straight line from 2025. The world would reach net zero around **2066** and stay below 2 °C with a two-in-three chance.

What does that mean for one person? Today the average person emits about **5.2 tonnes** of CO₂ a year. A 1.5 °C-class world needs emissions near zero, net, by 2050. The research's central value allows about **0.35 tonnes** per person gross, with net emissions about zero. Even a 2 °C world allows only about **1.7 tonnes** per person.

## What a 40 GJ world needs

Fitting a comfortable life into this budget is not automatic. The research names three conditions.

**First, about half of all final energy must be electricity.** Today most energy is still burned as fuel. The IEA's net-zero scenario has electricity at **53%** of final energy in 2050; the IPCC 1.5 °C pathways, **52%**. In the IPCC's 2 °C pathways it is **42%**. Heat pumps, electric trains, buses and cars, and electric cooking do the same work with less energy.

**Second, fossil CO₂ must be near zero by 2050.** Coal, oil and gas can no longer be the backbone of the system. In the IPCC's 1.5 °C pathways, CO₂ from energy and industry in 2050 is about **8%** of its 2020 level. In the 2 °C pathways it is about **31%**.

**Third, solar and wind must be built much faster than ever before.**

### A closer look: how fast must we build?

That third condition deserves a careful look.

In 2025, the world added **692 gigawatts (GW)** of renewable capacity, a record. Of that, **510 GW** was solar and **159 GW** was wind: **669 GW** together. The year before, it had added about **582 GW**. By the end of 2025, the world had about **5.14 terawatts (TW)** of renewable capacity in total. A terawatt is a thousand gigawatts.

How much is needed? The research worked it out in two steps.

1. The IEA's net-zero scenario adds about **820 GW** of solar and **320 GW** of wind a year by 2030. That is about **1.14 TW** a year.
2. That scenario uses 343 EJ in 2050. UNIRA uses 386 EJ, so it needs somewhat more. Scaled up, the research arrives at about **1.3 TW** a year.

So solar and wind must be added at about **1.1 to 1.3 TW a year** by 2030. That is **1.7 to 1.9 times** the 2025 record.

The research calls this a "first-order" estimate. It is a scaling of someone else's scenario, not a detailed plan. It tells us the size of the task, not the route.

It is a big step. But it is not a fantasy. At the COP28 climate summit, the world agreed to triple renewable capacity by 2030: from **3,630 GW** in 2022 to **11,000 GW**. The renewable energy agency IRENA calculates that this needs about **1,122 GW** a year, and growth of **16.6%** a year. That is close to the pace UNIRA needs. In other words, the world has already signed up to something similar.

## Getting there: thirty years of change

A target for 2050 is one thing. The road to it is another.

UNIRA's research modelled a 30-year transition for about 180 world regions. Each year, new clean energy goes first to the regions furthest below 40 GJ. Regions above 40 GJ reduce their use. Population follows the UN's 2024 projection. The table shows four cases.

| 30-year case | Every region at 40 GJ by | World final energy, year 30 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low: solar and wind at the 2025 record pace, training as today | not within 30 years | 380 EJ |
| Central: 1.2 TW a year of clean energy | **year 27** | 390 EJ |
| Central, and no wars (military energy freed) | year 27 | 400 EJ |
| High: 1.8 TW a year | year 27 | 383 EJ |

In the central case, everyone reaches a comfortable energy level by **year 27**. At no point does the world need more energy than it uses now.

### A closer look: reading the four cases

Each row of the table changes one thing: how fast clean energy is built.

In the **low** case, solar and wind are built at the 2025 record pace and no faster. Health training also stays as it is today. Even after 30 years, some regions are still below 40 GJ. The record pace is simply not enough. That is why the research treats a build rate below the 2025 record as a test that would prove UNIRA's physical claims wrong.

In the **central** case, the world builds about **1.2 TW** a year. Every region reaches 40 GJ by year 27. World energy in year 30 is **390 EJ**, below today's level.

The **no-wars** case keeps the central build rate but frees military energy. Rich regions can then cut more slowly, 3% a year instead of 4%. World energy in year 30 is a little higher, **400 EJ**, but still below the model's 409 EJ for today.

In the **high** case, the world builds **1.8 TW** a year. Convergence still comes in year 27. In the model, building faster than the central pace does not bring that date any closer.

The low case ends with the *lowest* world energy, 380 EJ, yet it fails. Low total energy is not the goal in itself. The goal is enough for everyone, inside the limit.

That last point depends on rich regions. While poorer regions rise, the world total must not grow. The model starts from its own estimate of today's world final energy, **409 EJ**, and keeps the total at or below that. To do so, **regions above 40 GJ per person must cut their energy use by about 4% a year**.

If weapons production and military fuel stop, **3% a year** is enough. The world's armed forces account for about **5.5%** of global emissions. Chapter 12 returns to that.

### A worked example: what 3–4% a year adds up to

Yearly percentages are hard to feel. Here is the simple arithmetic.

A cut of 4% a year, kept up for ten years, leaves about two-thirds of the starting energy use. That is a fall of about a third in a decade. A cut of 3% a year leaves about three-quarters after ten years: a fall of about a quarter.

Think of the imagined teacher in Lille. Over a decade, their flat is insulated, their gas boiler becomes a heat pump, and the bus becomes an electric tram. Much of the cut comes from machines that do the same work with less energy. Some of it comes from choices, and the meter by the door makes those visible.

### Why restraint matters

Why is restraint so important? The research tested what happens if new energy goes to regions in need, but rich regions keep their use. World energy then peaks at **453 EJ**, outside the climate envelope.

**Sharing and restraint must go together.** One without the other fails. Sharing without restraint breaks the climate. Restraint without sharing leaves billions without enough.

### A closer look: where clean energy goes today

The model's key rule is that new clean energy goes first to where it is most needed. How different is that from today?

The research checked. Developing and emerging economies, leaving out China, receive only about **20%** of the world's investment in clean energy. Yet these countries hold about **two-thirds** of the world's people. Africa receives about **2%**.

When the model is run with that pattern, the world's energy gap does not close. By year 15 it has *grown*: the shortfall is **14%** worse than at the start. With an ambitious cooperative market, sending half of new capacity to regions in need, **14%** of the gap closes by year 15. Under UNIRA's needs-first rule, **60%** closes. Chapter 13 tells that comparison in full.

Even with realistic mistakes, the advantage holds. With strong safeguards against waste and theft, UNIRA still closes about **52%** of the energy gap by year 15. Chapter 14 explains how.

## What changes for each country

If the world's energy were shared equally today, the change would be very different from place to place.

| Region | Change in final energy per person under equal shares |
| --- | --- |
| France | **−43%** (from 90 to 51 GJ), still above comfortable |
| United States | **−72%** |
| India | **2.7 times** more |
| Nigeria | **9 times** more |

These are large numbers, and they need to be read carefully.

First, they show equal shares of *today's* energy. UNIRA's actual target is 40 GJ for everyone, reached over a generation, as new clean energy is built. Rich regions come down step by step, at 3–4% a year, not overnight.

Second, energy does not move like grain. It cannot be loaded onto a ship. A person in Nigeria gets more energy when solar fields, grids and efficient buildings are built in Nigeria. That is why this is a decades-long building programme.

Third, a French person at 51 GJ is still above the comfortable level. A cut in energy is not a cut in comfort. Much of it comes from insulation, heat pumps and electric transport, which do the same work with less.

What does it cost rich regions overall? The research compared each region after 30 years inside UNIRA with the same region in a reformed market world. By that measure, in the **33 richest regions** *average* energy use per person is **6–12%** lower: about 6% in France, about 12% in the United States and Japan. That is an average for the whole region, not a loss for every person. Nobody falls below the comfortable level, health care and housing are unchanged, and 44% of their people are better off even at the lowest UNIRA income (Chapter 15). The reduction falls on the heaviest energy users. Chapter 15 asks whether that is a price worth paying.

What is impossible is the opposite path. Giving everyone the energy an average European uses today, about **82 GJ**, would need **1.6 times** today's world supply. That does not fit any climate budget.

## The rebound trap

There is a risk hidden in all of this, and Chapter 9 explored it in detail.

When energy becomes cheaper or easier to use, people often use more of it. This is called the *rebound effect*. A better-insulated home may simply be kept warmer.

The evidence is clear:
- rebound from energy savings is usually **below 30%**, but it is real;
- homes on flat-rate heating use up to about **30% more** than metered homes;
- free public transport raised trips by **14%** in Tallinn and **70%** in Dunkirk.

Energy, unlike food, has no natural ceiling. People stop eating when they are full. They do not stop wanting warmth, light, speed or space in the same way.

The research tested what rebound would do to the plan. If rich regions lose **20–30%** of their energy savings to rebound, world energy peaks at **424–436 EJ**. That is above today's 409 EJ in the model, and **outside the 1.5 °C envelope**. With a rebound of only 10%, the peak is **413 EJ**, just above the line.

The answer is written into Article 19 of UNIRA's draft constitution. **Energy, travel and data are always metered and paid per unit**, at UNIRA's fixed prices. They are never offered at a flat rate or without limit.

That is why the teacher in Lille sees a meter by the door. It is not there to punish. It is there because every unit counts, and everyone can see it.

## Written into the constitution

The draft constitution turns these findings into law. Article 21 says that total energy use, emissions, materials, land and water "stay within limits set from the best science". It adds that regions using more than a comfortable level of energy reduce their use each year, while regions below it rise.

Article 10 says that new energy systems go to regions by measured need and number of people, needs first. Article 6 guarantees everyone "energy, transport and digital access sufficient for a comfortable life".

Together, these three articles are this chapter in legal form: enough for all, restraint at the top, and limits set by science.

## Honest answers to hard questions

**"No country has ever cut energy use 3–4% a year for decades. Isn't this impossible?"**
It has never been done. The research says so plainly, and it is one of the largest uncertainties in this book. What makes it plausible is that much of the cut can come from technology: heat pumps, insulation, electric transport. What makes it hard is that it must last for decades and win public consent. Nobody should pretend otherwise.

**"If 1.5 °C is already lost, why bother?"**
Because the limits are graded, not a single cliff edge. The budgets show it: about **490 Gt** for a 50% chance of staying under 1.7 °C, and about **1,050 Gt** for 2 °C. The 2 °C limit is still open. UNIRA's plan fits it comfortably, and fits 1.5 °C with a temporary overshoot.

**"Will a cold country and a hot country really get the same 40 GJ?"**
Not necessarily in practice. The 40 GJ is the planning level the research uses for every region. But the research also notes that its thresholds are not yet adjusted for climate or age. A cold, ageing region may need more heat. A hot one may need more cooling. How to adjust is still open.

**"Why should the poorest wait while the rich cut slowly?"**
They do not wait. Under the needs-first rule, each year's new clean energy goes first to the regions furthest below 40 GJ. Rich regions, already above 40 GJ, are cutting, not receiving. The cut at the top and the rise at the bottom happen at the same time.

**"What about poorer people inside rich regions? Won't they suffer from the cuts?"**
The research looked at this directly. At the lowest UNIRA salary, half the population of France and Germany is already better off than today, and the poorest tenth gains **146% to 258%**. Fixed prices also end household price shocks such as the energy-price crisis of 2022. Chapter 15 gives the details.

**"Isn't 40 GJ too generous? Why not aim for the decent level and save the planet more?"**
That is a fair challenge. A decent life needs far less, about **15 GJ**. UNIRA chooses comfort because its aim is a good life, not a minimum one. The evidence says that choice still fits inside the 1.5 °C range. Aiming lower would leave more room for error. Aiming at European levels would not fit at all.

## What the evidence says

- A comfortable 40 GJ of final energy per person totals **386 EJ** in 2050, about **6–13% less** than the world uses today (409 EJ in the UNIRA model, 442 EJ per the IEA). It sits inside the IPCC 1.5 °C range and comfortably under 2 °C.
- An independent study finds 40 GJ per person gives "just under 400 EJ", matching UNIRA's figure.
- It needs about **half** of final energy as electricity, fossil CO₂ near zero by 2050, and solar and wind built **1.7–1.9 times** faster than the 2025 record of **669 GW**. The world's COP28 tripling goal already implies about **1,122 GW** of renewables a year.
- The 1.5 °C carbon budget is about **3–4 years** of today's emissions. Any path now means a temporary overshoot and some CO₂ removal. 2 °C is still within reach.
- In a 30-year transition, every region reaches 40 GJ by **year 27**. World energy stays at or below today's level if rich regions cut energy use about **3–4%** a year. Without that restraint, it peaks at **453 EJ**.
- Today only about **20%** of clean-energy investment reaches developing economies outside China, home to two-thirds of humanity.
- Equal shares cut energy use in France by about **43%** and in the USA by about **72%**, and raise it **2.7 times** in India and **9 times** in Nigeria.
- A rebound of **20–30%** would push world energy outside the climate envelope. Energy must stay metered.

## What we still don't know

- No country has ever cut energy use by 3–4% a year for decades. Whether rich regions can do it, and how people will feel about it, is untested.
- Solar and wind at nearly twice the record pace has never been done. The 1.9× figure is a first-order estimate, not a detailed plan.
- How much CO₂ removal the overshoot will need, and whether it can be done at scale, is outside this research.
- Two estimates of today's world final energy are used: **442 EJ** from the IEA and **409 EJ** in UNIRA's transition model. The conclusions hold with either, but the gap shows the uncertainty.
- The carbon-budget estimates differ in method and starting date, and one source is internally inconsistent about its start.
- The carbon budgets are global. How emissions and the remaining budget are shared between regions has not been modelled.
- The energy thresholds are not adjusted for climate or age.
- The 2050 population of 9.66 billion was not re-checked against the UN source in this research.
- There is no global statistic for military energy use. The 5.5% figure is for emissions, used as a proxy.
- Rebound under a fixed-price, metered system has never been observed. The 10–30% values come from today's economies.
