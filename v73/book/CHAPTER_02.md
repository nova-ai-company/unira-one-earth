# Chapter 2 — Where our working hours go

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.2–V6.3) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Picture a Monday morning in France. Before most people wake, bakers are already kneading dough. Nurses are halfway through the night shift. Lorry drivers are on the motorway. By nine o'clock, most of the country's thirty million working people have started their day: in shops and offices, in factories, schools and hospitals, on building sites and in kitchens.

Each of those hours is a piece of someone's life. If we want to give people more time, for their children, their friends, their rest, we first need to know where the hours go today. What do they produce? Who do they serve? And how many of them serve real needs?

This chapter follows France's working hours, product by product, from the person who works to the person who uses what is made. France is our first "region" because its statistics are among the most detailed in the world. The method works for any country.

There is a reason to start here, before any talk of a shorter week. Chapter 1 showed that the world as a whole has enough food and energy for a comfortable life. But a 30-hour week is a promise about *time*, and time is the one resource that cannot be stored or shipped. To know whether a shorter week is possible, we need to know what today's long weeks are for.

## Forty-five billion hours

In 2023, people working in France worked about **45.6 billion hours** in total. That counts everyone: employees and self-employed, full-time and part-time. Spread over about **30.4 million** working people, it comes to about **1,500 hours** per person per year.

That number surprises many people. A full-time employee usually works about 39 hours a week, but part-time work, holidays, public holidays, sickness and training all bring the yearly average down. To understand a shorter week, we have to start from these real hours, not from the 35 hours written in French law.

The total splits into two groups:

| Group | Hours a year | People |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employees | 37.8 billion | 27.1 million |
| Self-employed | 7.8 billion | 3.3 million |
| **All** | **45.6 billion** | **30.4 million** |

Behind these averages are very different working lives. France's statistics office, INSEE, reports them separately:

| Who | Usual hours a week | Hours actually worked in a year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-time employee | **38.9** | **1,656** |
| Part-time employee | 23.2 | 966 |
| Self-employed | 41.3 | 1,903 |

About **17.6%** of employees work part time. The self-employed work the longest weeks of all.

Here is a worked example. A full-time employee usually works 38.9 hours a week. Over a year they actually work 1,656 hours. Divide one by the other, and the result is about **42.6** weeks of real work. The rest of the year goes to paid holidays, public holidays, sick days, training and the extra days off that French employees receive in return for working more than 35 hours.

One more complication deserves honesty. The national accounts and the household survey do not agree exactly. Using national-accounts hours, a full-time employee works about 10% fewer hours a year than INSEE's survey suggests. The two sources define hours differently, and the gap is not resolved. The research avoids the problem by working with *ratios* of weekly hours, which both sources agree on closely, rather than with yearly levels.

### A closer look: why real hours matter

It may seem pedantic to insist on the true number of hours. It is not. An earlier version of the UNIRA research got this wrong, and the mistake is worth telling.

Version 6.1 took the yearly hours of a *full-time* employee, 1,656, and applied them to all **30.5 million** people in work. Most part-timers and many others work far less than that. The result overstated how many extra workers a shorter week would need.

The same version also used a rule of thumb: 30 hours a week times 46 working weeks, or 1,380 hours a year. That sounds like a 30-hour week. But French employees actually work about 39 weeks a year in the national accounts, after holidays and absences. Spread over 39 weeks, 1,380 hours is about **35.7 hours** a week. So the "30-hour" scenario was not a 30-hour week at all.

An independent review, run separately from the main research, caught both errors. The current research measures weekly hours directly, by group, and caps them at 30. Chapter 3 uses the corrected numbers.

There is a second lesson in that review. Averages by sector can mislead. In public administration, education and health, average yearly hours per worker are already between **1,140 and 1,297**, below the 1,380 of the old rule of thumb. That is because of part-time work and school calendars. A model built on those averages would wrongly conclude that these sectors need no extra staff for a 30-hour week. In fact, the full-time nurses and teachers inside those averages work far more than 30 hours. Only by separating full-time and part-time workers does the real picture appear.

## Why hours, not euros

Most books about the economy count in money. This one counts in hours. The choice is deliberate, and it is worth explaining.

UNIRA would have its own currency, the U, with fixed prices and a salary scale set by rule. Its money would not work like today's euros or dollars. So the research never converts U into euros, and it never treats today's prices as a guide to what things will cost in UNIRA. Today's economy is used for one purpose only: to measure the physical starting point that any future must begin from. What is made, what it is made from, and how many hours of human work it takes.

Money can also hide what matters. Take one example from the research. A 30-hour week in France, with nothing else changing, would put about **€448 billion** of today's output "at risk", valued at market prices. That sounds alarming. But it is not a measure of how much worse off people would be. It counts advertising and nursing at whatever price they fetch today. An hour of work, by contrast, is the same size wherever it is spent. It is the unit in which a shorter week is actually paid for.

## Following the hours to the people who use them

Every product carries hours inside it. A loaf of bread contains the baker's hours, but also some of the farmer's, the miller's, the truck driver's, and the hours of the people who made the oven and the electricity. Economists can trace these chains using *input-output tables*, which show what each industry buys from every other.

We used France's official table for 2022, which counted about **45 billion hours**. We asked a simple question: when all the chains are followed to the end, who uses the hours?

| What the hours end up serving | Billion hours | Share of all hours | Share in 2019 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What households buy (food, clothes, housing services, leisure…) | 14.4 | **32.0%** | 33.9% |
| Public services (health, education, administration) | 11.6 | **25.6%** | 25.0% |
| Investment (new buildings, machines, roads) | 7.7 | **17.2%** | 17.8% |
| Exports (goods and services sent to other countries) | 9.7 | **21.5%** | 19.5% |
| Non-profit organisations | 1.5 | **3.4%** | 3.4% |

Only about **one hour in three** ends up in what households buy for themselves. A quarter goes to public services such as health care and schools. A sixth builds the future: homes, machines, infrastructure. And more than a fifth is work for people in other countries.

The 2019 column is a check. The 2022 table came just after the pandemic, which might have distorted it. The shares in 2019 are very close. The picture is not an accident of one odd year.

That last point matters for a world federation. France also *receives* work from other countries: if it made everything it imports itself, it would need about **10.9 billion** more hours. France sends about **9.7 billion** hours of work abroad in its exports. It receives more work from the world than it sends. In UNIRA, these flows stop being "trade" and become what they really are: people in different regions working for each other.

### A closer look: how an hour is followed

How can anyone know that a farmer's hour ends up in a restaurant meal, and not in an export or a school?

Here is a short illustration. It is imagined, but it follows the method exactly.

Imagine a plate of roast chicken served in a café in Toulouse. The cook and the waiter work directly for the customer. That is the first round of hours. But the café also bought the chicken from a food manufacturer, the vegetables from a wholesaler, and electricity from a power company. Each of those had workers. That is the second round. The food manufacturer, in turn, bought chickens from a farm, packaging from a factory and transport from a lorry firm. That is the third round. And so on, through dozens of rounds, each one smaller than the last.

An input-output table records, for every one of **65** product groups, what share of its inputs comes from each of the others. With a standard piece of mathematics, the research adds up all the rounds at once. For every product bought by a final user, it finds the total hours behind it, across the whole chain.

Three cautions apply, and the research states them openly.

- **The table is in euros.** It is used only to measure *how much of each input* goes into each product, never to price anything. But euros distort the picture a little. Services with high margins look as if they use more inputs than they really do. Later versions will use physical units such as tonnes and kilowatt-hours.
- **Some hours had to be estimated.** France does not publish hours for every small industry. For about **51%** of the hours, the research split a published total between related industries in proportion to the number of people working in each. That assumes equal hours per person inside a group. Retail, for example, has more part-time work than wholesale, so this method slightly overstates retail hours.
- **Imports are counted at French technology.** The "10.9 billion hours" in imports is how long it would take *France* to make those goods. It is not a count of hours actually worked in China or Germany.

## The longest chains

Which things that households buy carry the most hours, counting the whole chain behind them?

- **Shopping itself.** Retail trade carries about **3.0 billion** hours: the shops, the shelves, the tills, the stock rooms.
- **Restaurants and hotels:** about **2.4 billion** hours.
- **Food manufacturing:** about **1.3 billion** hours.
- **Insurance:** about **0.66 billion** hours.
- **Residential care** for elderly and disabled people: about **0.60 billion** hours.
- **Health care paid by households:** about **0.57 billion** hours.

Some of these hours are clearly essential. Others exist partly because of how today's economy is organised, which is the question of the next section.

Notice what tops the list. More of the hours in what French households buy go into *selling* things in shops than into *making* the food they eat. That is not a sign that shop workers are idle. Shops do real work: they store, display and hand over goods. But it shows how much of a modern economy is about getting things to people, rather than making them.

## Hours that serve selling

Some work exists mainly because businesses compete to sell. Advertising and market research tell us to buy one brand instead of another. Part of finance, and much real-estate dealing, moves money and property from one owner to the next without producing anything new. Part of legal, accounting and consulting work deals with competition, tax optimisation and disputes over property.

In UNIRA there is no commercial competition for profit, no speculation, and simpler rules. Some of this work would not disappear. People still need information, insurance against risk, legal advice and payments. But a share of it would.

The research flagged these activities as candidates for reduction:

- advertising and market research;
- real-estate services, not counting the rent that home-owners are deemed to pay themselves;
- financial services;
- brokerage and fund management;
- insurance;
- legal, accounting, head-office and consulting work.

How much? We cannot know exactly, and deciding which activities to reduce is a democratic choice, not a calculation. What we can measure is how many hours each level of reduction would free, including all the hours in the supply chains behind those activities:

| Reduction of market-specific activity | Hours freed | Share of all hours (2022) | Share in 2019 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Modest (5–25%) | about 0.8 billion | **1.7%** | 1.6% |
| Moderate (15–50%) | about 1.75 billion | **3.9%** | 3.7% |
| Deep (30–75%) | about 3.0 billion | **6.6%** | 6.3% |

These shares are not huge, but they matter, as Chapter 3 will show: they cover a real part of the hours a 30-hour week needs.

### A closer look: what "hours freed" means

The research uses a method called *hypothetical extraction*. It asks: if a product were reduced by a given share, as something people buy and as an input to other products, how many hours would disappear from the whole economy?

This gives an **upper bound**. It assumes that nobody who used the product needs a substitute. In reality, a firm that stops paying for advertising might spend some of that effort elsewhere. And UNIRA itself needs work that does not exist today: a payment and invoice network, allocation bodies, audits, democratic assemblies. Those hours have not yet been counted. So the true saving will be somewhat smaller than the table shows.

One trap is worth naming. Temporary-work agencies appear in the statistics as a large activity, but their workers actually work in factories, warehouses and offices. Removing the agencies would not remove that work, so we did not count it as saved.

### What these hours do for a shorter week

How much of the 30-hour gap could this work cover? Chapter 3 tells the full story, but the short answer is: a real part, not all.

Under central assumptions, the research finds that a 30-hour week in France leaves about **10.1%** of all hours uncovered, after the other offsets. A moderate reduction of market-specific activity brings that down to **6.2%**. A deep reduction brings it to **3.5%**. So a moderate reduction covers about **40%** of what is left.

This is the first part of UNIRA's advantage that the research could actually measure. It comes from one of UNIRA's institutions, the end of commercial competition for profit. It is not an assumed bonus. But a reformed market economy could also shrink some of these activities. Chapter 13 tests that fairly.

### Two objections

**"These are real jobs held by real people. What happens to them?"** Nobody in UNIRA loses their income or their place in society. Everyone able to work does work, and everyone keeps the right to choose their occupation. What changes is the work itself. The hours freed from selling are exactly what makes a shorter week possible for everyone. In the research, they are not lost. They are counted as hours available to cover the 30-hour week, in care, building and everything else that is needed.

It helps to picture one such change. Imagine Camille, who spends her days today testing which shade of blue on a cereal box sells best. She is imagined, not interviewed. In UNIRA, the cereal box no longer competes with another brand on the shelf, so most of that work is not needed. Camille keeps her income while she trains. She might choose the care-assistant course, which in France takes about **11 months**. Or she might use the same skills in surveys that measure what people in her region actually need, which UNIRA's planning depends on. Either way, her hours move from selling to serving.

**"Isn't insurance, or legal advice, useful?"** Often, yes. That is why no activity is cut to zero in any scenario. Even the deep level keeps between a quarter and seven-tenths of each activity. People still need protection against risk, help with the law and ways to pay. The question is only how much of today's volume exists because firms compete to sell, and that is for people to decide together.

## Where a shorter week bites

Knowing where the hours go also shows where a shorter week would be felt most. If every employee's usual week were capped at 30 hours, and nothing else changed, each sector would need to produce more per hour to keep its output. The research measured how much, sector by sector.

The answer differs widely:

- **Agriculture** would need only about **7.7%** more output per hour. Most of its hours are worked by the self-employed, who are not capped in this scenario.
- **Electricity and gas** would need about **30.4%**, the most of any sector, because almost everyone there works full time.
- **Health and social work** would need about **16.9%**, and other services about **14.3%**. These sectors already have many part-timers, whose hours a 30-hour cap barely touches.

Another way to see the same thing is in people. If the lost hours were filled by new workers with the same working patterns as today's, the extra people would be needed mostly in:

| Sector | Extra people needed |
| --- | --- |
| Trade (shops and wholesale) | 0.81 million |
| Health and social work | 0.78 million |
| Manufacturing | 0.73 million |
| Public administration | 0.66 million |
| Professional services | 0.56 million |
| Administrative support | 0.53 million |
| Education | 0.43 million |

Public administration, education and health together would need about **1.87 million** more people. These are the services UNIRA guarantees. They cannot be cut, and Chapter 3 shows that in care, working faster is not an option.

### A closer look: why a school has no productivity figure

It would be natural to ask how fast each sector becomes more productive on its own, and so how long each would take to absorb a shorter week. The research did measure this, and found two surprises.

First, in eight sectors, measured output per hour did not rise at all between 2015 and 2019. They include agriculture, energy, transport, hotels and restaurants, real estate, administrative support and other services. In those sectors, waiting for productivity to grow would never close the gap.

Second, in public administration, education and health, the figure means very little. Statisticians measure the "output" of a school or a hospital largely by what goes into it: the staff, the buildings, the supplies. Roughly speaking, more teachers counts as more teaching. So the official productivity trend in these sectors says almost nothing about how much more efficient they could become.

That is one more reason the research treats care separately. A nurse's hour at a bedside is not something that can be squeezed.

## Hours the statistics do not see

Everything in this chapter is *paid* work. It is what the national accounts count.

A great deal of work is not counted at all: cooking at home, cleaning, caring for children, looking after an elderly parent. This unpaid work is essential, yet the national accounts leave it out entirely.

A shorter paid week could change that hidden work in two ways. People would have more time for their families, which is the whole purpose of UNIRA's 30-hour week. But some of the time freed may also shift into unpaid work of this kind. The research has not measured how much. It is a real gap, and it is listed at the end of the chapter.

## Public services and investment are not on the list

It is worth saying clearly what the research does *not* propose to cut.

Public services take about **25.6%** of France's hours. They deliver health care, schooling and the administration that keeps a society running. UNIRA guarantees free health care and education for life. These hours are not a candidate for reduction. If anything, Chapter 3 shows that health and care will need *more* hours, not fewer.

Investment takes about **17.2%** of hours. It builds homes, hospitals, railways and power lines. In UNIRA, this is how the energy transition and the world's housing programme are made. These hours are not avoidable either.

So when people ask whether a shorter week means less of the things that matter, the answer from the data is: it should not. The hours that can be saved come from elsewhere.

## What stock-first saves, and where

UNIRA's Article 11 says: maintain, repair and reuse before building new. Things that last longer need replacing less often. How many French hours would that save?

Surprisingly few. The domestic hours that go into the durable goods households and businesses buy (electronics, machines, vehicles, furniture, textiles) are only about **0.9%** of France's hours, because most of these goods are made abroad. Even if they lasted twice as long, after allowing for the extra repair work, France would save only about **0.35%** of its hours. Making France's imported durables would take about **1.27 billion** hours, three times more than the domestic figure.

So stock-first mainly saves work, materials and emissions **in other regions**: in the factories of Asia and the mines of Africa and South America. That is exactly why UNIRA thinks of the world as one entity. A rule that looks small in one region can be large for humanity. Chapter 11 shows how much it matters for the planet's materials.

## Food

Producing the food French households buy, through agriculture, fishing and food manufacturing, takes about **3.6%** of all hours. Less food waste would save part of that, but the research has not yet measured how much in France. Chapter 1 showed that halving waste is also what makes a comfortable diet possible for everyone on Earth.

## Beyond France

France is a pilot region, not the whole story. When the research treats the European Union as one pooled region, the picture is similar. Every European region faces the challenge of a shorter week at the same time.

For the world, the research has not yet followed hours product by product. That needs a world input-output model, which is one of the next steps. Chapter 1 gave the world's working hours in total. This chapter shows, for one region, what those hours are for.

## Monday morning, seen again

Go back to that Monday in France.

The baker, the nurse and the lorry driver are still at work. So are the shop assistant, the teacher and the builder. Nothing in this chapter says their work is useless. Most of it is not.

What the chapter shows is the shape of the whole. About one hour in three ends up in what households buy, and of that, more goes into selling than into making the food. A quarter keeps public services running. A sixth builds the future. A fifth works for people in other regions, and slightly more comes back the other way. A few hours in every hundred exist mainly because firms compete to sell.

Those few hours are where UNIRA starts. They are not enough on their own to pay for a shorter week. But they are real, they are measurable, and they come from changing how the economy is organised, not from asking anyone to work harder. The next chapter puts them together with everything else.

## What the evidence says

- France worked about **45.6 billion hours** in 2023, about **1,500 per working person**. A full-time employee works about **1,656** hours a year; a part-timer, **966**.
- Only about **32%** of hours end up in what households buy. **25.6%** serve public services, **17.2%** build the future, and **21.5%** serve other countries. France receives about **10.9 billion** hours of work in imports and sends about **9.7 billion** in exports.
- Moderate reductions in advertising, speculative finance, real-estate dealing and part of legal and consulting work would free about **4%** of all hours. Deep reductions would free about **6.6%**. A moderate reduction covers about **40%** of the hours a 30-hour week leaves uncovered in the central case.
- Public services and investment are not candidates for cuts.
- Longer-lasting goods save few French hours but many hours abroad: imported durables carry about **1.27 billion** hours of work.

## What we still don't know

- The input-output tables are in euros. We use them only to trace how products depend on each other, but prices distort the picture slightly. Future versions will use physical units.
- About half of the hours had to be shared between related industries in proportion to the number of people working in them.
- The hours freed are upper bounds. UNIRA's own institutions will need hours that are not yet counted.
- The table is for 2022; the hours gap for a shorter week uses 2023. The two years differ slightly.
- Unpaid work at home is not in the national accounts. A shorter paid week may shift time into it, and that has not been measured.
- In public services, official productivity figures are measured mostly from inputs, so they say little about possible efficiency gains.
- Which activities to reduce, and by how much, is a choice for people to make together, not for a model.
