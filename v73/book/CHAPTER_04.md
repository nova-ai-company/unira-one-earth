# Chapter 4 — What we learned from the past

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA evidence reviews (V6.2, V6.3, V6.9, V7.1, V7.2) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine a woman in a small town in East Germany, in the summer of 1990.

She is in her fifties. She has worked in the same factory for most of her life. On 1 July her money changes. Her wages are converted one for one into the West German mark. So are the prices in the shops. Her first 4,000 marks of savings also convert one for one. The rest of her savings convert at two old marks for one new one.

By the autumn, the factory is in trouble. Its products now cost as much as Western ones, and few people want them. Within a few months, output in the region's industry falls to about half of what it was the year before. Her colleagues start to be laid off. Later, so is she.

This woman is a composite, not a real person. But the numbers behind her story are real, and they appear later in this chapter. She stands for a simple truth: big changes have been tried before. Some went well, some badly, most both.

UNIRA proposes a very large change. This chapter looks at six earlier experiments, and asks what each one teaches.

## The 35-hour week in France

France cut its legal working week by law. Under the Aubry laws, the legal week went from 39 to 35 hours. On paper, that is a cut of **10.3%**.

What happened in practice was smaller. Some firms met the target by no longer counting breaks and holidays as work time. Across the whole economy, actual hours fell by a little under **5%** on one official measure, and a little over **6%** in the national accounts.

Even today, French full-time employees report about **38.9 hours** in a usual week. A law on hours is not the same as fewer hours.

**What worked.** In the firms that signed up early and received support, the government's own evaluation found that output per hour rose by about **4 to 5%**. Productivity gains covered roughly **40 to 50%** of the hours cut. Planners had expected only a third. People worked better when they worked less.

When Portugal cut its legal week from 44 to 40 hours, output per hour rose by about **4.4%**. In Iceland, about **2,500** public workers moved from 40 hours to 35 or 36, with no pay cut. Productivity and service "remained the same or improved" in most of the workplaces in the trials.

**What did not work as hoped.** The 35-hour week was also meant to share work and create jobs. The official estimate is about **350,000 jobs** over the years 1998 to 2002. But those jobs came together with large cuts in payroll taxes and with wage moderation. Studies that compared France with itself more carefully, region against region or large firms against small ones, found no clear effect on total employment. One concluded that "aggregate employment was unaffected but labour turnover increased."

In Japan, the move from 48 to 40 hours "did not contribute to job creation". Cutting hours does not create jobs on its own.

### A closer look: why careful studies disagree with hopeful ones

How can one study find 350,000 new jobs and another find none? The answer lies in what each one compares, and it is a lesson for judging any reform, including UNIRA.

The government's evaluation looked at firms that signed up early for the 35-hour week and received large cuts in payroll taxes. In those firms, effective working time fell by a little more than 10%, and employment rose by about 6 to 7%. The planners had expected productivity to make up a third of the cut. The evaluation concluded it made up 40 to 50%.

But there was a catch, which the authors stated. They did not measure output directly. They *assumed* that output stayed the same, and worked out productivity from the hours and jobs. Another study compared the same kind of volunteer firms with firms still at 39 hours. It found employment up by about **9.9%**. But the firms that switched early had already been growing faster before the reform. They were not a fair comparison.

Two later studies looked for fairer comparisons. One used a quirk of French history. In Alsace-Moselle, the 35-hour rules were applied more weakly than in the rest of France. If shorter hours created jobs, employment there should have grown less. It did not differ significantly. The other compared large firms with small ones. It found that "aggregate employment was unaffected but labour turnover increased."

The pattern is common. **When firms choose a reform, and are paid to adopt it, the results look good. When a reform falls on everyone, the gains shrink.** The same caution applies to the four-day-week trials discussed in Chapter 3. UNIRA's 30-hour week would fall on everyone. So the research uses the cautious estimates, not the hopeful ones.

### Other countries, other cuts

France is not the only country that has cut its working week by law. The research gathered the best studies it could find:

| Country and reform | What happened to jobs |
| --- | --- |
| France, 1982: 40 to 39 hours | Directly affected workers lost jobs, by "between 2% and 4%"; minimum-wage workers were hit hardest |
| Germany, 1984–94: union agreements | The main study concluded that shorter hours "may have reduced employment" |
| Portugal, 1996: 44 to 40 hours | About 2% fewer jobs, mainly through fewer hires |
| Japan: 48 to 40 hours, phased | "Did not contribute to job creation"; each hour cut in law cut only about 0.14 hours in practice |
| Chile, 2005: 48 to 45 hours | "No significant effects" on moving into or out of work |
| Five European reforms, 1995–2007 | Full work-sharing rejected; small, uncertain effects |

The Japanese figure is worth pausing on. For each hour cut from the legal week, actual hours fell by only about a seventh of an hour. A law on hours is not the same as fewer hours, as France also learned.

Some reforms did raise output per hour. In South Korea, a move from 44 to 40 hours raised output per worker in manufacturing plants by about **1.5%**, even though each worker worked less. But Korean weeks started very long. Chapter 3 showed that cutting very long weeks costs little, because tired workers produce little in their last hours. The Korean result does not carry over to a cut from 39 to 30 hours.

**Health care is different.** In Iceland, most workplaces absorbed the shorter week. Hospitals could not. Extra staff had to be hired, and about **two-thirds** of the extra cost of the national rollout was in health care. A nurse cannot care for a patient faster just because their shift is shorter. And research across nine countries found that each extra patient per nurse raised the odds of a patient dying in hospital by **7%**.

Other studies looked at nurses' hours directly, from the other side. In one, errors were more likely when shifts lasted more than twelve hours, or when nurses worked more than forty hours a week. In another, covering **31,627** nurses in **12** countries, very long shifts were linked to exhaustion and to nurses wanting to leave. Long hours harm care. But the lesson does not run backwards. Cutting a reasonable week further does not make each nurse able to cover more beds.

**What UNIRA learns.** Chapter 3 showed that a 30-hour week in France would need about **20%** more output per hour if nothing else changed. The French and Portuguese experience suggests that some of that will come naturally from shorter, less tiring days. It will not come for free, and it will not come in hospitals.

So UNIRA does not rely on shorter hours alone. Its rule that everyone able to work does work answers the jobs lesson. People excluded today must be brought in deliberately, with training, childcare and eldercare. This has never been done at the scale UNIRA needs.

An enforced 35-hour week in France looks within reach on today's evidence. A 30-hour week is about **2.5 times** harder. UNIRA has chosen 30 hours anyway, for family time. That choice carries a real risk.

## Fixed prices without balance: the shortage economy

UNIRA proposes that the prices of essentials, like food and health, never change. This sounds reassuring. History says it is also dangerous.

The Soviet Union and other planned economies kept the prices of staple goods frozen for decades. The economist Kornai studied what followed, in his book *Economics of Shortage* (1980).

The result was not stability. It was queues, rationing, informal markets and ever-rising subsidies. **The fixed price held. The goods did not.**

A price is not only a number on a label. It also matches what people want to buy with what exists. If the price cannot move, something else must. In the shortage economy, that was queues, favours and empty shelves.

Shortage also feeds itself. When people fear that goods will run out, they buy more than they need. We saw this in a modern, rich country. In Ireland in March 2020, at the start of the pandemic, grocery spending rose by **23%** and toilet-roll sales by **181%**. Scarcity, or the fear of it, drives hoarding.

Planned economies had a second problem: units reported what suited them, not what was true. For the Soviet case, the UNIRA review found only descriptions, no reliable numbers. But the problem is not only historical. In China, local statistics inflated growth by **1.7 percentage points a year** between 2008 and 2016.

**What UNIRA learns.** This is perhaps the most important warning in the whole book. UNIRA keeps fixed prices for essentials. So the burden of balance moves entirely to *physical* supply. Several design rules exist because of this lesson:

- production is planned to measured needs, not to a target that suits a factory;
- each region keeps reserves of goods, sized to the shocks it has actually faced;
- total money income must not exceed the value of the essentials available at the fixed price;
- a yearly cap on personal money keeps savings from piling up into demand that cannot be met.

There is a hopeful side. UNIRA's research estimates that security could avoid **25 to 75%** of panic-buying spikes. That is a model estimate, not a measurement. Security does *not* curb the use of energy, travel and data, so those stay metered, unit by unit.

Whether these rules are enough is not proven. This is the failure UNIRA must never repeat.

### A closer look: testing UNIRA against its own warning

The research did not just quote Kornai. It built a model of UNIRA's own money, for the French region, and looked for the shortage economy inside it.

It found it. In the first design, every household saved half its income. Only a small part of that saving could become new homes, because builders can only build so fast. The rest piled up as unspent money: about **1,610 billion U** a year. After 30 years, the pile was worth **21 years** of everything households consume. At fixed prices, that money would be claims on goods that do not exist. If people ever tried to spend it, the result would be exactly the queues and empty shelves that fixed prices are meant to prevent.

This is why UNIRA has a money cap. With a low cap and zero-interest credit for homes, the model's unspent money settles at about **12 months** of consumption and stays there. Without a cap, it reaches **152 months** after 60 years and keeps rising. Chapter 6 explains how the cap works.

The model found a second, slower leak. If average pay drifts upwards a little faster than productivity, demand at fixed prices runs ahead of supply. So the draft constitution adds a rule: average pay may rise only as fast as productivity.

These are not proofs that UNIRA will avoid shortage. They show that its designers looked for the danger where Kornai found it, and wrote rules against it before anyone has to queue.

### Is UNIRA just the old planned economy again?

It is a fair question, and the honest answer is: partly, and that is why this chapter matters.

UNIRA shares two features with the Soviet system. A world entity decides which region produces what. And prices of essentials are fixed. Both failed badly in the past.

It differs in others. Regions join by referendum and may leave by vote. The plan starts from published, measured needs, with the uncertainty stated, not from targets that suit a ministry. Every allocation is public. Independent auditors check every region. People choose their own jobs. Money cannot pile up beyond a cap. And reserves are sized to the shocks each region has actually faced.

One problem remains unsolved, and the research names it. Deciding production for about **8 billion** people and millions of products is what economists call the *knowledge problem*. Modern planning tools and computers make parts of it manageable. Preferences, quality and local conditions still need regional knowledge and freedom. Whether a world allocator can manage this is one of the tests by which UNIRA could be proven wrong. Chapter 16 lists it.

## The welfare state: guarantees and how they leak

Many countries built public systems that guarantee health care, education, pensions and help for those who cannot work. UNIRA builds on that idea. Health care and education are free. People who cannot work receive a guaranteed income. Retirees receive an income set by need.

The UNIRA research did not study the welfare state as a whole. It studied how well public help reaches the people it is meant for. Three lessons stand out.

**People do not waste help.** A common fear is that money given to poor people will be spent on drink or cigarettes. Across **44** estimates from **19** studies of cash transfers, there was no increase in spending on alcohol and tobacco.

**Targeting the poor is hard.** Tests that try to identify poor households from a few indicators make large mistakes. In nine African countries, they classified **80%** of poor households as not poor. Brazil's Bolsa Família missed **59%** of the people it was meant for in 2004, and reached many it was not meant for. It was still among the most progressive programmes in the world. Imperfect help is still help.

**Money leaks, and leaks can be fixed.** In Uganda in the early 1990s, only **13%** of school grants reached the schools. After newspapers began publishing how much each school should receive, the share captured along the way fell from about **80%** to under **20%**. Random public audits in Brazil cut corruption by **7.9%**, and by **12.7%** with repeat audits.

**What UNIRA learns.** Universal guarantees avoid the worst targeting errors, because everyone is covered. But leakage is a real risk for any large system. UNIRA's safeguards, in Chapter 14, draw on these results: public information, independent audits and electronic payments.

## The European Union: joining step by step

The European Union is a voluntary union of nations. It is the closest real example of what UNIRA proposes.

**It grew slowly, by choice.** The union began with **6** members in 1958 and grew to **27**, in waves. Nobody was forced to join. Norway voted no twice, and that was respected. The United Kingdom left in 2020. Entry was not automatic: candidates had to meet published conditions, the Copenhagen criteria.

**Small transfers, big convergence.** Rich members send money to poorer ones through what is called cohesion policy. For the donors, the cost was small: only **0.3 to 0.4%** of their output. For the receivers, it was large. Between 2010 and 2017, net transfers were **4.0%** of output in Hungary, **2.7%** in Poland, **2.2%** in Slovakia and **1.9%** in Czechia.

The countries that joined in 2004 rose from **60%** to **80%** of the EU's average income. Poland's story is the clearest. In 1995, its income per person was **44%** of the EU average. By 2025 it was **81%**.

**What did not go perfectly.** Not all money flowed one way. Profits sent home to foreign owners of firms in these countries were larger than the EU transfers: **4.2 to 7.6%** of output. Most older members also kept their job markets closed to the new members for some years. For Bulgaria and Romania, those limits lasted until 2014.

In 2023, Europe's auditors found irregular spending of **9.3%** in cohesion funds. Irregular does not mean fraud, but leakage never reaches zero, even in well-organised systems.

**What UNIRA learns.** UNIRA's transition rules copy the EU's best features. Regions join by referendum, in three stages: observer, associate and full member. Each stage has published criteria. Regions may leave by democratic vote.

Modest transfers worked, but equalisation took decades. UNIRA's own model agrees: closing the gaps in energy, health and housing takes many years.

## German reunification: the shock of one day

Return to the woman in the opening scene. Her story is the story of German monetary union, on 1 July 1990.

**What worked.** Small savings were protected. Adults could convert up to **4,000 marks** at one for one. For children up to 14, the limit was **2,000**. For people aged 60 or more, it was **6,000**. Larger savings, company debts and housing loans were converted at two for one. Debts and savings were treated on the same terms.

**What failed.** Wages and prices were converted one for one in a single day, at a politically chosen rate. East German firms suddenly had to pay Western wages and compete at world prices. Only **8%** of workers were in firms that could still cover their running costs.

Within a few months, industrial output fell to about **half** its 1989 level. More than **3 million jobs**, about a third, disappeared. Within two years, real output fell to **two-thirds** of its 1989 level.

The agency set up to sell off East German firms, the Treuhand, handled about **8,500** firms with more than **4 million** employees. **2.5 million** were laid off. It closed with a deficit of **DM 256.4 billion**. Buyers pledged **1.5 million** jobs, and **20 to 30%** of those promised jobs never appeared.

Property was another trap. The two German states agreed on "restitution before compensation": property taken in the past would go back to former owners where possible. This created uncertainty over who owned what, and slowed investment.

**What UNIRA learns.** UNIRA's transition rules are written almost line by line against this experience:

- there is no single "political" exchange rate for a whole economy;
- small savings are protected in full, up to the money cap;
- homes people live in are never seized;
- old property claims are settled by compensation, never by giving property back;
- new members receive support to keep their production running during the change.

One risk remains. UNIRA pays everyone on the same scale, from 5,000 to 25,000 U. UNIRA enterprises do not compete on price, which removes the mechanism that destroyed East German firms. But where a region's physical productivity is low, the gap is still there, and it must be modelled, not assumed away.

## Debt relief: freeing poor countries to spend on people

Many of the world's poorest countries once spent more on paying debts than on their people. Before relief, the countries in the international debt-relief programmes for heavily indebted poor countries spent more on debt service than on health and education combined.

**What worked.** Two programmes cancelled much of that debt. They cost about **USD 76.2 billion** and **USD 43.3 billion**, about **USD 119.5 billion** together. **36** of the **39** eligible countries completed the process.

The effect was large. In 2017, these countries spent **7.3%** of their output on reducing poverty, against **1.9%** on debt service. After relief, they spent about **five times** more on social services than on debt.

**What it costs.** Across **180** government debt restructurings in **68** countries, lenders lost **37%** on average. Countries that imposed bigger losses then paid higher interest and took longer to borrow again.

**What UNIRA learns.** UNIRA goes further than any of these programmes. When a region becomes a full member, all its public debts are cancelled. Inside one world entity, a debt between regions would be the entity owing money to itself.

Two lessons shape how. Savers and pension funds holding government bonds are protected: their bonds count as their savings, up to the money cap. Debts owed to lenders outside UNIRA are paid on a schedule until their regions join, because refusing to pay outside lenders has historically brought lawsuits and asset seizures.

## Wealth taxes: the problem of the open door

UNIRA caps how much money any person may hold. Money above the cap goes to the One Earth Fund. The closest past experiments are wealth taxes, and they carry a warning.

France taxed large fortunes for decades. In 2007 the tax raised about **€4.42 billion**, about **1.5%** of tax receipts. But wealthy people could leave. In 2006 alone, **843** people left France because of the tax, taking a net **€2.8 billion** with them. The tax was abolished in 2017–18.

Norway raised its wealth and dividend taxes in 2022. At least **30** very rich people left that year, more than in the previous 13 years combined. Those who moved to Switzerland held about **NOK 29 billion**.

The lesson is precise. The losses did not come from mass flight. They came from a few hundred very wealthy households who moved. As long as the door is open, a cap on wealth in one place leaks to another.

**What UNIRA learns.** UNIRA keeps the door open: every person may emigrate, and every region may leave. So the cap is designed against flight. It applies to wealth held on the day a region votes to join, and transfers abroad after the vote still count. It phases in over **10 years**. And it applies across the whole federation, not one country at a time. These rules reduce the risk. They do not remove it.

## What this means for UNIRA

The changes that worked were **voluntary, staged and rule-based**. The EU grew over decades, by referendum, with clear conditions. Debt relief followed published rules. Small savings in Germany were protected by a simple, fair limit.

The changes that failed were **sudden, or ignored physical reality**. East Germany converted a whole economy in one day. The Soviet Union fixed prices without making sure the goods existed. France hoped a shorter week would create jobs on its own.

UNIRA's design learns from both sides. But honesty requires saying where it goes further than any past experiment:

- **30 hours from the start.** No evaluated reform has cut working time this far. All the good evidence comes from cuts of about 10% or less.
- **Fixed prices forever.** The economies that froze prices did not avoid shortages. UNIRA's physical rules are designed for this, but they are untested at scale.
- **A money cap.** Wealth taxes leak when wealthy people can leave, as France and Norway found. UNIRA's ten-year phase-in and anti-flight rule reduce this risk but do not remove it.
- **One world planner.** No allocator in the evidence has worked at this scale.

The past does not prove that UNIRA will work. It shows which mistakes to avoid, and which promises to keep modest.

## Six experiments at a glance

| Experiment | What worked | What failed | UNIRA's answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| France's 35-hour week | Output per hour rose in firms that adopted it | No net jobs without subsidies; actual hours fell less than the law | Universal work organised on purpose; care staff planned separately |
| Fixed prices in planned economies | Prices stayed fixed | Queues, rationing, hoarding, false reports | Physical balance, reserves, money cap, audited data |
| Welfare and public programmes | Cash is not wasted; transparency cuts leakage | Targeting misses the poor; money leaks | Universal guarantees; public register; audits |
| European Union | Voluntary, staged joining; modest transfers | Slow convergence; leakage stays above 5% | Three-stage joining; right to leave; needs-based transfers |
| German reunification | Small savings protected | One-day conversion; restitution froze investment | No political exchange rate; compensation, never restitution |
| Debt relief | More spending on people | Lenders' losses raise future borrowing costs | Public debts cancelled inside UNIRA; outside lenders paid on a schedule |

## What the evidence says

- France's 35-hour week raised output per hour in early-adopting firms by about **4 to 5%**, covering **40 to 50%** of the hours cut. It did not, on its own, create jobs across the economy.
- Studies of firms that chose and were paid to cut hours look better than studies of reforms that fell on everyone. The fair comparisons, in France, Portugal, Japan, Chile and Germany, find no net job creation.
- Health care cannot absorb shorter hours by working faster.
- Fixed prices without physical balance produced queues and rationing. The price held; the goods did not.
- Leakage in public programmes is real, but transparency and audits cut it sharply.
- The EU's voluntary, staged enlargement worked. Transfers of about **2 to 4%** of the receiver's output, costing donors **0.3 to 0.4%**, helped the 2004 entrants rise from **60%** to **80%** of the EU average.
- Converting East Germany's economy in one day left only **8%** of workers in viable firms.
- Wealth taxes leaked through a few hundred wealthy households who moved: **843** left France in 2006, at least **30** left Norway in 2022.
- Debt relief of about **USD 119.5 billion** let poor countries spend **7.3%** of output on poverty reduction, against **1.9%** on debt.

## What we still don't know

- Whether productivity gains from a cut from 39 to 35 hours continue from 35 to 30. The best evidence near 30 hours suggests output becomes close to proportional to hours.
- Whether people excluded from work today can be brought in at the scale UNIRA needs. It has never been done.
- Whether UNIRA's physical rules, reserves and money cap can keep fixed prices stable through centuries of shocks. No economy has tried.
- Whether wealthy people will move money out before a region votes to join, despite the anti-flight rule.
- Much of this evidence comes from Europe. It may not transfer to very different regions.
- Several figures in the research files were found only in search summaries and are not used here. Some lessons, such as the Soviet "ratchet effect", rest on descriptions rather than numbers.
