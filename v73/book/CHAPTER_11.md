# Chapter 11 — Materials, land and food

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.3, V6.5–V7.1) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

On a Saturday morning in a small town in Brittany, a repair workshop opens its doors. A man brings in a washing machine that has stopped spinning. A teenager brings a phone with a cracked screen. An older woman brings a lamp that belonged to her mother. By noon, most of them leave with their things working again.

Across the square, a family does its weekly shopping. They buy less meat than their parents did, and more beans, vegetables and bread. At home, the leftovers from Friday become Saturday's soup.

This scene is imagined. The workshop, the family and the soup are composites. But each of them stands for a choice that the numbers in this chapter make necessary. Chapter 10 showed that energy is not the hardest limit on a comfortable life for everyone. This chapter looks at the limits that are: the materials we dig out of the ground, the land we farm, and the food we grow and throw away.

## The heaviest number: materials

Everything we use is made of something. Stone and sand for buildings and roads. Metals for machines. Wood, crops and fuels. Researchers add all of this up, by weight, and call it *material extraction*.

In 1970, the world extracted about **30 billion tonnes** of materials a year. Today it extracts about **106 billion tonnes**. Per person, that rose from **23 kilograms a day** to **39 kilograms a day**. Over a year, that is about **14.2 tonnes** for every person on Earth.

If historical trends continue, extraction would rise by another **60%** by 2060. The UN's International Resource Panel also models a "systemic change" path, in which extraction peaks around 2040 instead.

### A worked example: 39 kilograms a day

Each day, for each person on Earth, about 39 kilograms of material is dug, cut, harvested or pumped out of the ground. Most of it we never see: it becomes buildings, roads, machines and fuel.

The sustainable level discussed below, about **5.2 tonnes** a year, works out at roughly **14 kilograms** a day. That is the size of the change: from about 39 kilograms a day to about 14.

### How much is sustainable?

Researchers do not agree on one number, but they agree on the range:
- O'Neill and colleagues (2018) use **7.2 tonnes** per person, based on a world limit of 50 billion tonnes a year shared among 7 billion people;
- Dittrich and colleagues (2012) put the limit at **8 tonnes** per person by 2030, also from a world total of 50 billion tonnes;
- the UN's International Resource Panel proposes **6 to 8 tonnes** per person by 2050;
- Bringezu proposes about **5 tonnes** (between 3 and 6), based on returning to the world's extraction in the year 2000, about **50.8 billion tonnes**.

**A closer look.** Notice that most of these limits rest on the same idea: a world total of about 50 billion tonnes a year. The per-person figure then depends on how many people share it. With **9.66 billion** people in 2050, a world limit of 50 billion tonnes gives **5.2 tonnes** per person. That is UNIRA's central value. Some of these sources were read only as cited in O'Neill's paper, not in the original texts. The research marks them as partly verified.

| Material footprint per person per year | Tonnes |
| --- | --- |
| Today, world average | **14.2** |
| Sustainable, high | 8 |
| Sustainable, central | **5.2** |
| Sustainable, low | 3 |

Going from 14.2 to 5.2 tonnes means a cut of about **2.7 times**. The research calls this the hardest constraint after carbon. It is harder than energy, and harder than food.

## Not only UNIRA's problem

This is not a problem only for UNIRA. In 2018, O'Neill and colleagues found that **no country meets the basic needs of its people at a globally sustainable level of resource use**.

Their study set a fair share of several planetary limits for each person. A few of them show how tight the limits are.

| O'Neill et al. 2018: sustainable share per person per year | Value |
| --- | --- |
| CO₂ emissions (consistent with 2 °C) | 1.61 tonnes |
| Material footprint | 7.2 tonnes |
| Water drawn from rivers, lakes and aquifers | 574 m³ |
| Nitrogen | 8.9 kg |
| Phosphorus | 0.89 kg |

Only **34%** of countries stayed within the CO₂ limit alone.

The study's conclusion was mixed. Physical needs could probably be met for everyone within limits. But wider, qualitative goals would take **2 to 6 times** the sustainable level of resources at today's efficiency.

A second study, by Fanning and colleagues (2022), tracked countries over time. The share of countries overshooting Earth's limits grew between 1992 and 2015, from **32–55%** to **50–66%**. On current trends, overshoot deepens to 2050, without ending the shortfalls of the poor.

So the question is not whether we can keep using materials the way rich countries do now. We cannot. The question is how to live well on much less.

## Stock first: use what already exists

UNIRA's answer starts with a rule. Article 11 of the draft constitution says: "Before producing new, society maintains, repairs, renovates, reuses and converts what exists." It adds: "Nothing is produced only to sustain sales. There is no waste and no overproduction."

What does this mean in practice? The research looked at France, following the work hours behind every product. Chapter 2 tells that story in detail. Here are the parts that matter for materials.

Durable goods, like electronics, appliances, vehicles, furniture and textiles, use only about **0.4 billion hours** of French work a year. That is just **0.9%** of all hours. Making them last longer, net of the extra repair work, would save only a little French work time:

| Durable goods last longer by | Share of French work hours saved |
| --- | --- |
| +25% | 0.14% |
| +50% | 0.23% |
| +100% (twice as long) | 0.35% |

Why so little? Because most durable goods are not made in France. **Imported durables embody about 1.27 billion hours** of work. That is **three times** the domestic figure.

### A closer look: what "hours-equivalent" means

The 1.27 billion hours is not a count of hours worked in China or Vietnam. The research has no data on that. Instead, it asks a different question: how many hours would France need to make those imported goods itself, with its own factories and methods?

That is called an *hours-equivalent*. It shows that a lot of labour sits inside imports. It does not measure the actual hours, materials or emissions abroad. Across all goods, France takes in about **10.9 billion** hours-equivalent through imports and sends out about **9.7 billion** through exports.

The table above also rests on one assumption. Each hour of manufacturing saved is assumed to need **0.25 hours** of extra repair work. That number is a synthetic assumption, not a measurement.

### Why repair is a world rule

So stock-first does little to shorten the French working week. Its main gains happen **abroad**: less labour, fewer materials and lower emissions in the countries that make our phones, machines and clothes.

For a world federation, that is a real gain. A washing machine that lasts twice as long means one fewer machine mined, smelted, shipped and assembled somewhere else.

This is one reason UNIRA treats the world as one economy. Seen from one country, repair looks like a small saving. Seen from the whole planet, it is one of the main ways to cut material use.

## Building: the biggest pile of materials

Buildings are where the materials question becomes concrete, often literally. Chapter 7 showed what comfortable homes for everyone require: building or upgrading about **109 billion square metres** of floor space over about 30 years, mostly in Africa and South Asia. This section looks only at what that means for materials and carbon.

Each square metre carries carbon before anyone moves in. This is called *embodied carbon*: the emissions from making cement, steel and bricks, transporting them, and building.

| Upfront embodied carbon per m² of floor | Central value (range) | Over the whole programme | Share of the 1.5 °C budget |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional reinforced concrete | **400 kg CO₂e** (250–600) | **43 Gt** | about a third |
| Low-carbon frame: bamboo, timber, vaults, low cement | **150 kg CO₂e** (30–265) | **16 Gt** | about 12% |

Built the conventional way, the homes would use about a third of the world's remaining 1.5 °C budget of **130 Gt**. Built with low-carbon frames, about an eighth.

### A closer look: where the savings really come from

The evidence on building materials has one surprise. Changing the walls saves much less than most people expect.

- In **Malaysia**, the same building made with a concrete frame and fired brick emitted about **406 kg** per m². With interlocking compressed earth bricks, it emitted about **265 kg**: a cut of **35%**.
- In **Nepal**, compressed earth blocks emit **75–78%** less than fired brick, by weight.
- In **Kenya**, cement makes up **83–91%** of the footprint of compressed earth blocks. At 10% cement, the blocks are no better than machine-cut stone.

So the big savings do not come from the walls. They come from replacing the concrete frame, floors and roof with bamboo, timber or low-cement designs. One study found bamboo buildings at about **30 kg** per m², against **240** for concrete or brick. Overall, the choice of structure cuts upfront carbon by about **3 to 8 times**.

The central low-carbon value of 150 kg is a judgement. The research found no whole-house carbon assessment for a concrete home in India or sub-Saharan Africa.

### A closer look: how fast the pile must grow

The materials question is also a question of speed. How much must be built each year?

The research answers in two steps. First, the backlog. Bringing everyone to a comfortable 27 m² per person means adding about **65 billion m²**. Building about **4.7 billion m²** a year, kept up steadily, closes that backlog by year 27.

Second, the full programme. The world's population is still growing, and some need is met by upgrading existing homes. Counting both, about **109 billion m²** must be built or upgraded. For that, building capacity in regions of need must grow by **5–10%** a year. It peaks at about **6–7 billion m²** a year, the rate per person that China reached in 2011.

For comparison, the world's stock of homes grew by about **3.4 billion m²** in 2024. That is a *net* figure: new building minus homes lost. Gross construction is higher. The research found no source that splits new building by countries' income group.

Every square metre also takes work: about **33 to 64 hours** of labour, in the research's range. That is why Chapter 7 calls people, not materials, the binding limit on the pace. Materials decide the carbon. People decide the speed.

### Not only homes

Homes are not the only buildings a comfortable life needs. Schools and hospitals need materials too. The research gives their embodied energy per square metre:

| Building | Embodied energy per m² |
| --- | --- |
| Homes | 2–4 GJ |
| Schools | 4.5–7.5 GJ |
| Hospitals | 14–23 GJ |

For homes, **2–4 GJ** per square metre adds up to about **6.5 EJ** a year over 30 years. That is affordable in energy terms, under 2% of the 386 EJ a year of Chapter 10. In materials, it is heavy. That is why the choice of materials matters as much as the speed.

### Using what already stands

Stock-first applies to buildings too. About **40%** of the housing need could be met by upgrading or extending existing homes, using about **40%** of the materials of new building. Renovating an existing building emits **50–75%** less than building new.

Why not simply fill empty homes? Because they are in the wrong places. The large stocks of empty homes are far from where homes are missing.

| Empty homes | Number | Share of homes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| China, urban (2017) | about 65 million | 21.4% |
| European Union (2021) | 46.6 million | 19.7%, including second homes |
| Japan (2023) | 9.0 million | 13.8% |
| India, urban (2011) | 11.1 million | about 12% |

Homes cannot be moved between regions. India's empty urban homes would meet only about **0.5%** of the world's need. Most of the need is missing space, not empty or broken homes. Most of it must still be built.

This leads to one of the research's sharpest tests. If material use cannot fall by at least **2 times** while homes and new energy systems are built, UNIRA's physical claims fail.

## Land: how much cropland per person?

Now turn from what we build to what we eat.

In 2021, the world farmed about **1,580 million hectares** of cropland. That is about **0.2 hectares**, or **2,000 square metres**, per person. Cropland per person has already fallen by **18%** since 2000, as population grew.

Cropland is only part of the picture. All agricultural land, including pasture, covered about **4,788 million hectares**. Meadows and pastures alone took about **3,208 million**. Most farmland, in other words, is meadow and pasture, not crops.

How much can the world farm without destroying forests and wild land? The research uses three limits for 2050:

| Cropland per person in 2050 | m² | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | 1,346 | EAT-Lancet 2019 cropland limit |
| Central | **1,636** | no expansion of cropland |
| High | 1,816 | EAT-Lancet 2025 scenario |
| Today | about 2,000 | FAO |

### A worked example: the central limit

The central value is simple arithmetic. Keep today's cropland exactly as it is, about 1,580 million hectares, and share it among 9.66 billion people in 2050. Each person then has about **1,636 square metres**. That is a plot of about 40 metres by 40 metres.

The low value comes from the 2019 EAT-Lancet limit of about **13 million square kilometres** of cropland for the world. The high value comes from the 2025 EAT-Lancet scenario, in which cropland grows by about 11%.

Even the high limit is below today's level per person. With more people and no new land, each person's share of cropland must shrink. The food system has to do more with less.

## The planetary-health diet

In 2025, the EAT-Lancet Commission, a group of scientists, published an updated plan for feeding the world within Earth's limits. Its main message is hopeful: a **healthy diet for 9.6 billion people** is possible.

It sets clear limits:
- no conversion of intact land, with all farmland kept below **48 million square kilometres**;
- nitrogen surplus below **57 million tonnes** a year;
- phosphorus loss of about **4.6 million tonnes** a year;
- about **2,000 cubic kilometres** a year of water drawn from rivers, lakes and aquifers;
- food-system greenhouse gases of **5 billion tonnes** CO₂-equivalent by 2050.

And it names three conditions:
- diets must change, with about **33% less** beef, lamb and other meat from ruminant animals;
- food loss and waste must be **halved**;
- farm productivity must rise by **7–10%** above business as usual.

### A closer look: how certain is the land result?

Under the Commission's plan, total farmland falls by about **7%**, or roughly **340 million hectares**. Cropland rises by about **11%**. Less land goes to grazing, and somewhat more to crops.

Those central numbers hide wide ranges. Across the models the Commission used, the change in cropland runs from **−3% to +42%**. The change in total farmland runs from **−26% to +1%**. One model even shows cropland falling by **22%**.

So the direction is clear: less grazing land, roughly stable cropland. The size of the change is uncertain. That is why UNIRA's research uses a low, central and high limit, not one number.

### Calories: comfortable, not minimal

The Commission's reference diet provides about **2,400 kilocalories** a day for an adult. That is down from **2,500** in its 2019 report.

UNIRA's comfortable diet, introduced in Chapter 1, is **2,470 kilocalories** eaten per person per day: varied, plentiful and enjoyed. A decent diet, by comparison, is about **2,000–2,150**. For protein, a safe level is about **0.83 grams** per kilogram of body weight a day, or about **50–55 grams** for an adult. The world already supplies about **92 grams** per person.

Can that comfortable diet fit for 9.7 billion people? The research gives a clear answer: **only with a planetary-health diet and halved waste**. Halving waste is necessary, but it is not enough on its own. What people eat matters most for land and nitrogen.

## Water: the quiet limit

Water rarely makes headlines in debates about a good life. It should.

The research sets out how much water a home needs:

| Domestic water per person per day | Litres |
| --- | --- |
| Basic floor (WHO) | 20 |
| Decent living | 50 |
| Comfortable (derived) | about 100 |
| Observed where water is piped into the home | 155 |

Home use is only one part of the story. The EAT-Lancet limit of about **2,000 cubic kilometres** a year covers the whole food system. O'Neill's fair share, **574 cubic metres** per person a year, covers all water drawn from rivers, lakes and aquifers.

The research has not yet tested water limits region by region. Water needs also rise in hot climates and for hard physical work. This is a gap.

## Waste: the easiest saving

Food is lost at two stages.

Between the harvest and the shop, **13.3%** of food is lost: in storage, transport and processing. That share has not improved. It was about **13.0%** in 2015.

In shops, restaurants and homes, another **19%** is wasted. That is about **1.05 billion tonnes** a year, or **132 kilograms** per person. Most of it is thrown away at home: about **79 kilograms** per person, or **631 million tonnes** in all. Restaurants and canteens waste about **36** kilograms per person, and shops about **17**.

| Where food is lost or wasted | Share or amount |
| --- | --- |
| Harvest to shop (lost) | **13.3%** |
| Shops, restaurants, homes (wasted) | **19%**, 1.05 billion tonnes |
| — of which homes | 79 kg per person |
| — of which restaurants and canteens | 36 kg per person |
| — of which shops | 17 kg per person |

Together, food lost and wasted amounts to about **775 kilocalories** per person per day. Growing it takes about **20%** of the world's farmland.

### A worked example: a week's bin

Spread over a year, 79 kilograms of household waste per person is about **1.5 kilograms** a week. For a family of four, that is about six kilograms every week: food that was grown, bought, carried home and thrown away.

### A closer look: two numbers, two stages

The 13.3% and the 19% are measured at different points, by different agencies. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization counts losses from farm to shop. The UN Environment Programme counts waste in shops, food service and homes.

This matters for the calculations in Chapter 1. The food supply figures used there are measured *after* most losses on the way to the shop. So only the 19% consumer waste is applied to them. Adding both would count the same loss twice.

### What halving waste does

Chapter 1 showed what halving consumer waste would do for today's world:

| Comfortable diet (2,470 kcal eaten) for everyone today | World supply ÷ need |
| --- | --- |
| At today's waste | **0.99** |
| With consumer waste halved | **1.11** |

At today's waste, the world is 1% short. With half the waste, it has 11% to spare. Moving about **7%** of world food supply between regions then reaches everyone.

In UNIRA's 30-year plan, food is redistributed from the first year, and waste is halved over ten years. Everyone's diet is covered from year 1. Comfortable diets follow as waste falls.

Will people waste less simply because they feel secure? Chapter 9 looked at this. The evidence is weak. Household food waste differs by only about **7 kilograms** per person per year between richer and poorer countries. In the model, a security-driven fall in waste lifts the comfortable-diet ratio only from **1.105** to **1.11–1.12**. Halving waste has to be a deliberate goal, not a side effect.

Chapter 9 also found that ultra-processed food, freely available, led people to eat about **508 more kilocalories** a day. What is produced shapes what is eaten. Article 11's rule against producing "only to sustain sales" applies to food too.

## Food in the working week

How much of the economy does food take? Less than most people think.

In France, the farms, fishing boats and food factories that feed households account for about **3.6%** of all work hours. Food manufacturing alone takes about **1.3 billion hours**. Restaurants and accommodation take another **2.4 billion hours** a year, out of **45 billion** in total.

So cutting food waste will not, by itself, shorten the French working week much. The research has not yet measured how many hours less waste would save. Its value lies elsewhere: in land, water, nitrogen and emissions, and in food that reaches people who need it.

## Honest answers to hard questions

**"Will UNIRA tell me what to eat?"**
The draft constitution leaves daily life, culture and traditions to each region (Article 4). It does not set a menu. But the evidence is plain: a comfortable diet for everyone fits only if diets shift towards the planetary-health pattern. That plan asks for about a third less ruminant meat, not none. How regions bring that about, and whether people agree to it, is one of the largest open questions in this book.

**"Does halving waste mean I get less food?"**
No. The comfortable diet of **2,470 kilocalories** is food actually eaten. Waste is food that was bought and never eaten. Halving consumer waste is what lifts the world's supply from **0.99** to **1.11** times a comfortable diet for everyone. Less waste means more food reaching plates, not less on yours. What changes is how carefully food is bought, stored and used, and the mix on the plate: more plants, less ruminant meat.

**"Why not just farm more land?"**
Because the land would come from forests and wild places. The EAT-Lancet plan forbids converting intact land. Cropland per person has already fallen by 18% since 2000. More people and no new land means each person's share must shrink further.

**"Can the world build 109 billion square metres and still cut materials 2.7 times?"**
Nobody knows. The research names this as a test that could prove UNIRA wrong. Low-carbon frames, upgrading existing homes and making goods last are the main tools. Whether they are enough has not been shown.

**"If repair saves so few French hours, why bother?"**
Because the savings are real, just not in France. Most durable goods are made elsewhere. A longer life for a phone or a washing machine means less mining, smelting and shipping in other regions. For one world economy, that counts.

**"Won't better technology solve the materials problem on its own?"**
It may help. O'Neill's finding that wider goals need **2 to 6 times** the sustainable level of resources is measured *at today's efficiency*. Better efficiency could shrink that gap. But no evidence in this research shows it closing the gap alone. Fanning's study shows overshoot growing, not shrinking, under current trends.

## What the evidence says

- The world extracts about **14.2 tonnes** of materials per person a year, about **39 kg** a day. A sustainable level is **3–8 tonnes**, central **5.2**. That is a **2.7 times** cut, the hardest limit after carbon.
- No country today meets its people's basic needs at a globally sustainable level of resource use.
- Stock-first saves little French work time, because most durable goods are imported. Imported durables embody about **1.27 billion hours**-equivalent, three times the domestic figure. The gains are mostly abroad, which matters for a world federation.
- Low-carbon building frames cut embodied carbon from about **400** to **150 kg CO₂e** per m². Over the whole housing programme, that is **43 Gt** against **16 Gt**. The frame, floors and roof matter far more than the walls.
- Cropland per person must fall from about **2,000 m²** today to **1,346–1,816 m²** by 2050.
- A comfortable diet of **2,470 kcal** for 9.7 billion people fits only with a planetary-health diet and halved waste. Today **19%** of food is wasted by consumers and **13.3%** is lost before it reaches shops.
- With consumer waste halved, today's world food supply covers a comfortable diet for everyone **1.11 times**.
- Food chains account for about **3.6%** of French work hours.

## What we still don't know

- Whether a 2.7 times cut in materials is possible while about 109 billion m² of homes and much new energy infrastructure are built. If material use cannot fall by at least 2 times, UNIRA's physical claims fail. The research names this as a falsification test.
- How much labour, material and emissions longer-lasting goods would save abroad. The research measures imported hours-equivalent, not foreign hours, materials or emissions.
- The repair work needed per hour saved (0.25) is a synthetic assumption, not a measured value.
- Whether people will change diets as much as the planetary-health plan needs. Diet is the binding lever for land, and it depends on culture and choice.
- How land, nitrogen and water limits fall region by region. The research uses world averages per person.
- The EAT-Lancet land results vary widely between models: cropland changes from −3% to +42%.
- Some sustainable-materials limits were read only as cited by other authors, not in their original sources.
- No verified whole-house carbon assessment was found for concrete homes in India or sub-Saharan Africa. The low-carbon central value of 150 kg is a judgement.
- The food-waste figures are careful estimates, but they are uncertain. How many work hours less waste would save has not been measured.
