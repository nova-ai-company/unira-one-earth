# Chapter 5 — One Earth, many regions

*Second draft, 3 November 2026, updated with V7.8–V7.9. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.3–V7.9) and the draft Constitution of One Earth, and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine a morning market in a town anywhere on Earth: in Morocco, in Peru, in Indonesia. The call to prayer, or the church bells, or the temple drums sound at the hour they have always sounded. Children go to school in their own language. The town's festival takes place on the same day as for centuries.

And yet something has changed. The new clinic at the edge of town was built with resources allocated by need. Every unit is published in a register anyone can read. The town's young builders were trained through a programme paid for by the One Earth Fund. Nobody in the capital of a distant, powerful country decided any of it.

The town council still argues about the market's opening hours. The regional parliament still debates the school timetable. Those quarrels belong to the people who live there, and nobody outside may settle them.

This town is imagined, a composite of many places. But each part of the picture comes from the draft Constitution of One Earth. This is what UNIRA proposes: **one Earth, many regions.** This chapter explains how it is meant to work without any region ruling another. It also shows where the design is still untested.

## What a region is

The first article of the draft constitution is short. The Earth is one federation, called One Earth. Its members are **regions**.

What is a region? The constitution answers: "today's countries, or parts or unions of them, as their people decide." So a region could be France or India as they are today. It could be part of a large country, if its people choose. Or it could be several small countries that decide to join together.

The research worked with today's countries as regions. Its voting tests used **217** regions, covering the world's **8.12 billion** people in 2024.

Each region has its own democratic government and parliament, elected by its people. One Earth cannot dissolve a regional government. The only exception is a ruling of the Court of One Earth, for a grave breach of rights.

## What stays local, and what is shared

The constitution follows a principle called *subsidiarity*. In plain words: decisions are taken as close to the people as possible.

Each region governs itself according to its own religion, culture, language and traditions. The constitution names four areas that belong to the region:
- **education**;
- **local planning**;
- **public holidays**;
- **daily life**.

Each region also controls **its own borders**: who may enter, stay, work and settle, under its own visa rules.

One Earth decides only what must be decided together. The constitution lists six things:
- shared resources;
- allocation between regions;
- the currency;
- rights;
- the environment;
- peace.

That list is deliberately short. Everything not on it stays with the regions.

### Culture and belief

A region may give a religion, culture or language an official or traditional place in public life. A region with a state church may keep it. A region with a national language may keep it.

But every *person* keeps freedom of thought, conscience and belief. The constitution spells this out:
- no one may be forced into a religion or belief;
- no one may be punished for their religion or belief;
- no one may be punished for changing it, or for having none.

Minorities in every region are protected in their language, culture and worship.

So the balance is this. The region shapes public life. The person keeps their own conscience. Neither may erase the other.

### An imagined case

Picture a family who speak a minority language in their region. Their children attend a school run by the region, in the region's main language. The family also want their children to learn their own language and keep their own festivals.

Under the constitution, the region still sets the school system. That is part of "education", which stays local. But the family's language and worship are protected. If they were punished for them, they could take their case to the Court of One Earth. Every person may bring a case there.

This example is imagined. It shows how the two levels are meant to fit together. It does not show how a real court would rule.

## Borders and movement

Each region controls its own borders. That surprises some readers. If the world is one humanity, why keep borders at all?

The author's answer is about choice and culture. A region decides who settles there, as today. But the constitution also guarantees three things to every person:
- they may leave any region, including their own;
- they may always return to their own region;
- they may move freely within their own region.

Entry to *another* region follows that region's visa rules. These rules must apply equally, without discrimination by origin, religion or ethnicity.

### Protection in emergencies

A person whose life is threatened by persecution or disaster is protected. The constitution sets an order:
1. **First, in their own region**, through rebuilding and needs-first allocation by One Earth.
2. **Where that is impossible**, other regions provide temporary shelter. The burden is shared among regions by their means and population, through the One Earth Fund.
3. **People return home** when it is safe again.

### Would people still need to flee?

The hope behind these rules is simple. In a world without war, persecution or poverty, people should rarely need to leave home. The research tested that hope against the evidence. It found it largely right for refugees, wrong for disasters, and overstated for economic migration.

**Refugees.** At the end of 2025, **117.8 million** people were forcibly displaced. That is about one person in 70 worldwide. It was down from **123.2 million** a year earlier, the first fall in a decade.

| Forcibly displaced, end of 2025 | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Refugees | **41.6 million** |
| Asylum-seekers | **9 million** |
| People displaced inside their own country by conflict | **68.7 million** |
| Total | **117.8 million** |

UNHCR, which publishes these counts, defines "forcibly displaced" by its causes. They are persecution, conflict, violence, human rights violations and serious public disorder. Those are exactly the causes UNIRA says it removes.

When wars end, many people go home. In 2025, **14.7 million** people returned. Chapter 12 tells that story in more detail.

**Disasters.** Here peace does not help. In 2024, disasters forced people from their homes **45.8 million** times. That was the highest number since records began in 2008. The 15-year average is about **24 million** a year.

About **99%** of those displacements were caused by weather. Storms caused **25.2 million** and floods **19.1 million**. Many were short evacuations before a storm arrived. Almost all happened inside the person's own country.

In 2024 disasters displaced **more than twice** as many people as conflict did: 45.8 million against 20.1 million. A just world does not stop the weather. That is why protection starts in the person's own region, with good homes and quick rebuilding.

The future may bring more. The World Bank's Groundswell report projects up to **216 million** people moving within their own countries because of climate by 2050. Strong climate and development action could cut that by up to 80%. That would still leave about **43 million**.

**Economic migration.** The United Nations counted about **281 million** international migrants in 2020. That is less than 4% of the world's population. Refugees and asylum-seekers were **12%** of them. So roughly 88% of migrants were not forced to move.

Most of them moved for a better life. The same worker earns about **2.7 times** more in the United States than at home, in the median country. For a worker from the Philippines the gap is about **3.5 times**. For Haiti it is more than **7 times**.

In UNIRA, pay is on one scale everywhere. That income gap is the main motive, and it would largely disappear. But movement would not. Even rich countries have a few per cent of their people living abroad, for family, study, work or simple preference. The research judges that about **2–4%** of people living outside their home region is plausible. That figure is an assumption, not a measurement.

### A closer look: the "migration hump"

One finding deserves a warning. As poor countries grow richer, emigration does not fall at first. It **rises**.

Research by Clemens found that emigration rises until a country reaches about **PPP$10,000** of output per person. ("PPP" means the figures are adjusted for local prices.) Before that point, doubling output per person goes with about **35%** more emigration overall, and **74%** more to rich countries.

| Output per person | Share of people living abroad |
| --- | --- |
| About PPP$1,000 | about **4%** |
| About PPP$10,000 | about **11%** |

So while poorer regions catch up under UNIRA, pressure to move may grow for a while before it falls. Regional visa rules would face that pressure in exactly the years when catching up is fastest.

### A closer look: how these numbers were checked

Every migration figure above was read in source text that the research fetched. Two official websites, those of UNHCR and of IDMC, which counts disaster displacement, blocked automated access. For those, the research used exact copies of the official reports held elsewhere. The links are listed in the research files.

Figures that rest on judgement are marked as assumptions. Two are used here: the 2–4% of people living abroad, and the return rates after peace. A third figure, **29.9 million** disaster displacements in 2025, was seen only in a search summary. It is not used as a fact.

### An honest tension

The research flags one problem with regional border control. It works only if visas cannot be used to shut out people fleeing disasters, or seeking protection.

If a region could refuse everyone, its "control" would collide with the people the constitution promises to protect. The research suggested a different default: free or easy movement between regions, with duties to admit people in emergencies.

The draft constitution chose regional control, plus shared emergency shelter. Whether that balance holds in a real crisis is not yet known.

## Fairness first, decided by data and science

The preamble of the draft constitution names its core in one sentence: "**Fairness is the core of this Constitution.**"

Article 3 turns that into four principles for all public action:
- **Evidence:** decisions use data and science, published and open to challenge;
- **Equity:** resources go first to unmet needs;
- **Justice:** rights are equal and enforceable;
- **Sharing:** what the Earth provides belongs to all, and its use is accounted for openly.

### Data and science in every decision

Article 3a makes the first principle concrete. Every decision on production, allocation, prices, salaries, the money cap, the environment and public services must rest on measured data and the best available science.

The data, the methods and the reasons are published, with their uncertainty. Anyone can check them.

An independent **Scientific Council** reviews the methods. It is drawn from all regions and all relevant sciences. Its opinions are public. A decision that departs from its advice must say why.

And rules can be wrong. When new evidence shows a rule is wrong, it is reviewed. Some rules carry their own tests of success and failure. Chapter 14 describes the most important one: a built-in alarm on the allocation system.

### We produce what we need, for all regions

The same article sets the rule for production:
- production is planned from measured needs and population in every region, plus studied reserves for catastrophes;
- no more is produced than is needed;
- no region is left without what it needs.

This is the opposite of producing to sell. Nothing is made only to keep sales going.

## How resources are shared

Here is the heart of the economic design. Each year, new capacity goes to regions **by measured needs and number of people, needs first**.

What counts as new capacity? The constitution lists five things:
- energy systems;
- homes;
- health workers trained in their own region;
- food;
- materials.

Three separate bodies handle this, and none may do another's job.

| Body | Its job | Its limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Statistical Office** | Measures needs in every region at least once a year, with stated uncertainty | Independent of all other bodies; uniform, public methods |
| **Allocation Authority** | Applies a public formula to those needs | No power to favour any region |
| **Audit Authority** | Checks every region every year | Budget fixed by the constitution |

Changing the formula is deliberately hard. It needs two-thirds of the Assembly, a double majority of regions, a published opinion of the Scientific Council and review by a citizens' assembly drawn by lot. Even then, it takes effect only after the next Assembly election (Article 26a).

**Resources are not allocated by politicians.** That is the point of the design. A minister cannot send an extra power station to a friendly region. The formula decides, and the register shows what it decided.

### What "needs first" means in practice

Take energy. The comfortable level is **40 gigajoules** of final energy per person per year (Chapter 10). A region far below it receives new solar and wind first. A region above it receives none, and slowly reduces its use.

Surplus beyond a region's needs goes to the One Earth Fund for sharing.

### The formula, written down

An earlier draft of this book had to admit a gap: the constitution set the principle, but not the formula. The formula is now written, in **Article 10a**. It has four steps.
1. For each need (energy, health care, homes, food, education), a region's **gap** is the published target per person minus its measured level, times its population.
2. Targets take account of real differences in need, such as age, climate, disability and remoteness. Each adjustment is published with its evidence and approved by the Scientific Council.
3. **Health need** is weighted by the age of the population and by its burden of disease, in equal parts. Age alone is never used. Chapter 8 explains why.
4. **Decent for everyone first.** New capacity first closes every region's gap to the *decent* level. Only then does it go to the *comfortable* level. Within each step, the largest gaps are served first, in proportion to need.

The decent levels are **15.3 GJ** of energy, **44.5** health workers per 10,000 people and **15 m²** of living space per person. The comfortable levels are 40 GJ, 131 health workers and 27 m².

Why two steps? Because the research tested both versions in its 30-year world model, with the same resources. In the first version, all new capacity went towards the comfortable level from the start.

| Same resources, 30-year world model | One step | **Decent first, then comfortable** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year every region has decent energy | 8 | **4** |
| Year every region has decent health care | 12 | **7** |
| Year every region has decent housing | 20 | **9** |
| Person-years lived below decent energy | 12.7 billion | **8.4 billion** (−33%) |
| Person-years lived below decent housing | 37.1 billion | **29.6 billion** (−20%) |
| Comfortable gap closed by year 30 (energy / health / housing) | 100 / 100 / 50% | **100 / 100 / 50%** |

Serving the decent level first roughly halves the wait until every region has decent energy, health care and housing. And it costs nothing on the comfortable goal. That is why the research recommended it, and why it is now the constitutional rule.

The formula, its data and each year's results are published in the Public Allocation Register. Any region may challenge them before the Court.

### Where to produce

Article 10a also says where things are made: where it takes the fewest working hours, resources and transport, while giving every region productive work.

The research tried this on food. Every region keeps a decent diet. The shortfalls of **39** regions are covered from the surpluses of **140**. Only about **1.4%** of the world's food supply needs to move at all.

The question is how. If each surplus region simply sent food to every deficit region in proportion, the average calorie would travel about **8,420 km**. A planned route, matching nearest suppliers to nearest needs, cuts that to about **2,573 km**: **69% less transport**.

There is a limit, and the research names it. The food data describe supply, not production, so some "surplus" regions are in fact importers. A real plan needs crop-by-crop production data and real transport networks.

## A machine that advises, and people who decide

How does One Earth know what each region will need next year? The author's rule is that an artificial-intelligence model, a machine-learning model, helps by default. The research tested that rule on real history before writing it into law.

It asked the models to forecast needs from 2010 onward, using only data from before. Here is what it found.
- **Needs are very predictable.** The number of people in a region was forecast one year ahead to within about **0.1%**, and five years ahead to within about **1%**. Food supply per person was within about **1%** one year ahead.
- **Machine learning helps, a little.** It beat simple rules, such as "same as last year", for population, food and health workers. It did not beat them for short-term energy use, which today moves with prices, crises and weather.
- **Information is not the bottleneck.** In the 30-year world model, errors in the data, whether from statistics or from forecasts, cost at most about **1%** of progress. Misreporting and theft cost about **12–19%**.
- **The machine's real value is honesty.** If checks by the model helped cut theft from 8% to 4%, the share of the energy gap closed by year 15 would rise from **51.9%** to **55.6%**. That is far more than better forecasts could give. Whether the checks can really do this is an assumption, still to be tested.
- **Fairness comes first.** Forecasts of health workers were **3–6 times** less accurate in low-income regions, because their statistics are thin. For nurses, they were also biased about 6% too low. A planner who trusted them would under-supply the poorest regions.

So **Article 3b** sets conditions. The AI advises; people decide.
- A model is used only if it predicts better than simple published rules, on data it has not seen. It is re-tested every year, in public.
- Code, data and accuracy are open. No secret model may decide anything.
- Every model is tested for each region and income group. A model that is worse for a region may not be used there until the gap is fixed. Better statistics in the poorest regions come first.
- A responsible council decides, and must say why if it departs from the proposal. Anyone may go to the Court.
- No model may decide on a person's rights, on sanctions or on the use of force. A manual procedure always exists.
- Reserves are sized from the measured forecast errors.

**Article 3c** adds locks against tampering. Every model runs only in a published, signed version, rebuilt each year by the Audit Authority. Each forecast used for allocation must come from at least two models built by independent teams. No model may score people, rank their loyalty or pick them for audit or policing, and none may use personal payment data.

## A tension to watch: one planner, many regions

There is a second tension in the design, and the research names it openly.

One of UNIRA's design rules says that One Earth decides which region produces what. Everything is then sent to regions by population and need. The research recorded a question beside that rule: is it compatible with local autonomy, and with "no forced relocation"?

Two answers come from the research itself.

First, **people are never moved to production. Production goes to where people are.** Labour and production are pooled across regions by placing work where people are available.

Second, the research admits the "knowledge problem". Deciding production for about **8 billion** people and millions of products is very hard. Modern planning tools and computers make parts of it manageable. But preferences, quality and local conditions still need regional information and regional decisions.

So the balance is not settled. One Earth plans the large flows: energy systems, homes, health workers. Regions know what their people want. How these two levels share information, without one swallowing the other, is a design question still open.

## No region rules another

How can 217 regions of very different sizes govern together? Neither the giants nor a crowd of small regions should dominate. The draft constitution answers with **two chambers**.

### The Assembly of the Peoples

The Assembly has **1,000** members. They are elected every five years by direct, secret, universal vote.

Seats depend on population, but not in direct proportion. They follow the **square root** of each region's population. This rule was proposed by the mathematician Lionel Penrose. The constitution adopts it because it aims to give every citizen equal voting power in a union of regions.

What does a square root do? It shrinks big differences. A region with 100 times the people of another gets 10 times the seats, not 100 times.

Two limits complete the rule:
- every region has at least **1** seat;
- no region may hold more than **5%** of all seats.

The research tested the rule on 2024 populations.

| Region | Share of seats if seats followed population | Share under the square-root rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| India | **16.5%** | **4.4%** |
| China | **16.0%** | **4.4%** |
| United States | 3.9% | 2.1% |
| Indonesia | 3.2% | 2.0% |
| Pakistan | 2.9% | 1.8% |
| India and China together | **32.5%** | **8.8%** |

Under the square-root rule, no region comes near the 5% ceiling. The ceiling is a safety rail, not a working limit.

### The Council of Regions

The second chamber is simpler. Each region sends one representative with **one equal vote**. Large or small, rich or poor, every region counts the same.

### How a law passes

A law needs both chambers.
1. A **majority in the Assembly**.
2. A **double majority in the Council**:
    - at least **55% of regions**;
    - representing at least **65% of the world's population**, counted by the square root of each region's population.

The thresholds of 55% and 65% follow the Council of the European Union. The author agreed them on 3 November 2026.

There are no permanent seats, no vetoes, and no privileges of size, wealth or history.

## A vote, step by step

Rules like these are easier to follow with an example. The vote below is imagined. The thresholds and the tested numbers come from the research.

Suppose an ordinary law is proposed. (Changing the allocation formula needs more, as described above.)

**Step 1: the Assembly.** The 1,000 members debate and vote. A majority must vote yes.

**Step 2: count the regions.** In the Council, 55% of 217 regions is 119.35. Votes come in whole regions, so at least **120** must vote yes.

**Step 3: weigh the regions.** Each region carries a weight based on the square root of its population. The yes regions together must carry at least **65%** of the total weight.

Now imagine two outcomes.

**Outcome A.** India and China both vote no. Together they carry about **8.8%** of the square-root weight. That leaves over 91% of the weight with the other 215 regions. If enough of them agree, the law can still pass. Two giants cannot stop the world.

**Outcome B.** A large number of small regions vote yes, say 125 of them. They pass Step 2. But small regions carry small weights. If together they hold less than 65% of the weight, the law fails at Step 3. A crowd of small regions cannot impose its will either.

### Why the square root matters here too

The research found a trap in the first version of the rule. It counted population *raw*, person by person.

Under raw counting, a law needs 65% of humanity behind it. So any group holding more than 35% can block it. India and China together hold **35.2%** of the world's people. Under raw counting, those two regions alone could block any world law. That is a hidden veto.

Under square-root counting, the picture changes.

| What it takes | Raw population | Square-root weighting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fewest regions that can block a law | **2** | **21** |
| Fewest regions that can pass a law | 120 | **120** |
| Fewest regions that could meet the population test on their own | 16 | **63** |
| Regions needed to block by count alone | 98 | 98 |

So under the agreed rule, blocking a law takes at least **21 regions**. Passing one takes at least **120 of 217**. Blocking by headcount alone takes **98 regions**, more than 45% of them. No small group can impose its will, and no small group can stop the world.

### A closer look: how the voting rules were tested

The voting tests were calculations on real population figures for 2024, for all 217 regions and **8,117,484,624** people. The research also tried other thresholds with raw population. Lowering the population test to 60% only raised the smallest blocking group from two regions to four. Raising the regions test to 60% made laws harder to pass, needing 131 regions. Neither removed the hidden veto. The square-root count did. It also spreads power out: counted raw, just **16** regions could meet the 65% population test on their own; counted by square root, it takes **63**.

One limit applies to all of this. The tests use population numbers. They do not model how real regions would vote, form alliances or trade favours.

## Changing the constitution

Ordinary laws need the double majority. Changing the constitution needs more:
- **two-thirds** of the Assembly;
- a double majority of **65% of regions** representing **65% of the population** in the Council;
- and a **world referendum**.

In numbers, two-thirds of 1,000 is at least 667 members. And 65% of 217 regions is at least 142.

Some things can never be changed. The guaranteed rights of Article 6 cannot be reduced by any amendment. Neither can Article 9, which says no law, decision, sanction or emergency may reduce those rights.

## Power divided, power rotated

Power is split into **nine** parts, each held by a separate body (Articles 22 and 22a):
- making laws: the Assembly of the Peoples and the Council of Regions;
- carrying them out: the Coordination Council;
- allocating resources: the Allocation Authority, which runs the Allocation Authority's formula;
- money: the Monetary Authority (Chapter 6);
- armed force: the Peace Force;
- measuring needs: the Statistical Office;
- auditing: the Audit Authority;
- science: the Scientific Council;
- judging: the Court.

No person may sit in two of these bodies, or move from one to another within three years. They are chosen in different ways, some by election, some by appointment, some by lot. And no body chooses the people who audit or judge it. Above all, **no body holds arms, money and allocation together.**

The executive, the **Coordination Council**, has seven to fifteen members from different regions. The Assembly elects them for a single five-year term. The chair rotates every year, and no region may hold it twice in a row.

The **Court of One Earth** protects rights, settles disputes between regions, and reviews laws and allocations against the constitution. It has **21** judges, who serve one 12-year term and come from all continents. A third of them are replaced every four years, so no single majority appoints the whole Court. Every person may bring a case.

No one may hold One Earth office for more than **10 years** in total. Every office-holder declares their assets publicly each year. They are under the same money cap as everyone else.

Chapter 14 explains why each of these limits exists, and how the research attacked its own constitution to find the gaps.

## One Earth owns the arms

War is abolished. No region may keep armed forces or weapons of war, or make them. All arms belong to One Earth, in a single **Peace Force**.

The Force enforces the Court's rulings, protects people whose rights are under attack, and helps in disasters. Whoever controls the only armed force could dominate everyone. So it can be sent only with **both** a Court ruling **and** the double majority of regions. It is never used against peaceful protest, elections or a region's democratic institutions.

Regions keep civilian police for everyday crime. Chapter 12 explains the size of the Force, and Chapter 14 its controls.

## Does sharing by need actually work?

UNIRA's central economic claim is that directing new resources by need closes the world's gaps faster than a market world. Chapter 13 tests this in full. Chapter 14 tests what happens when the allocator makes mistakes, or when resources are stolen.

The short version fits in one table.

| Share of the shortfall closed | Energy, year 15 | Health workers, year 15 | Homes, year 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reformed market world | **−14%** (the gap grows) | −4% | −21% |
| Very cooperative market world | 14% | 4% | 10% |
| UNIRA, typical failures, no safeguards | 36% | 24% | 19% |
| UNIRA, strong safeguards | **52%** | **34%** | **40%** |
| UNIRA, perfect allocation | 60% | 38% | 50% |

Even with typical failures and no safeguards, sharing by need beats the most cooperative market world. Strong safeguards keep most of its advantage. The decisive danger is **leakage**: resources that never reach the people they were meant for.

## Joining by choice

Nobody is forced in. A region joins by referendum, in three stages: observer, associate, then full member. A region may also leave by referendum, with a negotiated settlement.

Would regions want to join? The research compared each region's material situation after 30 years, inside UNIRA or in a reformed market world. Compared with that reformed market world, **146 regions, home to 86% of humanity, gain.** In the 33 richest, *average* energy use per person is **6–12%** lower. That is a regional average, borne by the heaviest users: nobody becomes poor, and 44% of their people are better off even at the lowest income. (This 86% is a share of people. It is not the same as the 86% of today's consumption that a base worker can buy, in Chapter 6.)

A union of only the regions that gain would still work, at about half speed. Chapter 15 sets out who gains, what history teaches, and the rules for getting there.

## Questions a fair reader might ask

**"Isn't a world government a tyranny waiting to happen?"**
It could be, which is why the draft divides power nine ways. Terms are single and short. Officials are under the money cap. The only armed force needs both a court and the regions to move. Regional governments cannot be dissolved except by the Court for a grave breach of rights. None of this proves the design is safe. It shows that the danger was designed against from the start.

**"Why should India accept 4.4% of the seats, when direct proportion would give it 16.5%?"**
This is a real cost for India's people, and the book should not hide it. Under the square-root rule, a very large region gets far fewer seats per person than a small one. The constitution's reason is that the rule aims to give every citizen equal voting power in a union of regions. India still holds more seats than any other region, and an equal vote in the Council. Whether its people would accept the trade is a political question.

**"Can't the small regions gang up on the big ones?"**
The double majority stops that. Small regions can reach 120 votes in the Council. But they must also carry 65% of the square-root weight, and small regions carry small weights.

**"What if my region keeps losing votes?"**
Some protections do not depend on any vote. No law, allocation decision, sanction or emergency may reduce the guaranteed rights of Article 6. And a region may leave by referendum.

**"Won't everything become the same everywhere?"**
The design says no. Religion, language, education, local planning, holidays and daily life stay with the regions. One Earth decides only six shared things.

**"Can one world institution really know what 8 billion people need?"**
Not perfectly. The research calls this the knowledge problem and lists it among the tests that could prove UNIRA wrong. The answer it proposes has three parts: measure needs often, publish everything, and leave daily choices to the regions. For the big needs, the forecasts are good: population within about 0.1% a year ahead. But that covers energy, food, health workers and homes, not millions of products. Whether that is enough has not been shown.

**"Won't an AI end up running the world?"**
Not under Article 3b. The model proposes and people decide, in public, and must give reasons. No model may touch a person's rights, sanctions or the use of force. And the research found that the machine matters less for forecasting than for catching theft.

## What the evidence says

- Regions keep religion, culture, language, education, local planning, holidays, daily life and their own borders. One Earth decides only six shared matters.
- About **117.8 million** people were forcibly displaced at the end of 2025, all for causes UNIRA aims to remove. Disasters displaced **45.8 million** in 2024, **99%** of them weather-related, and peace does not remove those.
- Economic migration falls as incomes converge, but it does not vanish. While poorer regions catch up, emigration may first **rise**.
- Under square-root weighting, India and China hold about **4.4%** of Assembly seats each, against **16–17%** under direct proportion.
- With a 55%/65% double majority counted by square root, blocking a law takes at least **21 regions** and passing one at least **120 of 217**. Counted raw, **2** regions could block.
- Needs-first allocation closes the world's gaps where a market world, with the same resources, lets them grow. Strong safeguards keep most of that advantage.
- Serving the decent level first brings every region to decent energy in **4** years instead of 8, decent health care in **7** instead of 12, and decent housing in **9** instead of 20, with no loss on the comfortable goal (Article 10a).
- Planning where food comes from cuts transport by **69%**; only about **1.4%** of world food supply needs to move.
- Needs are highly predictable: population within about **0.1%** a year ahead. Information errors cost at most about **1%** of progress; misreporting and theft cost **12–19%**. The AI's main value is checking honesty, and it advises but never decides (Article 3b).

## What we still don't know

- No world federation has ever existed. The voting rules were tested on population numbers, not on real political behaviour.
- The formula's targets for climate, disability and remoteness are not yet set from evidence. The food plan uses supply data, not production data, and straight-line distances, not real transport routes.
- Whether AI checks would really cut theft is an assumption. Forecasts are weakest in the poorest regions, so their statistics must improve first.
- How One Earth's planning of large flows and the regions' own knowledge would share information is an open design question.
- Whether regional border control and the promise to protect people in emergencies can both hold in a real crisis.
- The 2–4% share of people living abroad, and the return rates after peace, are assumptions.
- Whether rich regions would choose peace, security and time over 6–12% of their energy use is a political question no model can answer.
