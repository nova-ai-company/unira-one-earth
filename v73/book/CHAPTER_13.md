# Chapter 13 — Better than the best market world

*Second draft, 3 November 2026, updated with V7.9. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.2–V6.9, V7.9) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine a container of solar panels standing on a dock. It could go to two places.

The first is a wealthy suburb, where families already have heating, cooling and a car each. They want cheaper bills, and they can pay well. The second is a district in a fast-growing city, where people cook on charcoal and the clinic has power for a few hours a day. They need the panels far more. They can pay far less.

This dock is imagined, and so are both places. But the choice is real, and it is made every day. In a market, the container usually goes where the money is. In UNIRA, it goes where the need is.

This chapter asks how much that single difference matters. It also asks a harder question. Could a kinder market do just as well, without the rest of UNIRA?

Chapter 5 gave the short answer in one table. This chapter opens the box. It shows how the test was built, what each part of UNIRA contributes, where the result is strong and where it is weak.

## A fair test

It is easy to make any system look good. You give it more workers, better technology or more generous assumptions than its rival. Then you announce that it wins.

The UNIRA research tried to avoid that trap. It compared three worlds with **matched resources**. All three have:
- the same population;
- the same technology;
- the same capacity to build power plants, homes and training schools;
- the same response of output to shorter hours;
- the same goals: a 30-hour week and a comfortable life for everyone.

Only the **institutions** change. That way, any difference in the results comes from how each world organises itself. It does not come from luck or wishful thinking.

This is sometimes called a *counterfactual*. It means a careful "what if?" The question is not "what will happen?" It is "what would happen, if only this one thing were different?"

## A closer look: what "closing the gap" means

Before the results, it helps to know what is being measured. The research looked at three needs that cannot simply be shipped from one place to another: energy, health workers and homes.

Food is different. Moving about **7%** of the world's food supply would cover everyone. But a power grid, a nurse or a house must be built or trained where people live.

For each of the three needs, earlier chapters set a comfortable level:

| Need | Decent level | Comfortable level | Where the world stands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Final energy per person, per year | 15.3 GJ | **40 GJ** | about 55 GJ on average, very unequally shared |
| Health workers per 10,000 people | 44.5 (WHO) | **131** (EU level) | 58 on average; **47.4 million** workers in all |
| Floor space per person | 15 m² | **27 m²** | about 26 m² on average: 7 in Ethiopia, 10.5 in India, 45 in France, 55 in the USA |

Averages hide the problem. The world has 58 health workers per 10,000 people on average. Yet **4.2 billion** people live in regions below even the WHO level. Workers are where they are, not where they are needed.

A region's **shortfall** is the distance between where it is and the comfortable level. Add up the shortfalls of every region below the line, and you have the world's shortfall.

"Closing 60% of the gap" means that 60% of that total distance has been covered. A **negative** number means the opposite. The world's shortfall is larger than when it started.

How can a gap grow when the world is building? Because people keep being born. The model follows the UN's 2024 population projection. By 2050 the world will hold about **9.7 billion** people. If new homes and clinics go mostly to places that are already comfortable, the places in need fall further behind.

## A closer look: how the test was run

The comparison did not build new models. It reran two that already existed, changing only the institutional settings.

The first is a **labour ledger** for France in year 1. It counts the hours the economy needs and the hours people work. Chapter 3 described it. It asks whether a 30-hour week can keep today's output.

The second is a **30-year world transition**. It divides the world into about **180** regions. Each year, the model decides where new capacity goes:
- clean energy from solar and wind;
- health workers, from a training plan, trained in-region;
- new homes, from the world's building capacity, which the model sets at **3.3–4.7 billion m²** a year.

In UNIRA's version, new capacity goes first to the regions furthest below the target. Regions above 40 GJ reduce their energy use each year. In the market versions, only part of the new capacity goes to regions in need. The rest goes where it goes today.

Each lever was set from evidence where evidence existed. Where it did not, the research said so. The comparison comes with **5** tests of its own.

## Three worlds

**World B** is a *reformed market*. It is a good version of today. Employment is close to the best any country achieves today: **84%** of adults aged 20 to 64. Wasteful selling activity is cut a little. Armies remain. New clean energy flows the way it does today.

**World B2** is an *ambitious cooperative market*. It is far kinder than today. Half of all new energy capacity goes to regions in need. Rich regions cut their energy use faster.

**World C** is **UNIRA**. Everyone able to work does work, about **88%** of adults. New capacity goes by need first. There is no commercial competition for profit, no army, and an ambitious health-training programme.

| Lever | B: reformed market | B2: cooperative market | C: UNIRA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Employment, ages 20–64 | 84% | 84% | **88%** |
| Activity that only serves selling | modest cut | modest cut | moderate cut |
| New clean energy to regions in need | **20%** | 50% | **100%** |
| Energy cut in rich regions, per year | 2% | 3% | 3%, plus military energy freed |
| Health training | +7% a year | +7% a year | ambitious, plus soldiers and prevention |
| New health workers to regions in need | 50% | 75% | **100%** |
| New homes to regions in need | 30% | 50% | 75% |

Where does the 20% come from? It is today's real pattern. Only about **20%** of the world's clean-energy investment goes to developing countries outside China. Yet those countries hold about **two-thirds** of the world's population. Africa receives about **2%**.

Aid tells a similar story. In 2025, rich donors gave about **0.26%** of their national income in development aid. That was down from 0.34% the year before. The long-standing target is **0.7%**. Only four countries exceed it: Norway, Luxembourg, Sweden and Denmark.

So B is not a caricature of markets. It is today's pattern of investment, with an employment rate near the best any country has reached. B2 is far more generous than anything today.

## The results

The research ran each world for 30 years. It measured how much of the world's shortfall in energy, health care and homes each one closes.

| Outcome | B | B2 | C: UNIRA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Everyone at comfortable energy (40 GJ) by | not within 30 years | not within 30 years | **year 27** |
| Everyone at EU-level care (131 per 10,000) by | not within 30 years | not within 30 years | **year 30** |
| Energy shortfall closed, year 15 | **−14%** (the gap grows) | 14% | **60%** |
| Health shortfall closed, year 15 | −4% | 4% | **38%** |
| Housing shortfall closed, year 30 | −21% | 10% | **50%** |
| France, year 1: extra output per hour needed for a 30-hour week | 5.0% | 5.0% | **0%** |
| Europe pooled, year 1: the same measure | 6.8% | 6.8% | **1.3%** |
| Peak world final energy | 409 EJ | 409 EJ | 409 EJ |

Look at the minus signs in column B. In a reformed market, the world's gaps do not close. They **grow**. Population keeps growing. New capacity flows mostly to those who can pay. Every year, the regions in need fall a little further behind.

B2 does better. But after 15 years it has closed only **14%** of the energy gap and **4%** of the health gap. UNIRA closes **60%** and **38%**.

Now look at the last row. All three worlds use the same amount of energy at their peak: about **409 exajoules**. That is today's level in the UNIRA model. UNIRA does not win by burning more. It wins by sending energy where it is missing.

## An illustration: one year, two allocators

Here is a simple way to picture the difference. It is imagined, with no numbers attached.

Picture two regions. One sits well above 40 GJ. Its homes are warm and its factories run all day. The other sits far below. Its schools close at dusk.

In a given year, the world builds a fixed amount of new solar and wind. In World B, most of it goes to the first region. Its people can pay, and its banks can lend. The second region gets a small share. Meanwhile, its population grows. Next year, its energy per person is barely higher, or even lower.

In World C, the new capacity goes first to the second region. The first region, meanwhile, cuts its use a little each year. Next year the gap is smaller. Year after year, the two meet near the comfortable line.

Neither world built more. Neither world had better panels. The difference is where the panels went.

## Which institution does the work?

UNIRA is a package of institutions. Which ones matter most? The research answered this by switching each lever from B to C, one at a time, and watching the result.

**Needs-first allocation of energy.** On its own, it brings every region to the comfortable level by **year 27**. This is the single most decisive institution. But alone it has a cost. Without restraint in rich regions and without the energy freed from armies, world energy would peak at **453 exajoules**. That is above today's level and outside the climate limits. **Allocation and restraint must go together.**

**Needs-first allocation of homes.** On its own, it moves the housing result from **−21%** to **+50%** by year 30.

**The health programme and training in-region.** Both are needed. Either one alone closes only about **10%** of the health gap by year 15.

**Universal work.** Raising employment from 84% to 88% cuts France's year-1 need for extra output per hour from **5.0%** to **1.9%**.

**No commercial competition.** Removing work that only serves selling, on its own, cuts the same need to **2.6%**. Together with universal work, the gap closes completely.

**Disarmament and faster energy cuts in rich regions.** These matter mainly for the climate, as Chapter 12 explained. They do little for how fast poorer regions catch up.

One warning about this method. Switching levers one at a time ignores how they interact. The energy example shows that interactions can matter a great deal.

## A closer look: why allocation needs restraint

The 453-exajoule result is worth slowing down for. It shows that a fair rule can still break the planet if it stands alone.

Suppose every region below 40 GJ rises towards it, and nobody above it changes anything. The world then simply adds energy at the bottom. Total use climbs well above today's.

The climate cannot carry that. Chapter 10 showed what it can carry. If every person on Earth used 40 GJ in 2050, the total would be about **386 EJ**. That is **6–13% less** than the world uses today. The range depends on the estimate of today's use: 409 EJ in the UNIRA model, or 442 EJ according to the IEA.

To stay at or below today's level while poorer regions rise, regions above 40 GJ must come down. The research found they must cut their use about **4% a year**. If weapons production and military fuel stop, **3% a year** is enough.

What does that mean for a rich region? Take France. If the world's energy were shared equally, France's final energy would fall from about **90 to 51 GJ** per person. That is a cut of about 43%. It is still above the comfortable level. Nigeria's would rise about **nine times**.

The supply side must also move fast. The world added a record **669 GW** of solar and wind in 2025. UNIRA's plan needs that pace multiplied by **1.7 to 1.9**. The central plan builds about **1.2 TW** a year.

So "needs first" is never only about giving. It is about giving and holding back, at the same time. A world that does one without the other fails, either the poor or the planet.

## A closer look: the year-1 numbers for France

The France row deserves a few more words, because it links this chapter to Chapter 3.

In 2023, France's workers put in about **45.6 billion** hours. Capping employees' usual week at 30 hours removes **16.7%** of those hours. To keep the same output, each remaining hour would need to produce **20.1%** more. At the 2015–19 trend, that is **32 years** of productivity growth.

UNIRA does not wait 32 years. It fills the gap in two ways.

The first is **more people working**. Going from 84% to 88% employment means workplaces for about **5 million** new workers in France. It also needs childcare and eldercare, so that parents and carers are free to work.

The second is **less work that only serves selling**. Advertising, speculative finance and real-estate intermediation could free between **1.7% and 6.6%** of all hours. UNIRA cuts this activity more deeply than either market world.

In World B, both levers are weaker. The gap left is **5.0%** in France. Across Europe, pooled together, it is **6.8%**. In World C, it is **0%** in France and **1.3%** across Europe.

## How kind would a market have to be?

A thoughtful reader will push back. "Fine," they might say. "But what if a market world were simply more generous? Send more aid and more investment to the poor, and keep the rest."

The research tested exactly that. It took the cooperative market, B2, and raised the share of new capacity sent to regions in need, step by step. In this test, the same share applies to energy, health workers and homes. Everything else stayed market: armies kept, today's pace of training growth, 84% employment.

| Share of new capacity to regions in need | Everyone at 40 GJ by | Energy closed, year 15 | Health closed, year 15 | Housing closed, year 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50% | not within 30 years | 14% | −4% | 10% |
| 65% | not within 30 years | 28% | 1% | 34% |
| 80% | not within 30 years | 41% | 5% | 50% |
| 90% | year 29 | 51% | 8% | 50% |
| 100% | year 27 | 60% | 11% | 50% |
| **C: UNIRA** | **year 27** | **60%** | **38%** | **50%** |

A careful reader may spot something. The 50% row shows −4% for health, yet B2 showed +4%. The reason is the health-worker share. B2 itself sends 75% of new health workers to regions in need. In this test, the 50% row sends only 50%.

Three things stand out.

First, a market world catches up on **housing** at about 80%. Second, it nearly matches UNIRA on **energy** only at about **90%**. That is more than four times today's 20%.

Third, at that point the distinction starts to disappear. A world that directs 90% of new capacity by need, by agreement, is no longer allocating by purchasing power. **In practice, it is needs-first allocation.** The real question is then how to build institutions that can direct capacity that way, and keep doing it.

## Health still lags

Even at 100%, the market world closes only **11%** of the health gap by year 15. At 90%, it closes **8%**. UNIRA closes **38%**.

Why such a gap, when sharing is the same? Because health workers are not shipped like panels. They must be trained, and training takes years and schools.

In France, training a general practitioner takes **10 years**: six years of medical school and four of specialist training. Nursing takes **3 years**, with **4,620 hours** of training from September 2026. Even a care assistant needs **1,540 hours**, about 11 months.

The world needs about **107 million** health workers to give everyone EU-level care. It has **47.4 million**. The research tested how long it takes to close that gap, depending on training:

| Training plan | Years until everyone has EU-level care (central case) |
| --- | --- |
| Training as today | never |
| Training grows 7% a year for 15 years | 37 |
| Plus 25% of the world's **27.4 million** soldiers volunteer | 36 |
| Plus healthier lives (3% less need) | 34 |
| Ambitious: 10% a year, half of soldiers, strong prevention | **23** |

The market worlds in this chapter train at the pace of the second row. UNIRA follows the ambitious plan. These years come from a separate health model. The full 30-year world transition gives year 30 for UNIRA.

UNIRA's health advantage comes from three things the market world in this test does not have:
- a much larger training programme, built in the regions that need it;
- former soldiers volunteering to train in health;
- prevention, from healthier and less stressful lives.

Disarmament helps most in the early years. By year 10, it lifts the world to **71** health workers per 10,000, against 64 without it. Chapter 8 tells this story in full.

Allocation alone cannot create nurses. A world must decide to train them, at scale, for decades.

## What if UNIRA's institutions work badly?

These results assume UNIRA's allocator works as designed. Chapter 14 looks at failure in detail. Here is the short version.

The research tested real-world errors: wrong census data, late data, regions that exaggerate their needs, and resources that go missing.

| Scenario | Energy closed, year 15 | Health, year 15 | Housing, year 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| UNIRA, perfect allocation | 60% | 38% | 50% |
| UNIRA, strong safeguards | **52%** | **34%** | **40%** |
| UNIRA, no safeguards | 36% | 24% | 19% |
| UNIRA, worst governance observed | 15% | 12% | −9% |
| Cooperative market (B2) | 14% | 4% | 10% |

With strong safeguards, UNIRA keeps about **85%** of its ideal performance. Even with typical failures and no safeguards, it still beats the cooperative market. Only under the worst governance ever observed does the advantage vanish.

One fairness point matters here. In this table, the market world keeps its ideal results. It was not stress-tested for leakage or bad data. Yet aid leaks in real life too: in aid-dependent countries, about **7.5%** was moved offshore by elites. So on this point, the comparison does not favour UNIRA.

## What the critics say, and what the research answers

A good test invites hard questions. Here are the strongest ones, with the most honest answers the research allows.

**"You gave the market no advantage. Markets are more efficient."**

That is a fair point, and the research does not settle it. All three worlds have the same technology and the same response of output to shorter hours. The model gives UNIRA no efficiency bonus. It gives markets none either.

What the model does not count matters too. UNIRA's own institutions need people. A digital currency, allocation bodies and a world democracy all take hours of work. The research says these hours are "not yet counted". A critic is right to ask for them.

**"Your reformed market is a straw man."**

World B uses verified numbers for the part that matters most: today's 20% share of clean-energy investment. Its employment rate, 84%, is close to the best any country reached in 2025. World B2 is more generous than anything that exists today. Aid is falling, not rising.

There is one real weakness. The 30% share of new homes in B is an assumption. No source splits new building by income group. The global building report used by the research says such estimates "are lacking".

**"Needs-first allocation is central planning. We know how that ended."**

Chapter 4 looked at the shortage economies honestly. Fixed prices without enough goods led to queues, rationing and black markets. UNIRA's answer is to plan in physical terms: production matched to needs, buffer stocks, and incomes that cannot exceed the goods available.

But be precise about what this chapter tested. It tested the allocation of **three** kinds of new capacity: energy, health workers and homes. It did not test planning millions of products for billions of people. The research names that as an open problem. It calls it the "knowledge problem", and lists it among its own tests.

**"A market that sent 90% by need would do just as well."**

On energy and housing, nearly. On health, no. And a market that sends 90% of its new capacity by need, by collective decision, has adopted UNIRA's core rule. The disagreement then becomes about the rest of the package: universal work, no army, the money cap. Those are fair things to debate. The comparison does not decide them.

**"The military energy you free up is a guess."**

Partly, yes. Military activity causes about **5.5%** of global emissions, within a range of 3.3–7.0%. That figure includes supply chains. But no global statistic for military *energy* use exists. The research uses the emissions share as a stand-in, and says so. Fortunately, this lever matters mostly for the climate, not for how fast poorer regions catch up.

## Can a world without profit still invent?

There is one critic this chapter has not yet answered. "Markets may be unfair, but they invent. Profit pays for the new drug, the better battery, the cheaper solar panel. Take profit away, and progress stops."

The comparison above cannot answer that, because it gave all three worlds the same technology. So the research looked at the evidence separately. It gathered **67** sources, read **61** of them in full text, and judged six ways of keeping invention alive without profit.

| Mechanism | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| A fixed share of work for research, open results, no patents | **Supported for research, weak for development.** Publicly funded research lies behind all **210** new medicines approved in the United States in 2010–16. Restricting access to gene data cut follow-on research by **20–30%**. |
| Prizes | **Supported**, but only for clearly defined problems |
| Pay differences for skill and experience | **Partly supported.** They raise effort only when people can see why they were earned. Unexplained differences cut output by **0.45** standard deviations in one study. |
| Expert-approved projects and cooperatives | **Partly supported.** A single expert gate is biased against new ideas, so money should come from several bodies, and partly by lottery. |
| Competition for quality, not profit | **Partly supported.** Plausible, but never tested at the scale of a whole economy. |
| A 30-hour week, for creativity | **Weak.** No causal evidence was found. |

The research also found a new problem in UNIRA's own design. Prices are fixed by the hours a product takes. So a team that halves those hours also halves what its product earns. That punishes exactly the people who improve things. Soviet economists made the same complaint in 1962.

**Article 3d** is the answer. In plain words:
- **At least 3%** of all working hours go to research and development, and at least a third of that to developing and scaling up new products, not only to research.
- **Results are open.** What One Earth or a region pays for is published, with its data. Inventors are named and honoured. Nobody may own an idea so as to stop others using it.
- **Many funders.** Research money comes from at least three independent bodies. Sound proposals are funded partly by lottery, so unusual ideas are not shut out. At least a fifth goes to high-risk work or to people funded for the first time.
- **Prizes and promises to buy** are offered for clearly defined problems, with rules fixed in advance.
- **Whoever improves, gains.** A team that cuts the hours needed for a product keeps part of the hours saved, for a limited time. It can take them as shorter hours at the same pay, a step up the salary scale, or resources for its next project. Its targets are never raised because it did better than planned (Article 3d.5).
- **Never above five times.** No prize or reward lifts anyone's pay above five times the base salary, or their savings above the money cap and protected accounts. A personal prize is at most one year's salary (Article 3d.12, Article 14.5).
- **Freedom to try, duty to stop.** Any group may propose a project, and needs approval from only one of several bodies. Every project has a budget, an end date and a test of success. A project that fails is closed, but the people who tried keep their pay level and may try again.
- **People choose.** For goods in common use, people can choose between designs from different teams, and production follows their choices.

And because the evidence is thin, Article 3d measures itself. Each year the Scientific Council publishes **eight** indicators: the share of hours in research, how fast the hours needed for the essential basket fall, the share of production from products less than five years old, and others. If research falls below 2.5% of hours, or the essential basket gets cheaper in hours by less than 0.5% a year, for **two** years running, the Assembly must review the rules. If results stay weak for **five** years, the rules on prices, pay and research funding must be reformed by law, including new rewards for innovators if that is what works.

Is this enough? Nobody knows. The research is honest that it is a design built on partial evidence. But it no longer rests on hope alone. It says in advance how failure would be noticed, and what must happen then.

## The honest caveat

It would be easy to end this chapter with a cheer. It would also be dishonest.

**UNIRA's advantage is entirely institutional.** The research gave it no hidden efficiency bonus. Technology, capacities and productivity are identical in all three worlds. UNIRA does better for three reasons only:
- it directs new capacity by need rather than by ability to pay;
- everyone able to work does work;
- it stops work that only serves selling.

That is the strength of the result. It is also its weak point. **The advantage holds only if the institutions work.**

The model shows what needs-first allocation and universal work would achieve. It cannot show that a world institution can direct new capacity by need without error, delay or capture. It cannot show that 88% employment is reachable.

No country has reached it. Here are the highest employment rates for ages 20–64 in 2025:

| Country | Employment rate, ages 20–64, 2025 |
| --- | --- |
| Iceland | **85.8%** |
| Malta | 83.6% |
| Netherlands | 83.4% |
| Czechia | 82.9% |
| Switzerland | 82.4% |
| European Union | 76.1% |
| France | 75.4% |

A fair critic can also say this. A cooperative market sending 80% of new capacity to regions in need would close much of the gap, without the rest of UNIRA. That is true for energy and housing, though not for health. The research names testing this boundary as its next step.

So the research sets its own trap. If a reformed market economy, with the same resources, reached the same outcomes, UNIRA would add nothing beyond its assumptions. That is one of the tests that could prove this book wrong. Chapter 16 lists the others.

## Back to the dock

Return, for a moment, to the imagined container of solar panels.

The research cannot say who should receive it in every single case. Real decisions involve roads, ports, skills and weather. What it can say is what happens when the same choice is made millions of times, for thirty years.

If the container usually goes where the money is, the world's gaps grow. If it usually goes where the need is, and rich places use a little less each year, the gaps close within a generation.

That is the whole argument of this chapter, in one picture. The next chapter asks the obvious follow-up question. Who decides where the container goes, and what stops them from sending it to their friends?

## What the evidence says

- With matched resources, a reformed market world lets the gaps **grow**: energy **−14%**, health **−4%**, housing **−21%**.
- A cooperative market closes **14%**, **4%** and **10%**. UNIRA closes **60%**, **38%** and **50%**.
- All three worlds peak at the same world energy use, about **409 EJ**. UNIRA wins by where it sends energy, not by using more.
- Today only about **20%** of clean-energy investment reaches developing countries outside China, home to about two-thirds of humanity.
- Needs-first allocation of energy is the most decisive single institution, but it must come with restraint in rich regions, or world energy peaks at **453 EJ**.
- A market world must direct about **90%** of new capacity by need to nearly match UNIRA on energy. By then it is practising needs-first allocation.
- Even then it lags on health: **8%** against **38%** by year 15. Nurses must be trained, not shipped.
- With strong safeguards, UNIRA keeps about **85%** of its ideal advantage.
- Open, publicly funded research is well supported by evidence; prizes work for clear problems; pay differences help only when people can see why. A fixed price per hour would punish innovators, so Article 3d lets teams keep part of the hours they save, within the five-times ceiling.

## What we still don't know

- The B and B2 lever values are policy scenarios, not observations. The 30% share of new homes in B is an assumption, because no source splits construction by income group.
- One-lever-at-a-time attribution ignores interactions between institutions.
- The cost of building UNIRA's institutions, the hours they need, and how people would behave in them are not modelled.
- Whether a world institution can direct new capacity by need without capture is untested.
- Whether 88% employment is reachable is unknown. No country has reached it.
- The comparison covers three kinds of new capacity. Planning the whole economy without prices is a much larger problem, and it remains open.
- No global figure exists for military energy use, or for the number of new doctors trained each year.
- Whether a world without profit would invent as fast as a market world. The evidence is weak for development and scale-up, and the Article 3d thresholds are provisional until starting values are measured.
