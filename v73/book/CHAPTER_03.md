# Chapter 3 — The 30-hour week

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.2–V6.4) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

It is half past three on a Tuesday afternoon. In a primary school in Lille, children pour out of the gate. Waiting for them, among the grandparents and childminders, is a father who has just finished his six-hour day. He takes his daughter's hand. They walk to the park, then home, where he cooks while she does her homework at the kitchen table. Her mother gets home at four.

This is an imagined scene, not a report. But it is the reason for this chapter. UNIRA's 30-hour week, six hours a day for five days, is not mainly an economic idea. It is a family idea: time for children, partners, parents and friends. The question this chapter asks is whether the world can afford it.

## Why 30 hours, and why from the start

UNIRA's draft constitution lists time among the basic rights, next to food, a home and health care. It promises "a standard working week of 30 hours (6 hours × 5 days), so that families can live together".

The author of UNIRA made two choices that shape this whole chapter.

The first is that the 30-hour week starts **from day one**. The research also tested a gentler path: an enforced 35-hour week first, then 30 later. The author rejected it. The 35-hour results are kept in this chapter only as a comparison, to show how much harder 30 hours is.

The second choice is that **everyone able to work does work**. The author called this the most important rule of all. The shorter week is for families. Universal work is what pays for it.

The rest of this chapter tests whether those two choices fit together. It starts with France, where the data are richest, and ends with the world.

## What a shorter week costs

Today a full-time employee in France usually works about **38.9 hours** a week. If everyone's working week were capped at 30 hours and nothing else changed, France would work about **16.7%** fewer hours.

To produce the same amount with fewer hours, each hour would have to produce more. How much more? About **20%** more output per hour. If the self-employed, who often work long weeks, were capped too, the figure would be about **29%**.

Is that a lot? Between 2015 and 2019, output per hour in France grew by only about **0.6%** a year. At that pace, a 20% gain would take about **32 years**. Simply waiting for productivity to rise will not deliver a 30-hour week soon.

So something else is needed. The rest of this chapter looks at four sources, and finds that together they can be enough, if one condition is met.

### A closer look: from 16.7% to 20%

Why does losing 16.7% of hours need a 20% gain, not a 16.7% one? It is a quirk of percentages, and it trips up many people.

Start with France's **45.6 billion** hours in 2023. Capping every employee's usual week at 30 hours removes about **7.6 billion** of them. About **38.0 billion** remain. To make the same output with 38.0 billion hours instead of 45.6 billion, each remaining hour must do the work of 45.6 ÷ 38.0, or about **1.20** hours today. That is the 20% gain.

The research did not simply cut everyone to 30 hours. It looked at each sector separately, and at full-timers and part-timers separately. A full-timer at 38.9 hours loses almost 9 hours. A part-timer at 23 hours loses nothing. Only by separating them does the true loss appear.

It also assumed that holidays, public holidays and sickness stay as they are today, so that yearly hours fall in proportion to weekly hours. Some choices would change the result:

| Scenario, France 2023 | Hours left | Extra output per hour needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employees capped at 30 h; self-employed unchanged (main case) | 37.97 billion | **20.1%** |
| Everyone capped, self-employed included | 35.33 billion | 29.1% |
| Main case, but full-timers give back two weeks of extra days off | 39.31 billion | 16.0% |
| Main case, using 2024 data | — | 19.9% |

The result barely moves between years. The main uncertainty is the self-employed. The two official sources disagree by about 21% on how many hours they work, so the research leaves them uncapped in the main case. UNIRA has not yet decided how the 30-hour rule applies to them.

### Against history, two ways

The 32 years quoted above uses France's productivity growth from 2015 to 2019. The research also measured a second window, 2015 to 2023, which includes the years after the pandemic. In that window, output per hour grew by only about **0.06%** a year. At that pace, a 20% gain would take about **301 years**.

Neither figure is a forecast. Both say the same thing: ordinary productivity growth will not deliver a 30-hour week in any reasonable time.

### For comparison: an enforced 35-hour week

France's legal week is already 35 hours, but full-timers actually work 38.9. What if 35 hours were enforced as a real cap?

| France, employees capped | 30 hours | 35 hours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours lost | 16.7% | 7.2% |
| Extra output per hour needed, no offsets | 20.1% | **7.8%** |
| Years of 2015–19 productivity growth | 32 | 13 |
| France's reserve of people who could work, as a share of the gap | 51–59% | **142–161%** |

At 35 hours, France already has more people willing and available to work than the hours it would lose. On central assumptions, output holds with no extra productivity at all. The research concludes that an enforced 35-hour week is within reach on today's evidence. A 30-hour week is about **2.5 times** harder.

That is the price of the author's choice. It is a price paid in organisation, not in comfort, as the rest of the chapter shows.

## Source one: work freed from selling

Chapter 2 showed that moderate reductions in advertising, speculative finance and similar activities would free about **4%** of France's hours, and deep reductions about **6.6%**. In UNIRA, where nobody competes to sell for profit, that is not a hope. It follows from the rules.

## Source two: rested people work better

People who work shorter days are less tired, make fewer mistakes, and organise their work better. When France moved towards a 35-hour week around the year 2000, firms that cut hours raised output per hour enough to recover roughly **40–50%** of the hours they gave up. That figure has an important caveat: those were volunteer firms receiving subsidies. Chapter 4 tells that story in full.

Evidence from many studies suggests that, in ordinary businesses, between **10% and 35%** of lost hours come back through higher productivity. In hospitals, care homes and schools, where much of the work is simply *being there* for people, it is nearer **0–15%**.

So shorter hours pay back part of their own cost. But not all of it, and less in care.

### A closer look: how much does a tired hour cost?

Researchers call the share of lost hours that comes back through higher productivity *theta*. If theta is zero, output falls exactly in line with hours. If theta is one, output does not fall at all. The question is where real workplaces sit between the two.

Two studies matter most for a cut down to 30 hours.

The first looked at British munitions workers, mostly women, during the First World War. Their hours varied a great deal, and their output was carefully recorded. The economist Pencavel found that "below 49 weekly hours, variations in output are proportional to variations in hours". Above about 49 hours, tiredness set in, and extra hours added less and less. Output peaked at about **63** hours a week. So cutting very long weeks costs little. Cutting already moderate weeks costs almost in full.

The second study followed agents in a call centre, most of whom worked part time, around 24 hours a week. Their schedules were set centrally, which made a fair comparison possible. The finding: "an increase in working hours by 1 percent leads to an increase in output by only 0.9 percent". In other words, theta is about **0.1** in the range from 20 to 35 hours.

Put together, these studies say that the first few hours cut from a 39-hour week come partly back, and the hours cut below 35 come back much less. The research therefore treats the cut in two steps. From 38.9 to 35 hours it uses the evidence from real reforms. From 35 to 30 it uses the fatigue evidence. The combined central value for businesses is about **0.20** in the short run.

What about the famous four-day-week trials? In the United Kingdom in 2022, **61** volunteer firms with about **2,900** staff cut hours from about 38 to 34 a week, and reported revenue holding up. But the firms chose to take part, and there was no comparison group. The research does not use such trials as evidence for a whole economy. Firms that expect it to work are the ones that sign up.

## Source three: everyone who can work, works

This is the largest source, and it is UNIRA's choice. Today, many adults who could work do not have a job, or work fewer hours than they want.

In France in 2023, about **2.3 million** people were unemployed, and almost **4.8 million** belonged to the wider group who want work: the unemployed, part-timers who want more hours, and people available for work but not searching.

The first lesson from history is sobering: **cutting working hours does not, by itself, create jobs.** The French 35-hour reform created jobs mainly where it was paid for by subsidies. Studies in Chile, Japan and Portugal found no net job creation. If UNIRA wants more people at work, it must organise it deliberately, with training, childcare, accessible jobs and the guaranteed salary.

### A closer look: how many people really find work?

How fast can people who are out of work today be brought in? An earlier version of the research simply assumed that half of them would join in the first year. The current version measures it.

Eurostat publishes how many people in each group have a job one year later. For France in 2023:

| Group | Share with a job one year later | Best three countries in Europe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unemployed | **41%** | 61% |
| Available but not searching, or searching but not available | **25%** | 36% |
| Part-timers moving to full time | **23%** | — |

These figures look encouraging, but they hide a trap. Most of these moves are ordinary churn. People find jobs every year anyway, and other people lose them. The pools refill. A 41% job-finding rate does not mean a new policy would add 41% of the unemployed to the workforce.

So the research asked a more modest question: what if France matched the best job-finding rates in Europe? That adds about **0.53 million** people in the first year. The earlier assumption had been **1.8 million**. The research calls this the most important correction in its labour work. Ordinary policy, even at its best, moves people slowly.

That is why UNIRA's rule matters. It does not wait for people to find jobs. It sets a target for how many adults work, and builds what is needed to reach it.

### Universal work

What if UNIRA does that? In 2025, about **75.4%** of French adults aged 20 to 64 had a job. The best countries in Europe reach about **83–86%**: Iceland **85.8%**, Malta **83.6%** and the Netherlands **83.4%**. UNIRA's principle is that everyone able to work, works.

| Employment rate, ages 20–64 | New workers in France | Extra output per hour still needed in year 1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Today's job-finding (best practice) | about 0.5 million | **10.2%** |
| 84% (today's best in Europe) | about 3.6 million | **2.6%** |
| 88% (beyond any country today) | about 5.1 million | **0% — the gap is closed** |

With universal work at about 88%, France keeps today's output under a 30-hour week from the first year, on central assumptions. Every adult works less, and more adults work.

The same holds for Europe as a whole, treated as one pooled region, if a little higher. At 84%, Europe would still need **4.4%** more output per hour. At 88%, **1.3%**. At 92%, nothing.

These results assume that new workers are only **75%** as productive as experienced ones in their first year. They also assume three conditions that the research names plainly:

- workplaces, equipment and supervision for 4 to 5 million new workers in France in year one;
- childcare and eldercare, so that parents and carers can work. Those care hours are themselves part of the demand;
- an employment rate higher than any country has ever reached.

### A closer look: the year-one ledger

The research brings all four sources together in what it calls a ledger: an account, in hours, of how the gap is filled.

Under today's job-finding at its best, the central case looks like this. A 30-hour week opens a gap of about **7.6 billion** hours in France. It is filled:

- **18%** by the productivity response to shorter hours;
- **6%** by bringing people into work at the best rates seen in Europe;
- **23%** by reducing work that serves only selling;
- the remaining **53%**, about **8.9%** of all hours, is uncovered.

That uncovered part is what produces the 10.2% figure in the table above. Then the research raised the employment rate step by step. At 84%, most of the uncovered part disappears. At 88%, all of it does.

| Year-one case, France | Productivity response | Bringing people in | Selling activity cut | Extra output per hour still needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | weak | best-practice gap | modest | 14.7% |
| **Central** | central | best-practice gap | moderate | **10.2%** |
| Optimistic | strong | full best-practice rates | deep | 0.5% |
| Universal work at 88% | central | rule 14 | moderate | **0%** |

### The Netherlands, and an uneven continent

The Netherlands offers a hint that this is not a dream. It has the shortest average working week in Europe, about **31 hours**, and one of the highest employment rates, about **83.5%**. Three in four of its people aged 55 to 64 are in work. Its own gap for a 30-hour week is the smallest in Europe, about **12.5%**.

Across 30 European countries, those with shorter weeks tend to have more people in work. The link is moderate. That is a correlation, not proof, and many other things differ between countries. But it points the same way.

The reserve of people who could work is spread very unevenly. Spain, Italy and Greece hold large reserves, between about **55% and 126%** of their own gaps. Countries that already employ most adults have little left: Germany about **25%**, Sweden **35%**, Czechia **22%**, Iceland **19%**. In those countries, the gap must be covered in other ways. For a world federation, that is an argument for regions helping one another, with people working where they already live.

### Is a duty to work fair?

A rule that everyone able to work must work raises a fair worry. Could it become forced labour?

The draft constitution answers in three lines. Every adult able to work has the right and the duty to contribute, "with free choice of occupation". Illness, disability, caring duties, study and retirement exempt a person from the duty, "without loss of income or rights". And "no one may be forced into a particular job". The research notes that these conditions are what keep the rule compatible with the international conventions against forced labour.

So the duty is real, but it is a duty to contribute, not an order to take a given job. People who cannot work receive a guaranteed income. The guarantee is what makes the duty humane.

## Source four: technology gives time back

Over the long run, technology raises output per hour. In most economies today, those gains become more consumption or more profit. In UNIRA, prices are fixed and nothing is produced only to be sold. So gains in productivity become something else: **free time**. The draft constitution writes this down: "Gains in productivity become shorter working hours, never higher prices or unemployment."

Our simulation of the UNIRA economy over 60 years shows what that means. If productivity grows by 1% a year, the working week can fall from **30 hours to about 23 hours within 30 years**, with the same comfortable standard of living, and to about 17 hours after 60 years. At 2% a year, it falls to about 17 hours after 30 years, and about 9 after 60. Artificial intelligence and automation, which many people fear today, would become a source of time rather than unemployment.

There is a discipline hidden in this promise. If average pay drifted up faster than productivity, people would hold more money than there are goods to buy at fixed prices. The research found that a drift of just 0.5% a year leaves demand **13%** above supply after 30 years. So the constitution adds a rule: average pay may rise only as fast as productivity. Time, not pay, is where the gains go. Chapter 6 explains why.

## Where it is hard: care

One part of the economy does not fit easily: health and care. A nurse cannot care for more patients just because they are less tired. A care home needs staff present day and night.

The evidence on this is grim. A study of hospitals across nine countries found that each extra patient per nurse raised the odds of a patient dying in hospital by **7%**. When Iceland moved its workers to a shorter week, most workplaces coped. Hospitals did not. Extra staff had to be hired, and about **two-thirds** of the extra cost was in health care.

Picture a hospital ward at night. This one is imagined. Two nurses cover twenty beds from nine in the evening to seven in the morning. Under a 30-hour week, each nurse works fewer nights a month. But the ward still needs two nurses every night, every week of the year. The hours that each nurse gives up must be worked by someone else. There is no faster way to sit with a frightened patient at three in the morning.

That is why the research treats care differently from a factory or an office. In a factory, a shorter day can be partly made up by better machines and better organisation. On a ward, most of the work is presence. If each worker's hours fall, coverage falls by the same amount, unless more people are hired. The evidence points to some indirect gains, such as less sickness absence and fewer staff leaving. But those gains are thin, and they do not replace people.

With a 30-hour week, France would need about **16.9%** more people in health and social work. In detail:

| Occupation | Working today | Extra needed | Training time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nurses | 607,644 | about **103,000** | 3 years |
| Doctors (upper bound) | 266,380 | about 45,000 | 10 years for a GP |
| Care workers | 859,956 | about **145,000** | about 11 months for a care assistant |

Training takes time: about **3 years** for a nurse and **10 years** for a general practitioner. No country can produce them in a year.

The pipeline is already tight. France trains about **24,800** nurses a year, while about **20,300** leave the profession. The spare capacity is only a few thousand a year. Even if nurse training doubled, it would take about **8 years** to close the gap. For doctors, doubling intake would take about **16 years**. And the health and social sector already has job vacancy rates of **2.9–3.8%**.

### What UNIRA can do about it

The research is clear that no training system can respond within the first year. If the 30-hour rule applies from day one, the right to care is at risk unless specific measures are designed. It names four:

- **care-assistant training** for the people brought into work, since the course takes about 11 months;
- **voluntary return** of qualified staff who have left the profession;
- **help between regions** under the One Earth framework;
- **redesigning tasks**, so that nurses spend more time on nursing.

Choosing among these is a decision for people and their representatives, not for a model. But one thing is certain. UNIRA must plan for care from the first day: training places, accessible courses for new workers, and help between regions. Chapter 8 returns to it at world scale.

## And the world?

Chapter 1 gave the world's answer. If 80% of adults aged 15 to 64 worked 30 hours, the world would have about **88%** of today's working hours. That 12% gap is smaller than France's, because the world has a larger reserve of people who could work.

There is a health bonus too. Today about **488 million** people work 55 hours a week or more. In one year, those long hours caused about **745,000** deaths from heart disease and stroke. A 30-hour week removes that exposure entirely. Chapter 8 weighs what that means for health care.

## Three questions a reader might ask

**"Why not start at 35 hours and get to 30 later?"** That would be easier, and the research shows it. But the author chose 30 hours from the start, for family time, and the research tests that choice rather than replacing it. The cost is clear: closing the gap in the first years depends on bringing people into work at a scale never seen.

**"Will new workers really be as good as experienced ones?"** Not at first. The research assumes they are **75%** as productive in their first year. That is a guess, not a measurement, and it is listed below as something to test.

**"What if universal work fails?"** Then the gap reopens. Under today's best job-finding rates, France would be about **9%** short of today's output. The research is honest that the gap closes only under the optimistic cases. Something would have to give: higher productivity over time, fewer goods, or a temporary shortfall managed so that essential needs come first.

**"Will I earn less if I work less?"** In UNIRA, pay is not counted in euros at all, so there is no simple before-and-after. The research did test what a base salary buys. A worker on the lowest salary, 5,000 U a month for a 30-hour week, who saves the maximum of half of it, can still buy about **86%** of what an average French person consumes today. By the research's measure, half the people of France, Germany, Italy and Spain would already be better off at that level, even at the bottom of the scale. And health care and education are free on top. Chapter 6 explains how the money works.

**"Who looks after the children while more parents work?"** This is a real cost, and the research counts it as one. Childcare and eldercare are among the conditions for universal work. Their hours are part of the demand the new workers must meet, not a free extra. A society that wants more parents at work, and shorter days for all, must build the care that lets both happen.

## Half past three, revisited

Return to the father at the school gate in Lille.

For him to be there at half past three, many things must be true at once. His employer must have reorganised the work, so that six hours do more than they did. Some of the work that once served only selling must have stopped. His neighbour, who had no job, must now be working too, perhaps in the after-school club where the girl's friends play. And somewhere a care home must have found the extra staff it needs, trained in a course that started a year earlier.

None of these is impossible. Most have happened somewhere, on a smaller scale. What has never happened is all of them together, at once, across a whole region. That is the test UNIRA sets itself.

## What the evidence says

- A 30-hour week in France, with nothing else changing, needs about **20% more output per hour**: about **32 years** of recent productivity growth. An enforced 35-hour week needs only **7.8%**, and is within reach today.
- Shorter hours partly pay for themselves: **10–35%** of lost hours come back in ordinary businesses, **0–15%** in care.
- Cutting hours alone creates no jobs. Bringing more adults into work must be organised. Matching Europe's best job-finding rates adds only about **0.53 million** people in year one.
- With about **88%** of adults aged 20–64 at work, France keeps today's output from year 1. Europe's best countries are at about **83–86%** today.
- Technology can then reduce the week further: about **23 hours** after 30 years at 1% productivity growth.
- Health and care are the bottleneck: about **103,000 nurses** and **145,000 care workers** are needed, and training takes years.

## What we still don't know

- No country has ever reached 88% employment. Whether it can be done, and how fast, is the central question of this chapter.
- New workers may be less productive in their first year. We assumed 75% as productive, an assumption to test.
- The evidence on productivity comes from cuts of about 10% in working hours. A cut from 39 to 30 hours is larger than anything studied.
- How the 30-hour rule applies to the self-employed is not yet decided, and their hours are uncertain.
- The workplaces and equipment that millions of new workers need take time to build. That lead time is not yet modelled.
- A shorter paid week may shift some time into unpaid work at home. That is not counted.
