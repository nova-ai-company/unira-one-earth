# Chapter 8 — Health, education and care for all

*First draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.3, V6.5–V6.8, Working Paper 2 and the draft constitution) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

It is early morning at a small health post in a rural district. The queue started before dawn. A mother holds a feverish child. An old man waits with a bandaged foot. A young woman is pregnant for the first time and has walked a long way.

There is one nurse. They know most of the people in the queue by name. They will see as many as they can before dark, and some will be told to come back tomorrow.

Far away, in another country, a soldier sits in a classroom. Their unit has been dissolved. They are not learning a new weapon. They are learning how to take blood pressure, clean a wound and spot the signs of a difficult birth. In a few years, they could be the second nurse at a health post like this one.

Both scenes are imagined. They are composites, not reports. But they hold the two ideas of this chapter. The first is that the world's deepest shortage is not food or energy, but trained people who care for others. The second is that the shortage can be closed, slowly, if the world decides to train them, and if it stops spending people on war.

## A promise written into the rules

UNIRA makes a simple promise. **Health care and education are free, for everyone, throughout life.**

The promise first appeared in the design rules. Rule 15, on retirees, ends with a short sentence: health care and education are free. The draft constitution turns it into a right. Article 6 lists what every person is guaranteed, and among them is "health care and education, free of charge, throughout life".

The constitution then protects that promise in three ways:

- **Article 8** says rights in Article 6 are never withdrawn as a punishment.
- **Article 9** says no law, allocation decision, sanction or emergency may reduce them.
- **Article 7** says study, like illness and caring for others, exempts a person from the duty to work, without loss of income or rights.

A promise on paper is easy. Keeping it is harder. To keep it, the world needs people: nurses, doctors, midwives, care workers and teachers. This chapter asks whether there can be enough of them, and how long it would take.

## The world's real shortage

Chapter 1 showed that the world already produces enough food for everyone, and more than enough energy for a decent life. Health care is different.

Today the world has about **47.4 million** doctors, nurses and midwives. That is about **58 per 10,000 people**.

The World Health Organization sets a minimum: about **44.5 health workers per 10,000 people**. On a world average, we are above it. The world has **1.32 times** what the minimum requires.

But that average misleads. Nurses and doctors cannot be shipped like grain. And UNIRA forbids forced relocation, so nobody can be ordered to move to another region. What matters is where health workers live. Today about **4.2 billion people** live in regions below the WHO minimum. In those regions, about **7.7 million** workers are missing.

UNIRA aims higher than the minimum. Its goal is a *comfortable* level of care, like the European Union's: about **131 health workers per 10,000 people**. Measured against that goal, the world has less than half of what it needs: **0.45 times**. About **7.3 billion people** live in regions below it.

| Level of care | Health workers per 10,000 | World supply ÷ need | People in regions below |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Today's world average | 58 | — | — |
| Decent (WHO minimum) | 44.5 | 1.32 | 4.2 billion |
| Comfortable (EU level) | 131 | **0.45** | 7.3 billion |

To bring everyone to the EU level, the world needs about **59 million more** health workers. That more than doubles today's total. By 2050, the world's population will reach about **9.66 billion**, so total need rises to about **107 million**.

This is the longest lead time in the whole UNIRA plan. Food can be moved within a year. A nurse takes years to train.

## For rich regions: build up, don't take away

Under strict equal shares, a rich region would simply hand over part of what it has. For food and energy, Chapter 1 showed that rich regions would still stay comfortable.

For health, equal shares would be a disaster. France has about **134** health workers per 10,000 people. Under equal shares it would fall to about **59**. That is still above the WHO minimum, but far below what French people have today.

So the UNIRA research draws a clear conclusion. **For health, "equal share" must mean building up the South, not taking from the North.** Nobody's hospital is emptied. New health workers are trained in the regions that need them. Article 10 of the draft constitution says it directly: the new capacity directed to regions by need includes "health workers trained in-region".

## First, a problem at home

Before looking at the world, look at one rich country on day one.

Chapter 3 showed that France can keep today's output under a 30-hour week, if almost everyone who can work does work. Care is the exception. In hospitals and care homes, much of the work is *being there*. Rested staff make fewer mistakes, but they cannot be in two places at once. In care, only **0–15%** of lost hours come back through higher productivity.

So a 30-hour week means more people. France's health and social work sector would need about **16.9%** more staff. In round numbers:

| Occupation | Working today | Extra needed for 30 hours | Training time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nurses | about 608,000 | about **100,000** | **3 years** |
| Doctors (upper bound) | about 266,000 | about 45,000 | **10 years** for a GP |
| Care workers | about 860,000 | about **145,000** | about **11 months** (1,540 hours) |

No training system can respond within the first year. That is true whatever happens to the rest of the economy.

The numbers are tight even today. France trains about **24,800** nurses a year, while about **20,300** leave each year. Job vacancies in health and social work already run at **2.9–3.8%**. If training intake doubled, the nurse gap would close in about **8 years**, and the doctor gap in about **16**.

What can be done in the meantime? The research lists options. Choosing among them is a democratic decision, not a modelling choice:

- care-assistant courses for the many new workers who enter work under UNIRA;
- the voluntary return of qualified staff who have left;
- help from other regions, under the One Earth framework;
- redesigning tasks, so that nurses and doctors do what only they can do.

If the 30-hour rule applied to care from the very first day, without these measures, the right to care would be at risk. UNIRA has to plan for this before it starts.

## How long until everyone has good care?

Now return to the world. The UNIRA research built a model of world training. It asks a simple question: how many years until every region reaches the EU level of 131 health workers per 10,000 people?

The answer depends above all on one thing: how many new health workers the world's schools can train each year. Since no one knows that number exactly, the model tries three levels: low, central and high. Each scenario adds one more step.

| Scenario | Years until everyone has EU-level care (low / central / high) |
| --- | --- |
| Training as today | never / never / never |
| Training grows 7% a year for 15 years (up to 3× today) | never / 37 / 27 |
| + a quarter of the world's soldiers volunteer for health training | never / 36 / 25 |
| + healthier lives (central prevention, −3% need) | never / 34 / 24 |
| **Ambitious:** training grows 10% a year, half of soldiers volunteer, strong prevention | **42 / 23 / 18** |

Three lessons stand out.

First, **if the world keeps training as it does today, it never gets there.** Population grows, and today's training only keeps pace.

Second, with an ambitious UNIRA programme, it takes about **18 to 42 years**, with a central estimate of **23 years**. That is roughly one generation. A child born at the start of the programme would be an adult by the time every region has good care.

Third, the biggest lever is not disarmament and not prevention. It is **training capacity itself**: the schools and the teachers who train health workers. That is the bottleneck.

The research also says what would prove this plan wrong. If world training capacity cannot grow beyond about **1.5 times** today's, EU-level care for everyone stays out of reach for more than **50 years**.

## Soldiers into carers

Rule 19 says there will be no more wars. Article 5 of the draft constitution goes further. Existing armed forces are dissolved during the transition. Their members are offered work, training and full income, "including in health, construction and civil protection".

How many people is that? The world's armed forces number about **27.4 million**. That is about **0.79%** of the world's labour force.

The model asks what happens if some of them choose health training. In one scenario, a quarter volunteer. In the ambitious one, half do. Nobody is forced: Article 7 guarantees free choice of occupation.

The effect is real, and it comes early. After **10 years**, with soldier volunteers, the world reaches about **71 health workers per 10,000 people**. Without them, it reaches about **64**.

But the effect is limited. Former soldiers still need training places, and training places are the bottleneck. In the table above, a quarter of soldiers volunteering saves only a year or two. Disarmament speeds the start; it does not replace the schools.

Those not drawn to health work are not lost either. They become available for every other kind of useful work: construction, civil protection, climate action. Chapter 12 returns to what disarmament frees.

## Healthier lives: real, but smaller than hoped

Rule 19 also makes a hopeful claim. In UNIRA, better lives, less stress and no fear of the future should prevent much illness. If people are healthier, fewer health workers would be needed.

The research tested this claim against the evidence. Here is what it found.

**The potential is large.** About **41–48%** of the world's disease burden is linked to risks that can, in principle, be changed: smoking, high blood pressure, polluted air, poor diet and others.

**Some of UNIRA's levers have clear effects.** Working very long weeks, 55 hours or more, exposes about **488 million** people. In one year it caused about **745,000** deaths from heart disease and stroke. A 30-hour week removes that exposure entirely. In low- and middle-income countries, unconditional cash transfers reduced illness within one to two years. Housing homeless people reduced emergency visits and hospital stays, although that evidence is weaker.

**But the net effect on workforce need is small.** There are four reasons:

- The 41–48% assumes risks fall to their theoretical minimum, not to a level any society can plausibly reach. UNIRA touches only part of those risks.
- More than half of all disease is linked to no known risk at all.
- Many premature deaths, which dominate preventable disease, use little care. Maternity care, child care, routine care, care for the old and rehabilitation stay the same.
- **People who live longer need care later.** One study found that lifetime health spending is *highest* for people who live healthily, because they live longest. In England, the share of older people with four or more diseases is projected to rise sharply.

There is even a twist. Where needs are unmet, more income can *raise* the use of care, because people can finally reach a clinic.

Putting it all together, the research estimates the net cut in health-worker need after 30 years at about **3%** in the central case and **14%** in the high case. In the low case it could be close to zero, or even slightly negative. Confidence is low.

| Horizon | Net cut in health-worker need (low / central / high) |
| --- | --- |
| 5 years | 0% / 2% / 6% |
| 15 years | 0% / 3% / 10% |
| 30 years | slightly negative / 3% / 14% |

In the central case, that is about **3 million** workers, against a gap of **59 million**.

So the honest verdict is this. **Healthier lives help, but they do not replace training.** The real benefit of healthier lives is not fewer nurses. It is more healthy years for everyone. That is worth reporting on its own, and it is one of UNIRA's best arguments.

## Why UNIRA does better than a market world

Could a reformed market economy, with the same people and the same technology, close the health gap just as well? The research tested this with matched resources (Chapter 13 tells the full story).

| By year 15, share of the health shortfall closed | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Reformed market | **−4%** (the gap grows) |
| Ambitious cooperative market | 4% |
| UNIRA | **38%** |

In UNIRA's central 30-year plan, every region reaches EU-level care by **year 30**. Neither market world gets there within 30 years.

The reason is not that UNIRA is cleverer. It is that UNIRA trains far more people, invites soldiers to join, invests in prevention, and sends every new health worker to the regions in greatest need. The research found that the training programme and training in-region are both needed. Either one alone closes only about **10%** of the gap by year 15.

Even a market world that directed **90%** of new capacity by need would close only **8%** of the health gap by year 15. On health, allocation alone is not enough. Scale is what matters.

## Education: the teachers behind everything

Education is the other half of the promise. Researchers put a decent education at about **9 years** of schooling, and a comfortable one at about **12**. Class size shows the gap: primary schools worldwide have about **25.7 pupils per qualified teacher**, while high-income countries have about **14.3**. Bringing every region to that level means training many more teachers. Three more things are clear from the work done.

**Teachers are the bottleneck behind the bottleneck.** Every nurse, doctor and midwife must be taught by someone. The health model found that schools and teachers set the pace of the whole plan. A world that wants more health workers must first train more teachers of health.

**The draft constitution pays for it at world level.** Article 17a lists what the One Earth Fund pays for in every region. It includes "the training of health workers and teachers", and the guarantees of Article 6 wherever a region's own means fall short. A poor region does not have to choose between its schools and its clinics.

**Learning is rewarded, not punished.** Study exempts a person from the duty to work, without loss of income (Article 7). And in UNIRA's salary scale, the multiplier rises with experience, knowledge and education (rule 12). Nobody has to give up an income to go back to school.

There is also evidence on keeping school money honest. When newspapers in Uganda published how much each school was supposed to receive, the share of school grants that never arrived fell from **87%** to under **20%**. That finding is one reason the draft constitution requires every allocation to be published in an open register.

Some questions remain unmeasured. How many years of schooling does a decent life need? How many more teachers does the world need, and where? Those are open tasks for the next round of research.

## What it asks of us

None of this happens by itself. Keeping the promise of free care and education asks four things of the world:

- a **health-worker training programme lasting 20–30 years**, in every region;
- training places, schools and teachers expanded faster than ever before;
- soldiers offered a real path into care, by choice;
- for France and other rich regions, a plan for the first years, when the 30-hour week needs more carers than exist.

It also asks patience. Food can be shared in the first year. Good health care for everyone takes a generation.

## What the evidence says

- The world has **47.4 million** health workers, about **58 per 10,000** people. **4.2 billion** people live in regions below the WHO minimum of **44.5**.
- Bringing everyone to the EU level of **131 per 10,000** needs about **59 million more** health workers, more than doubling the world total.
- Under a 30-hour week, France would need about **100,000 nurses** and **145,000 care workers** in year 1. Training takes about **3 years** for a nurse and **10 years** for a GP.
- Training as today never closes the world gap. An ambitious UNIRA programme closes it in **18–42 years**, central **23**.
- Schools and teachers are the bottleneck. Disarmament helps early (**71** vs **64** per 10,000 by year 10). Healthier lives cut need by only **3–14%** after 30 years.
- With matched resources, UNIRA closes **38%** of the health shortfall by year 15; a reformed market economy lets it grow.

## What we still don't know

- How fast the world's training capacity can really grow. The research's caps, volunteer shares and lead times are assumptions or judgements.
- The world's total number of medical graduates. No source was found; it is estimated from OECD ratios.
- How many soldiers would actually choose health work.
- The net effect of healthier lives on care needs. The direction is clear; the size is not, and longer lives add care later.
- France's reserve of qualified staff who have stopped practising. It is not reported.
- How many teachers, and how many years of schooling, a decent and a comfortable life need. These have not yet been measured.
