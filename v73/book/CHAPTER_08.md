# Chapter 8 — Health, education and care for all

*Second draft, 3 November 2026, updated with V7.9. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.3, V6.5–V6.8, V7.9, Working Paper 2 and the draft constitution) and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

It is early morning at a small health post in a rural district. The queue started before dawn. A mother holds a feverish child. An old man waits with a bandaged foot. A young woman is pregnant for the first time and has walked a long way.

There is one nurse. They know most of the people in the queue by name. They will see as many as they can before dark, and some will be told to come back tomorrow.

Far away, in another country, a soldier sits in a classroom. Their unit has been dissolved. They are not learning a new weapon. They are learning how to take blood pressure, clean a wound and spot the signs of a difficult birth. In a few years, they could be the second nurse at a health post like this one.

Both scenes are imagined. They are composites, not reports. But they hold the two ideas of this chapter.

The first is that the world's deepest shortage is not food or energy. It is trained people who care for others. The second is that the shortage can be closed, slowly, if the world decides to train them, and if it stops spending people on war.

## A promise written into the rules

UNIRA makes a simple promise. **Health care and education are free, for everyone, throughout life.**

The promise first appeared in the design rules. Rule 15, on retirees, ends with a short sentence: health care and education are free. The draft constitution turns it into a right. Article 6 lists what every person is guaranteed. Among them is "health care and education, free of charge, throughout life".

The constitution then protects that promise in three ways:

- **Article 8** says rights in Article 6 are never withdrawn as a punishment.
- **Article 9** says no law, allocation decision, sanction or emergency may reduce them.
- **Article 7** says study, like illness and caring for others, exempts a person from the duty to work, without loss of income or rights.

And the money is there. Article 17a lists what the One Earth Fund pays for in every region. It includes "the training of health workers and teachers". It also covers the guarantees of Article 6 wherever a region's own means fall short.

A promise on paper is easy. Keeping it is harder. To keep it, the world needs people: nurses, doctors, midwives, care workers and teachers. This chapter asks whether there can be enough of them, and how long it would take.

## The world's real shortage

Chapter 1 showed that the world already produces enough food for everyone, and more than enough energy for a decent life. Health care is different.

Today the world has about **47.4 million** doctors, nurses and midwives. That is about **58 per 10,000 people**.

The World Health Organization sets a minimum: about **44.5 health workers per 10,000 people**. On a world average, we are above it. The world has **1.32 times** what the minimum requires.

But that average misleads. Nurses and doctors cannot be shipped like grain. And UNIRA forbids forced relocation, so nobody can be ordered to move to another region. What matters is where health workers live. Today about **4.2 billion people** live in regions below the WHO minimum. In those regions, about **7.7 million** workers are missing.

UNIRA aims higher than the minimum. Its goal is a *comfortable* level of care, like the European Union's: about **131 health workers per 10,000 people**. Measured against that goal, the world has less than half of what it needs: **0.45 times**. About **7.3 billion people** live in regions below it.

| Level of care | Health workers per 10,000 | World supply ÷ need | People in regions below |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Today's world average | 58 | — | — |
| Decent (WHO minimum) | 44.5 | 1.32 | 4.2 billion |
| Comfortable (EU level) | 131 | **0.45** | 7.3 billion |

To bring everyone to the EU level, the world needs about **59 million more** health workers. That more than doubles today's total. By 2050, the world's population will reach about **9.66 billion**, so total need rises to about **107 million**.

This is the longest lead time in the whole UNIRA plan. Food can be moved within a year. A nurse takes years to train.

### A closer look: what these numbers measure

It helps to know exactly what is being counted.

**Who counts.** The world figure of 47.4 million covers doctors, nurses and midwives. It comes from the World Health Organization's health workforce data. Care workers are not included in it.

**What the WHO minimum means.** The figure of 44.5 is tied to 12 health indicators of the Sustainable Development Goals, at a low level of achievement. It is a shortage line, not a comfortable level. EU and OECD densities are about **three times** higher.

**Where 131 comes from.** The EU level was worked out by the research from Eurostat counts of doctors, nurses and midwives. It is a reference point, not a scientific finding about what people need. Choosing it is a design decision UNIRA makes openly.

**Why averages hide the problem.** The research counts people in regions below each level. A region's average can hide large gaps inside it, between city and countryside, or rich and poor. So the true number of people without good care is probably larger.

## For rich regions: build up, don't take away

Under strict equal shares, a rich region would simply hand over part of what it has. For food and energy, Chapter 1 showed that rich regions would still stay comfortable.

For health, equal shares would be a disaster. France has about **134** health workers per 10,000 people. Under equal shares it would fall to about **59**. That is still above the WHO minimum, but far below what French people have today.

So the UNIRA research draws a clear conclusion. **For health, "equal share" must mean building up the South, not taking from the North.** Nobody's hospital is emptied. New health workers are trained in the regions that need them. Article 10 of the draft constitution says it directly. The new capacity directed to regions by need includes "health workers trained in-region".

## First, a problem at home

Before looking at the world, look at one rich country on day one.

Chapter 3 showed that France can keep today's output under a 30-hour week, if almost everyone who can work does work. Care is the exception. In hospitals and care homes, much of the work is *being there*. Rested staff make fewer mistakes, but they cannot be in two places at once. In care, only **0–15%** of lost hours come back through higher productivity.

So a 30-hour week means more people. France's health and social work sector would need about **16.9%** more staff. In round numbers:

| Occupation | Working today | Extra needed for 30 hours | Training time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nurses | about 608,000 | about **100,000** | **3 years** |
| Doctors (upper bound) | about 266,000 | about 45,000 | **10 years** for a GP |
| Care workers | about 860,000 | about **145,000** | about **11 months** |

No training system can respond within the first year. That is true whatever happens to the rest of the economy.

### A closer look: how long training really takes

The research checked each training time against official French sources.

- **Nurses.** Training lasts **3 years**, six semesters. From September 2026 it includes **4,620 hours**, up from 4,200.
- **General practitioners.** Six years of medical school, then four years of specialist training. The fourth year was added for doctors in training who started in 2023. That makes **10 years** in all.
- **Care assistants.** The course is **1,540 hours**: 770 hours of theory and 770 hours of placement. That is about **44 weeks**, or **11 months**.

The numbers are tight even today. France trains about **24,800** nurses a year, while about **20,300** leave each year. Job vacancies in health and social work already run at **2.9–3.8%**.

What if France doubled its training intake? The nurse gap would close in about **8 years**. The doctor gap would close in about **16**.

### What can be done meanwhile?

The research lists options. Choosing among them is a democratic decision, not a modelling choice:

- care-assistant courses for the many new workers who enter work under UNIRA;
- the voluntary return of qualified staff who have left;
- help from other regions, under the One Earth framework;
- redesigning tasks, so that nurses and doctors do what only they can do.

If the 30-hour rule applied to care from the very first day, without these measures, the right to care would be at risk. UNIRA has to plan for this before it starts.

### An imagined ward

Picture a hospital ward in a French town, in the first year of UNIRA. Before, its nurses worked long shifts. Now each works 30 hours a week. Someone has to cover the missing hours.

Three new care assistants have just finished their eleven-month course. They were not working a year ago. They take on washing, meals and turning patients. That frees the nurses for medicines, wounds and watching for danger signs. Two retired nurses have come back part-time. The ward is still short. But it is less short than it would have been.

This ward is imagined. It shows how the options above might combine. It does not show that they will be enough.

## How long until everyone has good care?

Now return to the world. The UNIRA research built a model of world training. It asks a simple question. How many years until every region reaches the EU level of 131 health workers per 10,000 people?

The answer depends above all on one thing: how many new health workers the world's schools can train each year. Nobody knows that number exactly. So the model tries three levels: low, central and high. Each scenario adds one more step.

| Scenario | Years until everyone has EU-level care (low / central / high) |
| --- | --- |
| Training as today | never / never / never |
| Training grows 7% a year for 15 years (up to 3× today) | never / 37 / 27 |
| + a quarter of the world's soldiers volunteer for health training | never / 36 / 25 |
| + healthier lives (central prevention, −3% need) | never / 34 / 24 |
| **Ambitious:** training grows 10% a year, half of soldiers volunteer, strong prevention | **42 / 23 / 18** |

Three lessons stand out.

First, **if the world keeps training as it does today, it never gets there.** Population grows, and today's training only keeps pace.

Second, with an ambitious UNIRA programme, it takes about **18 to 42 years**, with a central estimate of **23 years**. That is roughly one generation. A child born at the start of the programme would be an adult by the time every region has good care.

Third, the biggest lever is not disarmament and not prevention. It is **training capacity itself**: the schools and the teachers who train health workers. That is the bottleneck.

### A closer look: how the training model works

The model's low, central and high cases differ in one key number. It is how many new workers the world can train each year, as a share of today's workforce: **3%**, **4.5%** or **6%**.

Why not simply count the world's graduates? Because nobody publishes that total for doctors. The World Health Organization reports about **25.3** new nursing graduates per 100,000 people a year. But it gives no world total for medical graduates. So the research estimated it from OECD ratios, and labels it an estimate.

The model then lets training grow, by 7% or 10% a year. It caps growth at three times today's capacity. Each year, new workers go first to the regions furthest below 131 per 10,000.

The research also says what would prove this plan wrong. If world training capacity cannot grow beyond about **1.5 times** today's, EU-level care for everyone stays out of reach for more than **50 years**.

## Who needs care most? Age, and illness too

"Furthest below" sounds simple. But a region's need for care depends on more than its headcount. Older people need more care. So the first version of the allocation formula counted people aged 65 and over **three times**.

The research then checked what that rule would do, using the World Health Organization's figures on healthy life expectancy. It found a hidden unfairness.

Counting by age alone sends care towards old, rich regions. Sub-Saharan Africa is young. But its people lose many more years of healthy life to disease, long before old age. An age-only rule does not see that. In fact, it gives the region a *smaller* share of new health workers than no weighting at all.

| Health need counted by | Sub-Saharan Africa's share of new health workers | Sub-Saharan Africa, health workers per 10,000 by year 30 | Europe and Central Asia, per 10,000 by year 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| People only, no weighting | 33% | 131 | 141 |
| Age alone (65 and over count 3×) | 30% | **116** | **151** |
| **Age and burden of disease, in equal parts** | **35%** | **142** | **141** |

Under the age-only rule, Africa south of the Sahara would end up far below Europe. Weighting by age *and* by healthy years of life lost to disease brings the two to the same level. And every region still reaches decent care by about year 7.

The author accepted this. Article 10a.3 now says that health need is weighted by age and burden of disease together, in equal parts, and that **age alone is never used**.

### An imagined pair of clinics

Imagine two district clinics. One is in a mountain town in Europe, where many patients are over seventy. The other is in a fast-growing town in West Africa, where most patients are young mothers and small children, and many are ill.

Under an age-only rule, the European clinic would look needier. Under UNIRA's rule, both count for what they are: one region with many older people, one with a heavy load of illness. Both receive new nurses.

The clinics are imagined. The weights are Article 10a.

## Soldiers into carers

Rule 19 says there will be no more wars. Article 5 of the draft constitution goes further. Existing armed forces are dissolved during the transition. Their members are offered work, training and full income, "including in health, construction and civil protection".

How many people is that? The world's armed forces number about **27.4 million**. That is about **0.79%** of the world's labour force.

The model asks what happens if some of them choose health training. In one scenario, a quarter volunteer. In the ambitious one, half do. Nobody is forced: Article 7 guarantees free choice of occupation.

The effect is real, and it comes early. After **10 years**, with soldier volunteers, the world reaches about **71 health workers per 10,000 people**. Without them, it reaches about **64**.

But the effect is limited. Former soldiers still need training places, and training places are the bottleneck. In the table above, a quarter of soldiers volunteering saves only a year or two. Disarmament speeds the start; it does not replace the schools.

Those not drawn to health work are not lost either. They become available for every other kind of useful work: construction, civil protection, climate action. Chapter 12 returns to what disarmament frees.

## Healthier lives: real, but smaller than hoped

Rule 19 also makes a hopeful claim. In UNIRA, better lives, less stress and no fear of the future should prevent much illness. If people are healthier, fewer health workers would be needed.

The research tested this claim against the evidence. Here is what it found.

### The potential is large

About **41–48%** of the world's disease burden is linked to risks that can, in principle, be changed. The largest single risks in 2021 were polluted air, at **8.0%** of the burden, high blood pressure at **7.8%**, and smoking at **5.7%**.

### Some of UNIRA's levers have clear effects

| UNIRA lever | What the evidence shows | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30-hour week | Very long weeks (55 hours or more) expose about **488 million** people. In 2016 they caused about **745,000** deaths from heart disease and stroke. | Strong; a 30-hour week removes the exposure |
| Secure income | Unconditional cash transfers in poorer countries cut illness by about a fifth (risk ratio **0.79**) within 1–2 years | Moderate certainty |
| Housing for all | Housing homeless people cut emergency visits (**0.63**) and hospital stays (**0.76**) | Weak: 4 trials, all at high risk of bias |
| No war | Conflicts killed **139,000–285,000** people a year directly in 2021–2025. In conflict-affected places, **22.1%** of people have a mental disorder at any time | Strong for the harm; benefit hard to size |
| Less job strain | Only **3.4%** of heart disease is attributable to job strain | Small |

### But the net effect on workforce need is small

There are four reasons.

- The 41–48% assumes risks fall to their theoretical minimum, not to a level any society can plausibly reach. UNIRA touches only part of those risks.
- More than half of all disease is linked to no known risk at all.
- Many premature deaths, which dominate preventable disease, use little care. Maternity care, child care, routine care, care for the old and rehabilitation stay the same.
- **People who live longer need care later.** One study found that lifetime health spending is *highest* for people who live healthily, because they live longest. In England, the share of people over 65 with four or more diseases is projected to rise from **9.8%** to **17.0%** between 2015 and 2035.

There is even a twist. Where needs are unmet, more income can *raise* the use of care, because people can finally reach a clinic. In the same cash-transfer studies, use of health services rose slightly (risk ratio **1.04**).

### Timing: some gains come fast, others take decades

- **Heart disease responds quickly.** Within 5 years of quitting, heavy smokers already have much lower risk than those who continue.
- **Cash transfers** reduced illness within one to two years.
- **Whole populations change slowly.** In Finland, deaths from coronary disease fell **80%** over 35 years.
- **Cancer takes decades.** Stopping smoking at 30 brings the risk of lung cancer by age 75 down to **2%**, against **10%** for stopping at 60.

### A closer look: how the research put it together

The research combined these findings into one estimate. It multiplied four things, in plain words:
1. the share of disease linked to changeable risks (central **45%**);
2. the share of that UNIRA could actually remove (central **25%**, a judgement);
3. how much less disease reduces the need for health workers (central **0.5**, a judgement);
4. a discount for longer lives that need care later (at 30 years, central **0.6**, so only 40% of the gain remains).

A small amount for peace was then added (central **0.5%**).

Here is the central case at 30 years, step by step:
- 45% × 25% × 0.5 ≈ **5.6%**;
- after the longevity discount, 5.6% × 0.4 ≈ **2.25%**;
- adding peace, 2.25% + 0.5% ≈ **2.75%**, rounded to about **3%**.

The high case, with bolder judgements, gives about **14%**. The low case gives about zero, or slightly less.

| Horizon | Net cut in health-worker need (low / central / high) |
| --- | --- |
| 5 years | 0% / 2% / 6% |
| 15 years | 0% / 3% / 10% |
| 30 years | slightly negative / 3% / 14% |

In the central case, that is about **3 million** workers, against a gap of **59 million**. Confidence is low.

So the honest verdict is this. **Healthier lives help, but they do not replace training.** The real benefit of healthier lives is not fewer nurses. It is more healthy years for everyone. That is worth reporting on its own, and it is one of UNIRA's best arguments.

## Why UNIRA does better than a market world

Could a reformed market economy, with the same people and the same technology, close the health gap just as well? The research tested this with matched resources. Chapter 13 tells the full story.

| By year 15, share of the health shortfall closed | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Reformed market | **−4%** (the gap grows) |
| Ambitious cooperative market | 4% |
| UNIRA | **38%** |

The reason is not that UNIRA is cleverer. It trains far more people, invites soldiers to join, invests in prevention, and sends every new health worker to the regions in greatest need. The training programme and training in-region are both needed. Either one alone closes only about **10%** of the gap by year 15. On health, allocation alone is not enough. Scale is what matters.

## Education: the teachers behind everything

Education is the other half of the promise.

### How much schooling is enough?

Researchers who study decent living put a decent education at about **9 years** of schooling. A comfortable one is about **12**.

Class size shows the gap. Primary schools worldwide have about **25.7 pupils per teacher**. High-income countries have about **14.3**.

Simple arithmetic shows what closing that gap means. Going from 25.7 to 14.3 pupils per teacher needs about **1.8 times** as many teachers for the same number of children. That is before counting population growth, or extra years of schooling.

### Teachers are the bottleneck behind the bottleneck

Every nurse, doctor and midwife must be taught by someone. The health model found that schools and teachers set the pace of the whole plan. A world that wants more health workers must first train more teachers of health.

### Who pays

The draft constitution pays for it at world level. The One Earth Fund pays for "the training of health workers and teachers", in every region. A poor region does not have to choose between its schools and its clinics.

### Learning is rewarded, not punished

Study exempts a person from the duty to work, without loss of income (Article 7). And in UNIRA's salary scale, the multiplier rises with experience, knowledge and education (rule 12). Nobody has to give up an income to go back to school.

### Keeping school money honest

There is also evidence on making sure school money arrives. In Uganda in the early 1990s, most school grants never reached the schools. Schools received only about **13%** of the grants. After newspapers published how much each school should receive, the share captured along the way fell from about **80%** to under **20%** (Reinikka & Svensson). That finding is one reason the draft constitution requires every allocation to be published in an open register (Chapter 14).

Some questions remain unmeasured. How many more teachers does the world need, and where? Those are open tasks for the next round of research.

## Questions a fair reader might ask

**"Won't free health care be overused?"**
In the research, the need for health workers is set by a service standard, 131 per 10,000 people. It is not set by how much people ask for. The evidence does show that more income can raise use of care where needs were unmet. That is a sign of care reaching people, not of waste.

**"Why not recruit nurses from abroad, as rich countries do today?"**
Because it moves the shortage instead of closing it. The research's rule for health is to train new workers in the regions that need them. The WHO itself traces its projected shortfall of **11.1 million** health workers by 2030 to training, distribution and migration.

**"Isn't 23 years far too slow?"**
It is slow, and the research says so. But training as today never closes the gap at all. A generation is the honest timescale for training tens of millions of people.

**"Is the EU level of care really necessary everywhere?"**
It is a reference, not a law of nature. The research chose it as "comfortable", and says plainly that choosing a level is a design decision. A lower target would be reached sooner.

**"What about care for old people, which is growing everywhere?"**
This is one of the hardest parts of the plan. Longer lives mean more years with several illnesses. In England, the share of older people with four or more diseases is projected almost to double in twenty years. Care for the old does not shrink with healthier lives. In France alone, the 30-hour week needs about **145,000** more care workers. The world model counts doctors, nurses and midwives, not care workers, so the full need for care at world scale is not yet measured.

**"Will soldiers really want to become nurses?"**
Nobody knows. Article 7 forbids forcing anyone into a job. The model tests a quarter and a half of soldiers volunteering. Both are assumptions.

## What it asks of us

None of this happens by itself. Keeping the promise of free care and education asks four things of the world:

- a **health-worker training programme lasting 20–30 years**, in every region;
- training places, schools and teachers expanded faster than ever before;
- soldiers offered a real path into care, by choice;
- for France and other rich regions, a plan for the first years, when the 30-hour week needs more carers than exist.

It also asks patience. Food can be shared in the first year. Good health care for everyone takes a generation.

## What the evidence says

- The world has **47.4 million** doctors, nurses and midwives, about **58 per 10,000** people. **4.2 billion** people live in regions below the WHO minimum of **44.5**.
- Bringing everyone to the EU level of **131 per 10,000** needs about **59 million more** health workers, more than doubling the world total.
- Under a 30-hour week, France would need about **100,000 nurses** and **145,000 care workers** in year 1. Training takes about **3 years** for a nurse, **10 years** for a GP and **11 months** for a care assistant.
- Training as today never closes the world gap. An ambitious UNIRA programme closes it in **18–42 years**, central **23**.
- Schools and teachers are the bottleneck. Disarmament helps early (**71** vs **64** per 10,000 by year 10). Healthier lives cut need by only about **3%** (central) to **14%** (high) after 30 years.
- With matched resources, UNIRA closes **38%** of the health shortfall by year 15; a reformed market economy lets it grow.
- Weighting health need by age alone would leave Sub-Saharan Africa at **116** health workers per 10,000 by year 30, against **151** in Europe and Central Asia. Weighting by age and burden of disease together gives **142** against **141**. Age alone is never used (Article 10a.3).
- Bringing primary classes to high-income levels means about **1.8 times** as many teachers per pupil.

## What we still don't know

- How fast the world's training capacity can really grow. The research's caps, volunteer shares and lead times are assumptions or judgements.
- The world's total number of medical graduates. No source was found; it is estimated from OECD ratios.
- How many soldiers would actually choose health work.
- The net effect of healthier lives on care needs. The direction is clear; the size is not, and longer lives add care later.
- France's reserve of qualified staff who have stopped practising. It is not reported.
- How many teachers the world needs, and where. This has not yet been measured.
- The equal split between age and disease burden is a judgement. Healthy life expectancy is a broad measure, given here for seven large world regions, not for each region of One Earth.
