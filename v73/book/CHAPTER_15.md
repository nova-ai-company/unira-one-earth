# Chapter 15 — The transition

*Second draft, 3 November 2026. Every figure comes from the UNIRA research (V6.4, V7.2, V7.4, V7.5 and V7.9) and the draft Constitution of One Earth, and is listed in the notes at the end of the chapter.*

---

Imagine the evening before a referendum, in a kitchen anywhere in a rich region.

The grandmother worries about her savings. She worked all her life for them, and she wants to know what happens to them. Her son worries about the mortgage. Her granddaughter, who works nights in a warehouse, asks a different question. Would she finally have a home of her own, and evenings free?

The family is imagined, a composite of many households. Their questions are real, and every region would ask them. Who gains? What happens to my money, my house, my debts? And what if it goes wrong, as other great changes have?

This chapter answers with the research. It starts with who gains, then looks at what history teaches, then sets out the eight rules for getting there. It ends where it began, in the kitchen.

## Who gains by joining

The research compared each region's situation after 30 years, inside UNIRA or in a reformed market world. It looked at three things people need: energy, health workers and floor space at home. It combined them into one measure of material gain or loss.

The result is clear. **146 regions, home to 86% of humanity, gain materially by joining**, compared with a reformed market world after 30 years. In 33 regions, home to 14%, the *average* falls a little on the same comparison. Neither number compares with today.

But averages hide people. Inside those 33 regions, about **495 million** people, mostly the poor and the lower middle, are better off even at the lowest UNIRA income (see below). Counted person by person, **about 92% of humanity is better off.**

| Region | Energy | Health workers | Floor space | Overall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ethiopia | 4.2× | 2.5× | 1.9× | **+170%** |
| Nigeria | 3.4× | 2.3× | 1.6× | **+136%** |
| Pakistan | 3.0× | 2.3× | 1.5× | **+116%** |
| India | 1.8× | 1.8× | 1.5× | **+72%** |
| Indonesia | 1.5× | 1.8× | 1.3× | **+54%** |
| Brazil | about the same | 1.3× | about the same | **+11%** |
| China | 0.82× | 1.3× | the same | **+2%** |
| France | 0.83× | the same | the same | **−6%** |
| Germany | 0.78× | the same | the same | **−8%** |
| Russia | 0.68× | about the same | the same | **−12%** |
| Japan | 0.69× | the same | the same | **−12%** |

Look at the columns, not just the last one. In the poorest regions, everything grows. An Ethiopian family would have four times the energy, two and a half times the health workers, and nearly twice the space.

China is the interesting middle case. It would use less energy than in a market world, but have about a third more health workers. The two roughly cancel out.

## A closer look: how gain and loss were measured

Two choices shape this table, and both are worth knowing.

**The comparison is with a market future, not with today.** Each column compares a region in year 30 under UNIRA with the same region in year 30 in a reformed market world. That world is World B from Chapter 13. So "France −6%" does not mean French people would have 6% less than today. It means 6% less than France would have in 30 years under a good market.

**The three needs are combined as ratios.** For each need, the research divides the UNIRA amount by the market amount. Then it averages the three, on a scale where doubling and halving count equally. That is what "mean log ratio" means in the notes.

Here is the French case, worked through. Energy is 0.83 of the market amount. Health workers and floor space are unchanged, so their ratios are 1. Averaging the three on that scale gives a result of about **−6%**. In plain words, a cut of about a sixth in one need out of three counts as a loss of about a sixteenth overall.

This measure has limits. It counts only energy, health workers and floor space. It leaves out time, peace, security, food, clean air and much else. Those things matter to voters. They are not in the number.

## What rich regions give up

Now look at the rich regions. Only one column moves: energy. Their health care is unchanged. Their homes are unchanged.

So the loss of **6 to 12%** is a loss in energy use only. A French household would use about a sixth less energy than in a market world. An American one would use about a third less. Both stay at or above the comfortable level of Chapter 10.

The largest loss in the model is about **12%**. A group of the richest regions all sit near it:

| Regions | Loss on the material measure |
| --- | --- |
| Luxembourg, Iceland, Belgium, Australia, Finland, Sweden, Netherlands, Norway, Austria, United States | about **12.2%** |
| Malta, Canada | about 12.1–12.2% |
| South Korea | about 12.0% |
| Japan, Russia | about 11.6–11.7% |

Why do so many sit at almost the same number? The model gives them nearly identical results. Each keeps its health care and homes, so only energy changes. And each keeps a comfortable level of energy.

For these regions, joining is a choice, not a bargain. It is worth it if peace, security, a 30-hour week and a stable climate count for more than some energy. No model can decide that. Only their people can, by vote.

## Inside rich regions, many are better off

National averages hide who wins and who loses. So the research looked inside rich regions, at each tenth of the population, from poorest to richest.

It started from the lowest possible UNIRA income: a worker on the base salary of **5,000 U** a month who saves the maximum, half of it. That leaves **2,500 U** a month to spend. At UNIRA's fixed prices, that buys about **86%** of what an average French person consumes today.

The line falls right in the middle. The poorest half of France lives today below the level of the lowest UNIRA income.

Across these 31 regions, **44% of the people, about 495 million, are better off even at the lowest UNIRA income.** The poorest tenth gains between **+74%** and **+446%** everywhere. In the United States, about a quarter of the people are better off at the very lowest income, and the poorest tenth gains about **+154%**. In Japan more than half are better off, and the poorest tenth gains about **+319%**. Nobody anywhere falls below this basket.

But the lowest salary is only the floor. Most people will earn more: under UNIRA, pay rises with experience, knowledge and the complexity of the work, from 1 to 5 times the base salary. In the research's money model the average is about 1.9 times. Here is the share of people who consume less today than each UNIRA salary would buy, even while saving half of it:

At the average UNIRA salary, about **three in four** people in the rich regions would consume more than they do today. Free health care, education and a guaranteed home come on top.

Why not everyone? Because in some regions a small group consumes far more than even the highest UNIRA salary allows. In the United States, about the richest tenth consumes more than twice the national average. The data come in tenths, so the very richest, the top 1%, cannot be separated, and they consume more still. These are the people with several homes, large fortunes and private jets. UNIRA limits them to two homes, a money cap and energy and travel paid per unit. They keep a comfortable life, but with less than today. Saying "everyone gains" would be untrue, and this book does not say it.

And this is the floor, not the average. Many people will earn above the base salary. Free health care, free education and guaranteed housing come on top, and they are not counted here. Fixed prices also end household price shocks like the energy crisis of 2022.

So even in the regions whose average falls, a large share of the voters gains, and everyone keeps a comfortable life. The granddaughter in the warehouse is one of them.

One caution belongs here. These comparisons use income shares for each country (2019–2024) and apply them to household consumption. In the United States, households pay a large part of their health care out of that consumption, which UNIRA provides free, so the gains there are understated. They measure spending power, not wealth. Someone with low income but large savings would be counted among the poor. The savings rules later in this chapter matter for them.

## A union of the willing still works

What if the rich regions say no? The research tested a union of only the 146 regions that gain.

That union holds **86%** of the world's people. But it holds only **62%** of the world's energy use and **65%** of its health workers. It must do more with less.

| Share of the gap closed | Whole world joins | Only the gainers join | Very cooperative market |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Energy, by year 15 | 52% | **20%** | 14% |
| Health workers, by year 15 | 34% | **15%** | 4% |
| Homes, by year 30 | 40% | **25%** | 10% |

The "whole world" column already includes strong safeguards against error and leakage, from Chapter 14. That is why it shows 52% and not the ideal 60%.

The union of gainers works at about **half speed**. Even so, it beats the most cooperative market world on all three needs.

This is perhaps the most important result for the transition. UNIRA does not need everyone on day one. It can start with the willing, show results, and grow.

## Equal rights from the first day

The research found a hard fact. If every person on Earth were paid at least 5,000 U from the first day, people would try to buy about **twice** what the world produces. With fixed prices, that gap would become empty shelves.

So the transition rests on three parts, in the author's words:
- **D, real access,** decides what must be delivered. From day one, everyone has the right to suitable housing, health care, nutritious food, education and essential energy, whatever their salary. One Earth first counts what exists, redistributes it, repairs and renovates it, and only then produces what is missing.
- **Q, fair shares,** decides how scarce goods are divided. Goods that are genuinely scarce and can be moved (food, medicines, clothes, equipment) are shared by needs-based quotas, adjusted for household size, age, climate and health. A quota right can never be sold. Quotas relax and end as soon as supply is sufficient: UNIRA is not meant to ration forever.
- **C, money,** decides how purchasing power grows. About three-quarters of what people buy is local services (shops, restaurants, transport, care), and those must be built where people live. So the part of salaries that pays for local services rises as each region's services grow, and salaries are never cut.

Success is measured by how people actually live, not by the money in their accounts. The constitution states the principle plainly: *every person has an equal right to a dignified and comfortable standard of living; scarce resources are allocated according to legitimate needs and sustainable availability; and money reflects the economy's real capacity to deliver.*

The research compared this hybrid with two alternatives in the same simulated world. Converging salaries alone left the poorer half of humanity at about 19% of the full level at first. Equal salaries for everyone from day one left most of their money unspendable, an invitation to black markets. The hybrid gave the poorer half about 25% at once, about a quarter more, and brought them to 90% several years sooner. It held up when the share of shippable goods changed, when some goods were diverted, and when shipping was limited.

An idea that was tested and set aside is also worth telling. An "equality credit" would have saved part of the salary gap for later. It did not improve real lives; it only moved promises into the future. UNIRA chose real investment in the regions furthest behind instead: training, care, infrastructure and equipment.

None of the dates is a promise. The constitution keeps the numbers (salary amounts, reserves, shipping limits, the expected time to convergence) outside its permanent articles, as research values to be set by law and corrected as real data arrive.

## What history teaches

Large transitions have been tried before. The research reviewed **61** findings on how they went. **51** of them were verified in fetched text. The other 10 are not used as facts. Chapter 4 told the stories of German reunification, the European Union and debt relief in detail. Here are the lessons that shape the transition rules, with a few new ones.

**Converting everything at once can destroy an economy.** On 1 July 1990, East Germany adopted the West German mark. Wages and prices were converted one to one, at a political rate. Within months, industrial output fell to about **half** its 1989 level. Only **8%** of workers were in firms that could still cover their costs. More than **3 million** jobs, about a third, disappeared.

**Selling everything off fast can fail too.** The Treuhand agency took over about **8,500** firms employing more than **4 million** people. It laid off **2.5 million**. It closed with a deficit of about **DM 256 billion**, against about DM 67 billion in revenue.

**Giving old property back can freeze investment.** Germany chose "restitution before compensation" for property seized in the past. Claims tied up titles and slowed rebuilding.

**Compensation eaten by inflation is confiscation in disguise.** Japan's land reform after 1947 cut tenant farmers from **43.5%** to **11.8%** of farmers. But landlords were paid under 1,000 yen per unit of land worth about 20,000 by 1950. Inflation did the rest. Taiwan did better. It paid landlords **70%** in land bonds and **30%** in shares of state firms, steering them into industry.

**Land reform can raise output.** South Korea's 1950 reform bought land above **3 hectares**, and land of absentee owners, with bills paid over **5 years**. Counties with 10 points more tenancy saw rice-yield growth 5 points higher afterwards. But Korean bills, too, were eroded by wartime inflation.

**Wealth taxes leak when the wealthy can leave.** France's wealth tax raised **€4.42 billion** in 2007, about 1.5% of tax receipts. In 2006 alone, **843** taxpayers left because of it, a net **€2.8 billion**. In Norway, at least **30** very rich people left in 2022, more than in the previous 13 years combined. Those who moved to Switzerland held **NOK 29 billion**. The research draws a careful conclusion. The losses came from a few hundred very wealthy households moving, not from mass flight.

**Wealth is highly concentrated.** Worldwide, the richest 10% hold **75%** of wealth. The poorest half hold **2%**. The richest 0.001% hold more than **6%**. A money cap touches very few people deeply. But those few hold a great deal.

The encouraging lessons are just as clear.

**Voluntary, staged joining works.** The European Union grew from **6** members to **27** over decades, by choice and with clear entry rules. Norway voted no twice, and that was respected.

**Modest transfers bring large catch-up.** Donor countries paid only **0.3–0.4%** of their output. Poorer members received about **2–4%** of theirs. The 2004 entrants rose from **60%** to **80%** of the EU average. Poland went from **44%** in 1995 to **81%** in 2025.

**Small savings can be protected.** Germany converted savings one to one up to **4,000 marks** for adults, **2,000** for children and **6,000** for people over 60. Larger sums converted at two to one.

**Cancelling debt frees money for people.** Debt relief for the poorest countries cost about **USD 119.5 billion**. Afterwards, they spent **7.3%** of output on fighting poverty, against **1.9%** on debt.

**Mortgages can be converted without collapse.** Iceland's 2014 plan was capped at **ISK 4 million** per household and cut the average mortgage by about 20%. It reached **56,000** households, and **2,500** of them moved back above water. Hungary converted the foreign-currency loans of more than **400,000** borrowers. It locked the rate just before the Swiss franc shock of January 2015.

**People judge by the shop, not the statistics.** The euro added only **0.12–0.29 points** to measured inflation in 2002. But people *felt* prices rise, because cafés and groceries went up. The research draws three practical lessons: publish prices in both currencies, ban rounding up, and watch everyday items closely. Fixed prices for essentials remove much of this fear.

## The eight transition rules

From these lessons the research drafted eight rules. The author has made the hardest decisions. The constitution turns them into its transitional provisions.

| Rule | In one line |
| --- | --- |
| T1 | Join by referendum, in three stages |
| T2 | No single political exchange rate |
| T3 | Savings keep their buying power; the money cap phases in over 10 years |
| T4 | Homes are never seized; mortgages become zero-interest credit |
| T5 | Enterprises: compensation, never restitution |
| T6 | No public debts, with savers protected |
| T7 | A right to leave, for regions and for people |
| T8 | Protect production during the change |

**T1. Staged joining.** A region votes to join. As an *observer*, it shares data and builds the safeguards of Chapter 14. As an *associate*, it receives needs-based transfers and adopts the 30-hour week. As a *full member*, it adopts the U and all the rules. Each stage has published entry criteria.

Why build the safeguards first? Because Chapter 14 showed they are worth about as much as the allocation rule itself. A region should not receive needs-based transfers before it can show where they go. Transitional provision T-7 makes this a rule for One Earth as a whole: the Court, the Audit Authority, the Inspector-General of the Force, the Democracy Monitor and the Citizens' Council must exist before the first U is issued, the first allocation is made or the first Force unit is formed.

**T2. No political exchange rate.** This is the lesson of 1990. At full membership, wages move directly onto the UNIRA scale: **5,000 U** a month, times a multiplier up to five. Prices of essentials are set from the work and resources they contain, the same everywhere. Living standards come from what is allocated by need, not from a rate someone invented.

**T3. Savings and the money cap.** Savings convert so that they buy the same basket of food, housing and energy as before. The research calls this "basket parity". Savings up to the cap stay with their owner in full, and money above it first fills the protected home, retirement and time accounts of Chapter 6. This answers the grandmother: an ordinary lifetime of saving is kept.

Savings above the cap go **directly to One Earth**, not into bonds. That was the author's decision. The money returns as homes, energy and care in the regions that need them.

The cap does not arrive overnight. It starts high and falls each year for **10 years**, to its steady level, never below **1.5 years** of base salary (**90,000 U**). Chapter 6 explains why a cap is needed at all. Without it, a 60-year simulation showed unspent money piling up to **152 months** of consumption, and still rising. With it, the pile stayed stable at about **12 months**.

To stop wealth from fleeing before the vote, an **anti-flight rule** applies. The cap counts wealth held on the day the region votes to join. Money sent abroad after that day still counts as held.

**T4. Homes.** Homes are not money, and they are never taken. The two-home limit applies to new purchases. Extra homes may be sold to the regional pool at the fixed price within about **10 years**, or passed on to heirs. Mortgages become **zero-interest** credit in U, repaid from salary. This answers the son.

**T5. Enterprises.** Firms become cooperative or public enterprises. Owners' pay becomes a salary, capped at five times the base. Surplus goes to the region. Owners receive their share in U under the same cap rules. Old property claims are settled by compensation, never by handing property back.

**T6. No public debts.** At full membership, all public debts of member regions are cancelled. Inside one world entity, a debt between regions would be One Earth owing money to itself.

Ordinary savers are protected. Government bonds held by households, pension funds and banks on savers' behalf count as those people's savings. They are kept up to the cap. Pension promises are replaced by UNIRA's needs-based retirement income.

Debts to creditors outside UNIRA are paid by One Earth on a negotiated schedule, until those regions join. Defaulting on them has, in the past, brought lawsuits and seized assets. Paying them avoids asset seizures and trade blockades against new members. When the whole world has joined, no public debt remains anywhere.

**T7. The right to leave.** A region may leave by democratic vote, with a negotiated settlement. Every person may emigrate and keep their money up to the cap. The author confirmed this right. A union people cannot leave would not be voluntary.

Article 41a now gives the procedure, so the centre cannot stall. A request comes from the regional parliament or from 10% of voters. The referendum is held within **2 years**, and a simple majority wins if at least half the voters take part. Exit takes effect **2 years** later unless both sides agree otherwise. The region keeps what was built there and receives its population share of the common reserves. Until the day it leaves, its people keep every right and every allocation, and the Peace Force withdraws with its weapons. The right to leave is part of the protected core that no amendment may remove (Article 42a).

**T8. Protect production.** New members keep their farms, factories and services running with support from the union. Trade with non-members continues at negotiated prices. East Germany shows what happens when a currency shock is left to destroy production.

Two more provisions complete the picture. Armed forces are dissolved within **5 years** of full membership, and every member is offered work, training and full income. And one rule runs through the whole transition, fixed by the author. **Energy, travel and data are always metered and paid per unit**, never at a flat rate. Free or flat-rate access raises the use of such goods by **14–70%**, and the climate cannot absorb that.

## A closer look: the money cap in numbers

The cap came out of the money model in Chapter 6. That model followed **6,000** simulated adults in one region for **60 years**, under UNIRA's rules.

| 60-year simulation | No cap | Low cap, about 1.4 years, with zero-interest home credit | **Chosen: 1.5 years, with protected accounts** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unspent free money, in months of consumption | 152, still rising | **12**, stable | about **13** |
| Flow to One Earth, share of regional consumption | about 0% | about **20–23%** a year | about **22%** (19.8% with the time account) |
| Adults at the cap | not applicable | 57–61% | **40%** |

Two things stand out. First, the cap is not a rule for a handful of billionaires. Even at the chosen level, **four adults in ten** reach it. Second, the flow to One Earth is large. It is what pays for homes, energy and training in the regions that need them.

That is also why the cap must stay moderate. The research found that a cap set above the price of a home cannot keep money stable. Its first proposal was a cap of about **1 to 1.5 years** of base salary, recalculated each year, with homes bought on zero-interest credit. The author then chose a floor of **1.5 years**, with protected home and retirement accounts, after seeing that higher caps barely reduce the share of people at the cap but make a sudden spending rush much more dangerous: **+26%** of a year's goods at 1.5 years, **+78%** at 5 years.

These numbers come from a model of people, not from people. How real savers would respond to a cap has never been observed. The research lists this among the tests that could prove UNIRA wrong.

## An illustration: one region's road

Here is an imagined region, to show how the stages fit together. The order comes from the rules. The details are invented.

The region holds a referendum, and the yes side wins. From that day, the anti-flight rule applies. Wealth sent abroad still counts.

As an observer, the region opens its books. It sets up an audit office and a statistics office that meet One Earth's standards. It starts publishing its allocations in the public register. Nothing changes yet in people's wages.

As an associate, the region begins to receive needs-based transfers. If it is poor, new solar farms, clinics and housing sites appear. Its working week moves to 30 hours. Employers must find more workers, so jobs open up for people who had none.

As a full member, the region adopts the U. Wages move onto the UNIRA scale. Mortgages become zero-interest credit. Savings convert at basket parity. Public debts are cancelled. The money cap starts high, then falls a little each year for ten years.

At no point does anyone lose the home they live in. At no point are they forbidden to leave.

## Back to the kitchen

Now return to the imagined family on the night before the vote.

**The grandmother's savings.** They convert so that they buy the same food, housing and energy as before. Up to the cap, they stay hers in full. For an ordinary lifetime of saving, nothing is taken. Her pension promise is replaced by a needs-based income for life, covering food, daily life and one holiday a year. Her health care is free.

**The son's mortgage.** His home stays his. His mortgage becomes zero-interest credit in U, repaid from salary. If he owns a third home, he may sell it to the regional pool at the fixed price within about ten years.

**The granddaughter's evenings.** Her week becomes 30 hours. If she earns the base salary and saves half, she still consumes more than the poorest half of her country does today. She can buy a 100,000 U home in about **3.3 years** of saving, or faster with zero-interest credit.

None of this settles how they will vote. The grandmother may still fear change. The son may still prefer the world he knows. That is their right, and the referendum is how they use it.

## What the critics say, and what the research answers

**"Taking savings above the cap is confiscation."**

It is a transfer, and the research does not hide that. It openly says that taking savings above the cap directly is "legally and politically harder" than paying in bonds. The author chose it anyway. Two limits apply. The cap is phased in over ten years. And homes are never taken. Whether voters accept this is exactly what each referendum decides.

**"The rich will leave before the vote."**

Some will try. France and Norway show it. The anti-flight rule counts wealth held on the day of the vote, wherever it goes afterwards. The ten-year phase-in softens the blow. Neither removes the risk. No reliable measure exists of how many would try.

**"This is East Germany all over again."**

The research takes this seriously. UNIRA avoids the single political exchange rate, protects production and keeps trade open. Its enterprises do not compete on price, which removes the mechanism that sank East German firms. But one risk remains. If wages in U are high compared with a region's real productivity on day one, a similar shock could follow. The research says this must be modelled, not assumed away.

**"Why would any rich region ever vote yes?"**

On average, they lose some energy. But inside them, half the people are better off even at the lowest UNIRA income. Add peace, a shorter week, free care and a stable climate, and the balance may tip. Or it may not. The model cannot vote.

**"Outsiders will strangle a small union."**

The rules are written to avoid giving them a reason. Debts to outside creditors are honoured on a schedule. Trade continues at negotiated prices. And even a union of only the gaining regions closes the world's gaps faster than the best market world.

**"Debt cancellation is a gift to irresponsible governments."**

Inside UNIRA, no region carries public debt at all. The rule is not a one-off rescue. It is a permanent change. According to UNCTAD, about **3.3 billion** people live in countries that spend more on interest than on health or education (*A World of Debt*, 2023).

## What the evidence says

- **86%** of humanity, in **146** regions, gains materially by joining, compared with a reformed market world after 30 years. Ethiopia gains about **170%**, India **72%**.
- In the 33 richest regions, *average* energy use per person is **6–12%** lower than in a market future. Health care and homes are unchanged, nobody falls below the comfortable level, and 44% of their people are better off even at the lowest income.
- Inside France, Germany, Italy and Spain, **half** the people are better off even at the lowest UNIRA income. The poorest tenth gains **146–258%** across the regions studied.
- A union of only the gaining regions works at about half speed and still beats the best market world.
- History warns against one-shot conversion, fire sales, restitution and inflation-eaten compensation. It supports staged joining, protected small savings and capped debt relief.
- The eight transition rules answer these lessons directly: staged joining, no political exchange rate, basket parity, homes never seized, compensation not restitution, no public debts, a right to leave, and protected production.

## What we still don't know

- Taking savings above the cap directly is legally and politically harder than paying in bonds. The research says so openly.
- The anti-flight rule and slow phase-in reduce the risk of the very rich leaving before a vote. They do not remove it. No reliable measure exists of how many would try.
- If wages in U are high compared with a region's real productivity on day one, a shock like East Germany's could follow. Planned production avoids price competition, but the productivity gap is real.
- The material measure covers energy, health workers and floor space. It leaves out time, peace and security, which matter to voters too.
- The comparison inside rich regions uses income, not wealth.
- Full catch-up is a path of decades, not a first-year event. The EU's 2004 entrants took about 20 years to rise from 60% to 80%.
- There is no verified measure of how strongly wealthy people respond to a wealth cap. Only counts of departures exist.
