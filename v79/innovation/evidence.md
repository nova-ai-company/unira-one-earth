# V7.9 — Motivation and innovation without profit: evidence review

**Question.** Critics say that without profit, people stop trying and nobody innovates. The Soviet Union is their proof. Does the evidence support UNIRA's six proposed mechanisms, and what must change?

**Method.** We searched for primary, peer-reviewed or official sources and read each abstract or official page. Every number is in `evidence.csv` (67 rows) with its link. 61 rows say `verified_in_fetched_text = yes`, meaning we read the number in a fetched page. 6 rows say `no`, meaning we saw it only in search snippets. We do not use those 6 rows for any verdict. Many full texts were behind paywalls, so we read abstracts, official summaries or press releases. The CSV says which source each number came from.

**Short answer.** The critics are right about one thing. Profit is not the main source of new ideas, but it is the main way ideas get turned into products people use. Public money already pays for much of the basic science behind new products. Openness usually helps. Prizes work for problems that can be clearly defined. Intrinsic motivation is real and drives quality. The Soviet failure came from specific, avoidable design faults: the ratchet, plan bonuses that punished new products, soft budgets, and no user choice. **But UNIRA's fixed price per hour copies one of those faults** (see section 3). It must be fixed before the design can claim to answer the critics.

## 1. What the evidence says

### 1.1 Who pays for breakthroughs

| Finding | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| New drugs approved 2010–16 with NIH-funded research behind them | 210 of 210 | Cleary et al. 2018, PNAS |
| NIH project costs linked to them (2000–16) | >$100 bn, ~20% of NIH budget | same |
| Drugs discovered in public-sector institutions (40 years) | 153 | Stevens et al. 2011, NEJM |
| Drugs with a public-sector patent (direct role) | <10% | Sampat & Lichtenberg 2011 |
| Indirect public role, all drugs / priority-review drugs | ~half / ~two-thirds | same |
| US patents relying on federal research | ~one third | Fleming et al. 2019, Science (press release) |
| US basic research paid by government / business (2022) | 40% / 37% | NCSES |
| US R&D paid by business (2022) | ~$570 bn, ~76% | NCSES |
| OECD R&D, total / business-performed (2023) | 2.70% / 1.99% of GDP | OECD via Scottish Gov |
| US public money for mRNA covid vaccines | $31.9 bn, of which $337 m before 2020 | Lalani et al. 2023, BMJ |

**Reading.** Public research sits upstream of most breakthroughs: the Internet (DARPA), GPS (US government, free to all), the Web (CERN, made public domain in 1993), and the targets of every new drug. But **business pays for about three quarters of rich-country R&D**, and most of that is development. Most of the mRNA money (92%) went on buying vaccines, not on research. Public science starts the job. Turning it into products is most of the cost. Mazzucato's claim that every iPhone technology was state-funded comes from a publisher's description and is disputed.

### 1.2 Does public R&D pay back?

- $10 m more NIH funding → **2.7 more private patents** (Azoulay et al. 2019). This is causal, based on quirks in peer-review rules.
- 10% more government (defence) R&D → **5–6% more private R&D**. It crowds private R&D in, not out, and raises productivity (Moretti, Steinwender, Van Reenen 2025).
- Social returns are "many multiples" of cost (Jones & Summers 2020). Markets under-invest in innovation. Direct public funding is among the best short-run tools (Bloom, Van Reenen, Williams 2019).

### 1.3 Patents, openness and prizes

- With no patent laws, 19th-century countries **still innovated**, but in different industries (Moser 2005).
- Celera's private control of gene data cut later research **20–30%** (Williams 2013). Gene patents themselves had no measurable effect (Sampat & Williams 2019). The harm came from restricted access, not from the patent label.
- Opening access to research mice brought in more researchers and more varied work (Murray et al. 2016).
- Prizes: the English agricultural prizes (1839–1939) raised entries and patent quality. **Medals mattered most** (Brunt, Lerner, Nicholas 2012). The $1.5 bn vaccine Advance Market Commitment produced 3 vaccines and immunised >150 m children (Kremer, Levin, Snyder 2020). The $10 m Ansari XPRIZE drew about $100 m of team R&D (XPRIZE, self-reported). The Longitude prize warns what goes wrong: the Board changed the rules, and the dispute lasted decades.

### 1.4 Motivation

| Finding | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Rewards tied to a task undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation | d = −0.28 to −0.40 (128 experiments) | Deci, Koestner, Ryan 1999 |
| Positive feedback raises it | d = +0.33 | same |
| Intrinsic motivation predicts **quality**; incentives predict **quantity** | k=183, N=212,468 | Cerasoli et al. 2014 |
| Small pay can be worse than no pay | — | Gneezy & Rustichini 2000 |
| Piece rates raised output per worker | +44% | Lazear 2000 |
| Rewards for staff ideas raised idea quality, no crowding-out | — | Gibbs, Neckermann, Siemroth 2014 |
| Long-horizon, failure-tolerant funding (HHMI) → more high-impact papers | "much higher rate" | Azoulay, Graff Zivin, Manso 2011 |
| Tolerating early failure + rewarding long-run success → more discovery | lab | Ederer & Manso 2013 |

**Reading.** Money works for simple, measurable tasks. For creative work, the best results come from autonomy, recognition, long time horizons and tolerance of failure. Pay tied too closely to a measure narrows effort.

### 1.5 Pay differences and fairness

- When coworkers could not see why others were paid more, unequal pay cut output by **0.45 SD** and attendance by **18 points**. When the higher pay was visibly earned, it had **no effect** (Breza, Kaur, Shamdasani 2018).
- Learning you earn below your unit's median lowers satisfaction and raises job search. **Rank matters more than level** (Card et al. 2012).

### 1.6 Worker co-operatives

- Dissolution risk **29% lower** than conventional firms (Uruguay, Burdín 2014).
- As productive or more productive in every industry studied (France, Fakhfakh, Pérotin, Gago 2012). A meta-analysis of 43 studies finds participation and ownership linked to higher productivity (Doucouliagos 1995).
- Pay gaps much narrower (Pérotin 2016). Mondragon: €11.05 bn sales, 70,500 workers (2023).
- Innovation: mixed, not lower (French co-ops, Mirabel & Quignon 2025).
- Caveat: these co-ops work inside markets. They show that narrow pay and shared ownership can be productive. They do not show that a whole economy can do without markets.

### 1.7 Why Soviet innovation failed

| Cause | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Growth came from piling up capital; returns fell fast | Easterly & Fischer 1995: growth 1960–89 worst in the world after controls |
| Productivity growth vanished after the 1930s | Ofer: GNP growth fell from 5.8%/yr (1928–40) to 1.3% (1986–90); TFP ~30% of growth only in the 1930s |
| **Ratchet**: good results led to harder targets | Weitzman 1980 |
| **Plan bonuses punished new products** | Liberman 1962: profitability falls when firms master new products |
| Defence took the best technology (~15% of GNP) | Ofer |
| Soft budget: losers never stopped | Kornai (not verified in fetched text) |

### 1.8 Working time and creativity

- France's 39→35 hour cut: hours fell 6%, output only 1%, because **TFP rose 2%** (Carry et al. 2026, NBER).
- UK 4-day pilot: 56 of 61 firms continued, revenue +1.4% (campaign report, no control group).
- Volunteer work produces huge value. Open source software is worth an estimated **$8.8 trillion** in demand-side value (Hoffmann, Nagle, Zhou 2024). English Wikipedia has 7.26 m articles. But >95% of OSS value comes from 5% of developers, and Wikipedia's active editors fell from ~50,000 (2007) to ~30,000 (2014).
- **We found no causal study showing that shorter hours raise innovation.**

### 1.9 How research money is allocated

- Reviewers systematically **score highly novel proposals lower** (Boudreau et al. 2016, randomised).
- Among NIH's top-scored grants, the score explains only **~1%** of later productivity. The authors propose a lottery among fundable proposals (Fang, Bowen, Casadevall 2016).

## 2. Verdicts

| # | Mechanism | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Salary multipliers 1–5× | **Partly supported** | Money raises effort and quantity (+44%). Co-ops show narrow pay can be productive. But pay gaps people cannot see as earned cut output by 0.45 SD. Multipliers for "complexity" are hard to observe. Nothing tests whether a 5× cap loses top talent. |
| 2 | Competition for quality: teams design, people choose, winners get recognition + a multiplier step | **Partly supported** | Medals and recognition work (agricultural prizes). Rewards for ideas raise quality. User choice fixes the Soviet "no exit" fault. But there is no test at the scale of a whole economy. A *permanent* multiplier step is pay tied to one result, which narrows creative effort. |
| 3 | Fixed research budget (3–4% of hours), open peer review, open results, no patents | **Supported for research; weak for development** | Public research has high returns and crowds in other R&D. Openness helps or is neutral. But peer review penalises novelty and predicts output poorly. Markets pay for most development, about 3/4 of R&D. A research-only budget leaves that gap empty. |
| 4 | Innovation prizes for defined problems | **Supported** (within limits) | AMC, agricultural prizes and XPRIZE all worked. They only fit problems that can be specified in advance. Rules must be fixed and judged independently (Longitude). |
| 5 | Freedom to start projects/co-ops, funded if expert-approved, higher multiplier for founders | **Partly supported** | Co-ops survive and perform well. But a single expert gate shows the same bias against novelty as peer review. A soft budget (no stop rule) was a Soviet failure. |
| 6 | 30-hour week frees time for creative work | **Weak** | Shorter hours can raise productivity per hour (TFP +2%). Volunteers create huge value, but a few do most of it and participation can fall. There is no causal evidence that free time produces innovation. |

None of the mechanisms is **contradicted**. But one existing UNIRA rule works against all of them (section 3, change A).

## 3. Design changes the evidence suggests

**A. Fix the innovation penalty in fixed prices (critical).** Price = 146 U × embodied hours. If a team invents a way to make a product in half the hours, the product's price halves, and so does the team's revenue. That is Liberman's 1962 complaint in a new form. *Change:* a team that cuts the hours needed for a product keeps an **innovation dividend**. For example, it keeps half the hours saved for 3 years, as free time, a bonus fund, or extra multiplier points. The saving then passes to everyone.

**B. No ratchet.** A unit's targets may not be raised because it beat them. Targets follow measured needs, not past performance.

**C. Hard budgets for projects, soft landings for people.** Every project has a stop date and a published test of success. Failed projects end and the money stops. The people keep their salary level and can start again (HHMI and Ederer–Manso: tolerate failure, reward long-run success).

**D. Several independent funders, partial lottery, a novelty quota.** Have at least three independent research funders (One Earth, regions, scientific societies). Reviewers screen for soundness, then fund by **lottery among proposals above the bar**. Keep at least 20% for high-risk or new-entrant work. Fund some people, not projects, for 5–7 years.

**E. Fund development, not only research.** Add a **development and scale-up** budget and **advance purchase commitments** from One Earth's own buying (the AMC model). These replace the ~3/4 of R&D that business funds today.

**F. Make multipliers visible and earned.** Publish the criteria, use peer and user evidence, and give every worker a right to appeal. Prefer recognition and *time-limited* bonuses for single achievements over permanent pay steps.

**G. Real user choice.** Production follows what users choose. Designs nobody chooses are retired. Choice data are public.

**H. Protect civilian innovation from defence.** Cap the share of research hours spent on defence and publish it (Soviet defence absorbed ~15% of GNP and its best technology).

## 4. Draft constitution article

**Article 3c — Innovation and improvement.**
1. **Research and development are a duty of One Earth.** At least 3% of all working hours go to research and development, and at least a third of that to developing and scaling up new products and methods, not only to research.
2. **Open results.** Results paid for by One Earth or a region are published openly, with their data. Inventors are named and honoured. No one may own an idea so as to stop others from using it.
3. **Many funders, fair choice.** Research money is given by at least three independent bodies. Proposals are first checked by experts for soundness. Among those that pass, money is given partly by lottery, so that new and unusual ideas are not shut out. At least a fifth goes to high-risk work or to people funded for the first time.
4. **Prizes and purchase promises.** For a clearly defined problem, One Earth may offer a prize or a promise to buy the solution. The rules and judges are fixed in advance and may not be changed against those who have already competed.
5. **Whoever improves, gains.** A team that reduces the hours needed to make a product keeps part of the hours saved for a limited time, as set by law. A unit's targets are never raised because it did better than planned.
6. **Freedom to try, duty to stop.** Any group may propose a project or co-operative. It needs approval from one of several independent bodies, not all of them. Every project has a budget, an end date and a published test of success. A project that fails its test is closed. The people who tried keep their salary level and may try again.
7. **People choose.** For goods in common use, people can choose between designs from different teams whenever this is practical. Teams whose designs are chosen are publicly recognised. Production follows the choices people make.
8. **Fair and visible rewards.** The criteria for salary multipliers are published, and everyone may see why they are paid what they are paid and may appeal.
9. **Measured every year.** The Scientific Council (Article 3a) publishes, for One Earth and for each region:
    - (a) the share of working hours in research and development;
    - (b) the hours of work needed to produce the basket of essential needs, and how fast this falls;
    - (c) the share of production from products or methods introduced in the last 5 years;
    - (d) the share of research money going to new applicants and to high-risk work;
    - (e) user satisfaction with the quality and choice of common goods;
    - (f) the number of new projects and co-operatives started per 100,000 working people, and how many survive 5 years;
    - (g) how fairly workers judge their pay, and whether they feel free to propose improvements;
    - (h) the delay between a new technology being proven anywhere in the world and its general use in One Earth.
10. **Automatic review and reform.**
    - If, for 2 years in a row, research and development falls below 2.5% of working hours, or the hours needed for the essential basket fall by less than 0.5% a year, or the measure in 9(d) falls below 15%, the Assembly must review this Article and the salary rules within one year.
    - If, for 5 years in a row, the hours needed for the essential basket fall by less than 0.5% a year, or the measures in 9(c), 9(e) or 9(h) are worse than in the 5 years before, the rules on prices, salaries and research funding must be reformed by law. Any measure that fixes the failure is allowed, including new rewards for innovators.
11. **Defence does not starve civilian invention.** The share of research hours spent on defence is published every year and limited by law.

*Evidence: V7.9 (`v79/innovation/evidence.md`). The thresholds are provisional. They must be set from measured starting values when a region joins (Article 3a.4).*

**Note on thresholds.** The 0.5%/year floor follows from the Soviet case, where productivity growth stalled and then turned negative after about 1970 (Ofer). Rich economies have usually done better than this over the long run, but we did not fetch a source for a precise benchmark. The 2.5% R&D floor is a little below the 2023 OECD average of 2.70% of GDP. The thresholds for 9(c) and 9(f) need baseline data that we did not collect.

## 5. Honest limitations

- **Almost all evidence comes from market economies**, mostly the US. Public research there feeds firms that develop and sell products. That a public system does research well does not prove it can do development and diffusion without them.
- **Mechanisms 2 and 6 have no direct test.** Their verdicts rest on analogies (prizes, medals, volunteers).
- **Co-op evidence is selected.** Co-ops choose their members and markets, and they compete. Their results may not carry over to a whole economy.
- **The Soviet failure had many causes** (war, defence, isolation, politics). We cannot say how much each design fault cost. Kornai and Berliner were not verified in fetched text.
- **Several numbers come from abstracts read through a summarising fetch tool, or from press releases** (Fleming 2019, Stevens 2011 "twice the rate", OSS value). Their wording may lose nuance. The mapping of the three Deci et al. d values to reward types follows the tool's reading of the abstract. The OSS value estimate is disputed.
- **Some figures differ between versions**: Breza (working paper −0.24 SD vs published −0.45 SD), Azoulay (2.3/3.1 vs 2.7), Moretti (4.3% vs 5–6%), mRNA DoD ($72 m vs $77 m). We used the published versions.
- **No PDF full texts were read.** The tool could not extract text from them.
- **The thresholds in Article 3c are proposals to be tested,** not measured results.
