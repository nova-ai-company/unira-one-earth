# What protects democracies from capture? Evidence review for One Earth (V7.9)

*3 November 2026. This review supports `STRESS_TEST.md` and `POWER_ARTICLES.md`. Rows are listed in `evidence.csv`, where "E n" is row n (row 1 is the first row after the header). A row marked `yes` was checked in text I fetched or downloaded during this session. `partial` means only the citation or a press paraphrase was seen. Figures I saw only in search snippets, and could not confirm, are listed under "Not verified" and are not used.*

## 1. The threat today is erosion, not tanks
- **The scale.**
  - 74% of the world's people live in autocracies.
  - 92 countries are autocracies and 87 are democracies.
  - Only 7% of people live in liberal democracies.
  - 44 countries are autocratizing, and they are home to 41% of humanity (V-Dem 2026, E1–E4).
- **Media goes first.** Media censorship is the most common tactic of autocratizing governments, used by 32 of the 44 (73%). Freedom of expression is "often the first domino to fall" (E5, E6).
- **How backsliding has changed.** Bermeo (2016) finds open coups and blatant fraud declining, while executive aggrandizement and strategic manipulation are rising (E14).

**Implication:** a world constitution must above all resist legal-looking capture by elected majorities.

## 2. Coups and the armed force
- **How often coups succeed.** The Powell & Thyne data, as packaged to 2015, record 236 successful and 238 failed coups since 1950, so roughly one attempt in two succeeds (E7).
- **Regime-style coup-proofing.** Coup-proofing by loyalty, parallel militaries and secret police works at the cost of military power (Quinlivan 1999, E8).
- **Stacking and purging.** Excluding groups trades coup risk for civil war: purged co-conspirators are 16 times more likely to rebel (Roessler 2011, E9). Building or dismantling ethnic armies provokes officer revolts (Harkness 2016, E10).
- **Counterbalancing.** It cuts the success of coups, not their number, and works mainly because some soldiers resist. Creating a new security force raises coup risk the next year (De Bruin 2018, E11, E12).
- **Weapons control.** Two-person rules and permissive action links make it physically impossible for one person to arm a nuclear weapon (E40, E41).

**Implication:**
- mix the Force by rule, not by loyalty;
- no parallel forces;
- weapons under many keys;
- a protected right to refuse;
- extra care during the build-up.

## 3. Term limits and amendments
- **How common evasion is.** Since 2000, at least a third of presidents who reached their limit tried to stay (about half outside the strongest democracies), across 234 incumbents in 106 countries.
- **The routes.** 66% of attempts used amendment, and attempts to delay elections succeeded about two-thirds of the time.
- **Courts and resistance.** Courts helped in 30% of successful overstays. About a third of attempts failed, mostly because of popular resistance (Versteeg et al. 2020, E15–E19).
- **Consolidated democracies.** Leaders there usually respect limits, and evasion happens by amending or replacing the constitution (Ginsburg, Melton & Elkins 2011, E20).
- **Constitutions are short-lived.** Only half last more than 19 years (Elkins, Ginsburg & Melton 2009, E21).
- **Eternity clauses.**
  - The German Basic Law makes federalism and its core principles unamendable (Art. 79(3)) and gives a right to resist (Art. 20(4)) (E28, E29).
  - Unamendable provisions rose from 17% of constitutions written in 1789–1944 to 53% of those written in 1989–2013 (Roznai, E30).

## 4. Courts
- Established independent courts prevent slides toward authoritarianism (163 countries, 1960–2000), but **newly formed courts are associated with more regime collapse** (Gibler & Randazzo 2011, E22, E23).

**Implication:** a world Court will be weakest exactly when it is new, so its size, budget and jurisdiction must be fixed in the text.

## 5. Emergencies
- **The risk.** Democracies are 75% more likely to erode under a state of emergency (60 democracies, 1974–2016) (Lührmann & Rooney, working paper version, E24).
- **Weak national clauses.** Only 55% of emergency clauses set a time limit, and nearly 40% allow decrees with force of law (E25).
- **Outcomes.** Of 54 autocratization episodes that began in democracies, 40 ended in breakdown (E26).
- **A recent example.** Canada's 2022 account freezes under emergency powers were later held by a federal court to violate protection against unreasonable search and seizure (E27; the government appealed).

## 6. Money, payment data and AI
- **Central banks.** Politically controlled central banks are associated with higher and more variable inflation, with little effect on growth (Alesina & Summers 1993, E35). Committed rules can beat discretion (Kydland & Prescott 1977, E36, citation only).
- **Privacy designs for digital currency.**
  - The ECB's digital euro design offers cash-like privacy offline and uses pseudonymisation so the issuer cannot directly link online payments to users (E37).
  - BIS Project Tourbillon prototyped payer anonymity, at a performance cost when quantum-safe cryptography is used (E39).
- **AI.** The EU AI Act bans social scoring (E45). The Dutch childcare-benefits model used nationality as a risk factor, which led to discrimination (E46).

## 7. Citizens' assemblies
- **Spread.** The OECD catalogued 289 representative deliberative processes (1986–2019), 14 of them institutionalised, including Ostbelgien's permanent 24-member Citizens' Council chosen by lot (E31, E32).
- **Ireland.** The Citizens' Assembly had 99 members chosen by lot. 87% of them agreed the Eighth Amendment should not be retained in full. The 2018 referendum then passed with 66.40% (E33, E34).

**Limit:** this shows lot-based bodies are workable and can unlock decisions. It does not show they prevent capture.

## 8. Exit
- **Ethiopia.** Article 39(4) sets out the full procedure: a two-thirds vote of the group's council, a referendum within 3 years, a majority vote, and division of assets by law (E42).
- **European Union.** Article 50 lets a state leave under its own constitutional rules, with a 2-year default exit date (E43).
- **Nevis.** In 1998, 61.7% voted to leave St Kitts and Nevis, short of the two-thirds required, so Nevis stayed (E44).

## Honest limitations
1. **No federation of this scale or kind exists.** All the evidence comes from nation states, regional unions (the EU) or sub-national units. One Earth would have no outside power to balance it and no country to flee to. This makes some risks larger than in any studied case, especially capture of the only armed force and of the only currency. The transfer is by analogy.
2. **Most findings are correlations.** Emergencies, courts and term limits are all associated with outcomes, but reverse causation is likely. For example, emergencies are declared when democracy is already ill (the authors say so).
3. **Exact numbers are judgement.** These include 30 days, 2%, 21 judges, 200 U and 50% turnout. The evidence identifies the routes to capture, not the exact thresholds.
4. **Federalism and decentralisation.** I did not fetch a quantitative study showing that federalism or decentralisation protects against backsliding, so no row claims it. The design relies on it by reasoning (separating power across many regions) and on the stress test.
5. **Exit effects are not established.** Whether a clear right to secede reduces or increases conflict is contested. I verified the legal texts, not their effects.
6. **Some sources are secondary.**
   - Wikipedia for PALs.
   - A press paraphrase for the Canadian judgment.
   - The newDemocracy page for the OECD figures.
   - The V-Dem working paper rather than the published Comparative Politics version.
   - An R-package page for the Powell & Thyne counts, because the uky.edu dataset returned 403.
