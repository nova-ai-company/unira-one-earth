UNIRA V9.0 — can fair distribution of resources reduce armed conflict?

Question (author): can UNIRA's fair distribution of resources reduce armed conflict, and would fewer conflicts let humanity reach Level 1 (essential security) and Level 2 (comfortable life, Article 10c) sooner? The question is to be studied with evidence, separating correlation from causation, with no invented percentages. Scenario D is a conditional upper bound, not a forecast. Constitution (author's decision, 2026-11-04): the three proposals were approved and incorporated as Articles 5.6, 10e.6 and 10f, with conflict prevention added to 10f; see CONSTITUTION_CONSISTENCY_CHECK.md.

Short answers

  1. How much of today's conflict burden is plausibly linked to deprivation?
    • Observed: today, 31% of the poorest quarter of countries are in armed conflict, against 2–4% in each of the other three quarters (World Bank FY26 list; living standards from water, sanitation, clean cooking and electricity access).
    • If that whole pattern were causal: about 24–67% of today's conflict risk would be linked to deprivation (central 40%).
    • But not all of it is causal. Conflict destroys services (reverse causation), and income largely stands for state capacity (Fearon & Laitin 2003).
    • Plausibly causal share: anywhere from about 5% to about two thirds, central about a fifth (40% × a causal fraction of one half). No more precise figure is justified.
  2. How much could UNIRA realistically reduce it? Over 80 years, the share of conflict-years avoided compared with no reduction (A) is:
    • B, moderate: about 13%;
    • C, strong: about 26%;
    • D, conditional upper bound: about 68%, but D adds hypothetical diplomacy and reconciliation, so its gain is not UNIRA's alone.
    • Little changes in the first 20 years of any scenario except D.
  3. Is a positive feedback loop plausible? Yes, but it is slow, and it starts by working against UNIRA:
    • conflict halves delivery exactly where conflict risk is highest, so living standards rise slowly there (a "conflict trap");
    • the virtuous loop becomes visible only after year 20–40.
    • Backlash against programmes (Crost et al. 2014) matters locally but changes the world totals by about 1%.
  4. Which conflicts would remain? In B and C:
    • Where: the poorest and weakest states. The Central African Republic, DR Congo, Chad, Niger, Madagascar, Ethiopia, Mozambique and Malawi top the list in every run.
    • Why: about two thirds of the remaining risk is not linked to living standards. It comes from territory, identity, exclusion from power and state weakness.
    • Also outside the model: wars between states and great-power wars.
  5. How much faster are Level 1 and Level 2?
    • Level 1 for 90%: almost no change (year 16 in every scenario).
    • The last 5–10% of people: this is where conflict matters. Compared with A:
    • Level 1 for 99%: about 3–8 years sooner in B–C, 21 years sooner in D;
    • Level 2 for 95%: about 4–6 years sooner in B–C, 14 years sooner in D.
    • Universal Level 1 (100%) is reached within 80 years only in D (year 57).

Method (conflict/model.py)

Conflict model. Each of 179 countries is in conflict or at peace each year (a two-state chain over 80 years):

  • Starting state: the 19 countries on the World Bank FY26 conflict list that the model covers. South Sudan and Palestine lack data.
  • Onset risk combines two parts:
  • a frozen part (share s_ne) that stays at today's level whatever happens to living standards: territory, identity, exclusion, state weakness;
  • a responsive part that follows each country's living standards, through a fraction c of the observed pattern between poverty and conflict that is assumed causal.
  • The pattern itself (slope β = 4.75) is estimated by maximum likelihood from which countries are in conflict today. It is an association, not a cause.
  • Ending risk: a conflict ends with a 10% chance a year, giving a median duration of about 7 years. Better living standards raise that chance (tau).
  • Relapse: in the 10 years after a conflict ends, onset risk is 4 times higher, so about 40% relapse within 10 years (the literature finds 20–57%).
  • Backlash: in conflict and fragile countries, onset risk rises by half (B) or a quarter (C, D, with safeguards) in programme years 1–3.
  • Calibration: the base risk H is set so that A keeps today's number of conflicts on average. A then stays at 17–19 countries in conflict, with 13–24 in 80% of runs at year 40.

Coupling with V8.9 (v89/integrated/simulate.py, accelerated delivery):

  • Conflict slows UNIRA:
  • delivery is halved during conflict, as in V8.9;
  • fragility cuts delivery by a quarter;
  • the quarter of the population living inside conflict zones gets no service but food.
  • UNIRA lowers conflict: each country's living-standard index (today's observed access; later the higher of that and 0.7 × Level 1 + 0.3 × Level 2 coverage) lowers onset risk and raises the chance of ending.
  • Solving the loop: conflict and living standards are iterated three times with the same random draws.
  • Uncertainty: 30 random runs per scenario (median and 10–90% range) and 14 one-at-a-time sensitivity tests (10 runs each).

Reading the comparison with V8.9.

  • Level 2 for 90% is earlier in A (year 55) than in V8.9 (year 72), even with no conflict reduction. V8.9 assumed the same 21 conflicts last 80 years. In A, conflicts end after about 7 years and new ones start elsewhere, as in history. That gives every country periods of peace in which to build.
  • For the last 1% of Level 1, A is later than V8.9 (year 60 against 53). A also takes services away from the people inside conflict zones, and new conflicts hit countries that V8.9 treated as peaceful.
  • What these differences mean: they come from modelling conflict realistically, not from UNIRA.
  • The gain attributable to UNIRA's effect on conflict is the gap between A and B or C.

Yearly bottleneck (first run). The bottleneck is the limiting factor, for Level 2:

  • years 1–10: training health workers (about 7 bn people short, i.e. everyone not yet at Level 2);
  • years 20–40: local services in the poorest regions;
  • from year 60 in A and B: conflict (people in conflict countries);
  • in C, conflict becomes the bottleneck only around year 80; in D, it never does.

Sensitivity (one change at a time from B, 10 runs):

  • Conflict-years depend most on:
  • the causal fraction c (1,471 at c = 0.25 against 1,275 at c = 1);
  • the slope β (1,445 at half the fitted slope);
  • the frozen share (1,295–1,444).
  • Backlash from 0 to +185% changes conflict-years by under 1%. Relapse and duration assumptions change them by about 3%.
  • Level 1 for 90% and 95% stays at years 16–18 and 27–29 in every test.
  • Level 2 for 95% ranges from year 65 to 77. The largest delays come from a bigger conflict-zone share (40%) and a weak causal effect.

Limitations

  • The conflict list is narrow: the World Bank list holds 21 conflict situations; UCDP counts 61 state-based conflicts, many of them small or in larger states (for example India and Pakistan). The model is about conflicts severe enough to affect a whole country.
  • Not modelled: wars between states and great-power wars. Russia–Ukraine shows these can dominate battle deaths.
  • Countries are treated as wholes. Conflict is local, and inequality between groups is not represented.
  • The living-standard index measures access to basic services, not income or political inclusion.
  • No duration dependence: the model does not let long wars become harder to end.
  • The main unknown, the causal fraction c, cannot be estimated from data available today. Proposal 2 would let monitoring measure it during implementation.

Reproduce

python3 conflict/model.py writes outputs/conflict.json (about 10 minutes). The tests in conflict/tests/ cover:

  • reproducibility;
  • the observed gradient;
  • scenario ordering;
  • the calibration of A;
  • that diplomacy appears only in D;
  • the risk rules;
  • that no date is earlier than if all conflicts ended now.

V9.0 — consistency check of the three approved constitutional changes

Decision (author, 2026-11-04): the three V9.0 proposals are approved. Article 10f was extended from avoiding harm during delivery to active conflict prevention. The changes were checked against Articles 10c and 10e and against the existing protections before publication.

The changes, as incorporated in v75/constitution/UNIRA_CONSTITUTION_DRAFT.md

  • Article 5.6 — Prosperity does not guarantee peace. This is the author's wording, unchanged. Only a cross-reference "(Article 10f)" was added at the end.
  • Article 10e.6 — Monitoring of conflict effects. It extends the independent monitoring body of 10e.4 to assess whether programmes reduce or increase conflict risks. It requires:
  • separating observed patterns from shown causes;
  • independent evaluation;
  • community feedback;
  • protection of sensitive information;
  • yearly publication and adjustment.
  • Article 10f — Peaceful delivery and conflict prevention. Five points:
  • delivery that does not increase tensions: fair allocation, local participation, protection against discrimination, independent grievance mechanisms;
  • services never withheld because a region is considered unstable;
  • assessing the conflict risks of large programmes;
  • conflict prevention: mediation, early warning, peaceful dispute resolution, cooperation between communities, with a civilian One Earth Mediation Service;
  • independent supervision and no surveillance or repression.

Open points (left to law and research, not decided in the text)

  • How the Mediation Service is appointed, and its size. Under Article 22a.4, it must not be chosen by a body it supervises or one that supervises it. To be set by law.
  • What "large programme" means in 10f.3, and the method of the conflict-risk assessment.
  • How effective mediation and early warning are at world scale: not yet established. This is the next research priority set by the author: preventing conflict through fair institutions, representation, transparent allocation and peaceful dispute resolution.

Conclusion: no inconsistency was found with Articles 10c and 10e or with the protected core. One possible ambiguity in Article 10e.3 ("taking practical delivery into account") is resolved by Article 10f.2.

V9.0 — proposed constitution changes

Status (2026-11-04): all three approved by the author and incorporated, with the author's additions. See CONSTITUTION_CONSISTENCY_CHECK.md for the final texts and the consistency check:

  • Proposal 1 became Article 10f — Peaceful delivery and conflict prevention, extended at the author's request from avoiding harm to active prevention (mediation, early warning, peaceful dispute resolution, cooperation between communities), with independent supervision and a ban on surveillance or repression. Essential services are never withheld because a region is considered unstable.
  • Proposal 2 became Article 10e.6 (monitoring of conflict effects), with independent evaluation, community feedback and protection of sensitive information.
  • Proposal 3 became Article 5.6 in the author's own wording.

The original proposals, as first presented, follow for the record.

Proposal 1 — Conflict-sensitive delivery (new Article 10f, after Article 10e)

Finding. Development programmes can provoke violence while they are being set up: insurgents attack projects that would strengthen the institutions they fight. One programme in the Philippines saw insurgent attacks rise by 110–185% where projects started (Crost, Felter & Johnston 2014, regression-discontinuity design). In the V9.0 model, this backlash matters little worldwide. It does matter in the countries where it occurs.

Proposed text. Article 10f — Delivery that does not cause conflict.

  1. In regions in conflict or institutional fragility, essential services are delivered in a way that does not give any armed group or faction a reason or an opportunity to attack them. The services are planned with the communities that receive them, and run by them where possible. They are delivered equally to every group living in the area, without favouring any group, faction or region, and their allocation is published.
  2. Before a large programme starts in such a region, an independent assessment estimates its conflict risk. The programme is adjusted if the assessment finds a serious risk.
  3. Attacks on essential services are recorded and published, and the Peace Force (Articles 5a–5c) protects water, health, food and energy sites only when the local community asks for it.

Why each element.

  • Equally to every group: inequality between groups is linked to ethnic civil war (Cederman, Weidmann & Gleditsch 2011).
  • Planned with communities: local participation is the safeguard that lowers backlash in scenarios B–D. The size of that reduction is an assumption.
  • Protection only on request: this keeps the Peace Force from being seen as an occupying force.

Proposal 2 — Independent conflict monitoring (addition to Article 10e, point on monitoring)

Finding. The decisive unknown is how much of the link between poverty and conflict is causal. In the model that is the fraction c, between 0 and 1. It cannot be settled in advance. It can be learned during implementation.

Proposed text (new point in Article 10e): The independent monitoring body also records, every year and in each region, armed-conflict events, deaths, displacement and attacks on services, using open international data. It publishes an assessment of whether better living standards are being followed by less violence. Programmes are adjusted to what is observed, not to what was expected.

Proposal 3 — Wording safeguard: peace is not promised

Finding. Even the strongest economic scenario (C) leaves most of the remaining conflict risk unrelated to living standards: territory, identity, exclusion from power, state weakness. Only scenario D, which adds hypothetical diplomacy and reconciliation, comes close to ending conflict, and D is an upper bound.

Proposed text (in the Preamble's notes or Part I): Fair distribution removes some causes of armed conflict, but not all of them. One Earth therefore works for peace by political means as well: mediation, reconciliation, the protection of minorities, and power shared between groups. One Earth does not claim that prosperity alone will bring peace.

Not proposed

  • No change to Article 10c (the two living-standard levels) or to Article 10e's targets. V9.0 confirms that conflict delays them; it does not justify lowering them.
  • No "peace dividend" article. Resources freed by fewer conflicts are already counted through faster delivery. Military spending is already handled by Article 5a.
