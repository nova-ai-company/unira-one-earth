# UNIRA — VERSION HISTORY, RESULTS AND THEIR LIMITS

**Interpretation:** Numbers labelled *synthetic* are outputs of deliberately chosen assumptions, not observed economic benefits. Some summaries were extracted from prior discussion; verify exact scenario data against archived CSV/JSON before quoting.

## Early framework V1–V3.3
- Hypothetical population 8.2bn; hypothetical workforce 4.35bn ×30h/week =130.5bn weekly hours. A separate illustrative comparator 3.6bn employed ×43h =154.8bn hours/week; matching that output at unchanged employment assumptions would require roughly **18.6%** more output per hour. Different workforce assumptions make this NOT a real-world forecast.
- Base wage 5,000 U/month for 4.35bn workers implies **261 trillion U/year** nominal payroll; theoretical gross child allowances can be enormous before means-testing. These are bookkeeping identities, not a claim about purchasing power or affordability.
- OECD ICIO 2025 (95 economies + rest of world through 2022), World Bank, ILO, FAO, IEA, Eurostat, INSEE and national statistics were identified as candidate data sources. Some early World Bank API fetch attempts failed; do not imply global calibration was completed.

## V3.4–V4.0: stock-first and digital system
- V3.4 stock-first inventory model: maintain/repair/reuse before production; synthetic cases suggested no new housing/appliance production under chosen stock assumptions, some food production required.
- V3.5 housing optimisation of repair, renovation, conversion and new construction, including environmental trade-offs; synthetic.
- V3.6 digital currency/offline resilience; V3.7 verified invoice validation; V3.8 links physical stocks and payment; V3.9 prototype purchase → validated invoice → wallet → inventory → ledger (reported 10 tests); V4.0 synthetic three-region resource sharing and ecological accounting.

## V4.1–V4.6: cities and housing evidence
- Vallauris 2023 INSEE pilot: 20,728 dwellings, 13,962 primary, 5,522 secondary/occasional, 1,244 vacant. Illustrative recovery of 124/483/898 units under hypothetical vacancy/secondary conversion fractions. **No observed recoverable count.** User subsequently insisted on MAJOR cities rather than Vallauris as primary examples.
- Five-city programme: Paris, Greater London, New York City, Tokyo and Cairo. Distinct administrative boundaries and vacancy definitions; never add Paris municipality to overlapping greater-area totals.
- Paris 2022 municipality: 136,957 vacant dwellings cited in earlier package. Paris 2023 and greater-area numbers appear in older notes; verify year and boundary before comparisons.
- London 2025: 105,138 recorded empty homes (different classification from NYC rental availability). London 2024 earlier reference 93,602. Do not blend years.
- NYC 2023: 33,210 available rental units and 1.41% rental vacancy, NOT all vacant dwellings or recoverable housing.
- Tokyo 2023: 896,500 vacant dwellings cited, with approx 629,000 for rent, 43,800 for sale and 214,200 'other'; category subtotals may not sum due to rounding/other classes; verify official source.
- Cairo: no comparably defined verified city-wide vacancy figure in available work; keep missing rather than invent.
- Paris overcrowding: 2023 municipality ~28.2% moderate and 2.6% severe; 2022 Paris urban area ~19.8% moderate and 2.9% severe. London ~6.7% households overcrowded 2023–24. **Definitions differ; avoid direct rankings.**

## V4.7–V5.3: housing allocation
- V4.7 exact/optimisation housing interventions, costs and illustrative lifecycle emissions; results synthetic. V4.8 England energy-efficiency upgrade benchmark £7,480 and 56% A–C ratings in 2024, London EPC 70, NYC vacancy. Energy upgrades ≠ comprehensive renovation.
- V4.9 exact allocation: synthetic 100 households (20 accessible); reported min-cost €8.565m and 4,416 tCO2e; other carbon-weighted scenarios differ depending on constraints. Review source code for objective definitions.
- V5.0 lexicographic stock-first optimisation (minimise new homes then cost/carbon); results synthetic, not validated.
- V5.1 city evidence register; V5.2 housing needs/overcrowding and non-duplication of households; V5.3 synthetic 100 households: 20 repair/adapt, 35 deep renovation, 25 conversion, 20 new, cost €14.6m; €8m budget yields 74 housed/26 unmet in one scenario. Synthetic coefficients.

## V5.4–V5.7: real resource accounting and money
- V5.4 compare current-style/reformed/UNIRA housing intervention mixes in hypothetical 100-home case. Illustrative UNIRA mix required 19,700 labour hours, 66 t cement, 347.5 tCO2e vs current-style 25,700 hours, 117 t cement, 670 tCO2e. **Differences are assumptions about reuse, NOT observed UNIRA advantage.**
- V5.5 100-household hypothetical annual income 10.2m U; spending allocation 7.65m U vs specified basket cost 5.484m U. Purchasing-power arithmetic is not price stability. Food and housing shortages can occur despite balanced public finances; public finance can fail despite physical availability.
- V5.6 transaction ledger for households, firms, public and investment funds, explicit issuance; food shortage 30 units, public shortfall 300,000 U, labour shortage 2,000h in independent synthetic stress cases. Not yet fully stock-flow consistent.
- V5.7 institutional balance sheets, bank lending/deposits, inventory, capital and depreciation; stress cases food 30 units, labour shortage 3,000h, public shortfall 310,000 U; borrowing can fill a financing gap but not create goods. Balance-sheet identities alone ≠ macroeconomic closure.

## V5.8–V5.9: multi-year and multisector
- V5.8 synthetic 20-year 100-household abstract consumption-bundle model: baseline 0 shortage years but **20m U cumulative borrowing**; 2.5% annual population growth 19 shortage years; no replacement investment 19; severe shock 1. Borrowing ceiling in that version was reported diagnostic, not truly binding. Units are abstract bundles.
- V5.9 six-sector 20-year prototype (food, housing, energy, healthcare, manufacturing, infrastructure) with Leontief-style input-output requirements and capacity/labour/investment constraints. Baseline 0 shortage years; energy shock 1; 2.5% demand growth 16; no replacement investment 14; limited investment budget 11; labour shortage 20. Fixed proportions and uniform rationing are simplistic and **not endorsed social allocation policies**. 'Unmet index' sums are not physically meaningful cross-sector quantities.

## V6.0–V6.1: France empirical anchors
- V6.0 France 2023 RTE electricity generation 494.7 TWh, weather-adjusted consumption 445.7 TWh, fossil generation ~32.6 TWh; INSEE 2023 GDP €2,822.5bn. Production and adjusted consumption are not directly subtractable as spare capacity. Index scenarios synthetic, not energy balance forecasts.
- V6.1 INSEE end-2025 employed people ~30.5m (includes self-employed/part-time); INSEE 2025 effective annual hours **1,656 for full-time employees**, not all workers. Illustrative 30h×46 weeks =1,380 annual hours. At fixed employment and output, required productivity/hour increase = current hours /1,380 −1: 1,400→1.45%, 1,500→8.70%, 1,600→15.94%, 1,656→20.00%. **Only 1,656 is a reported full-time benchmark; other hours are sensitivity assumptions.** Do not multiply 30.5m by 1,656 to claim national hours.

## Core research findings (appropriately qualified)
1. Real needs and existing stock must be measured together; vacancy counts are not recoverable housing counts.
2. Stock-first can lower physical input needs under some assumptions but may conflict with long-run operational emissions or suitability.
3. Money and goods are distinct; balanced accounts can coexist with unmet needs, and goods with funding gaps.
4. Investment/depreciation, labour, intersectoral inputs and shocks matter over decades.
5. Shorter workweeks imply an arithmetic productivity/output trade-off, but avoidable output, staffing, unpaid work and sector heterogeneity must be incorporated.
6. No model so far establishes UNIRA's global feasibility, real purchasing power, lower emissions or superiority over credible reforms.
