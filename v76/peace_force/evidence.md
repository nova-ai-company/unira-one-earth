# One Earth Peace Force: evidence for a size ceiling and safeguards (v76)

Scope: this supports Constitution v75 Article 5a.4, which says the Peace Force's size and budget must stay under a constitutional ceiling. The sources for every number are in `evidence.csv`. A row marked `verified_in_fetched_text = yes` means the figure appeared in the text of a page or PDF that was actually fetched. A row marked `derived` was computed from verified numbers. A row marked `no` was seen only in search snippets and must not be cited as verified.

## 1. UN peacekeeping is the closest real-world benchmark

| Metric | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Uniformed personnel, 30 Sep 2025 | **59,376** (50,963 troops, 5,852 police, 934 observers, 1,627 staff officers) | UN Fact Sheet Sep 2025 |
| Total personnel, 11 operations | 67,783 | same |
| Contributing countries | 121 | same |
| Budget for 2024-25 | **USD 5.6 bn** | UN "How we are funded" page |
| Uniformed personnel, 31 Mar 2015 | **106,595**, total 125,396, 16 operations | UN Fact Sheet Mar 2015 |
| Budget for 2014-15 | USD 8.47 bn | same |
| Peak, April 2015 | about 108,000 uniformed | Pew Research 2016 |
| Cost per uniformed peacekeeper | USD 94k (2024-25), USD 79k (2014-15) | derived |
| Troop reimbursement | USD 1,428 per soldier per month | UN |

**Effectiveness.** Hultman, Kathman & Shannon (2013, AJPS) studied African civil wars from 1991 to 2008. They found that when more UN troops and police are deployed, fewer civilians are targeted. Several thousand troops plus several hundred police substantially reduce killings. Walter, Howard & Fortna (2021, BJPolS) reviewed the quantitative studies and describe "an extraordinary relationship between peacekeepers and peace". Both claims were checked against the papers' abstracts.

## 2. Disaster response

- **Haiti 2010:** the US deployed 21,493 armed-forces personnel within two weeks, with 25 ships and 79 helicopters (HASC).
- **2004 tsunami:** the US had more than 13,000 military personnel in Operation Unified Assistance by 7 January 2005 (White House). A later figure of about 16,000 could not be checked because the source returned an error.
- **Türkiye 2023:** about 5,000 international rescuers in 90 teams (INSARAG). Two other figures appeared only in search snippets: 199 teams in total, and 249,000 domestic responders (AFAD).
- **EU Civil Protection Mechanism:** the EU states plus 10 participating states. It was activated 64 times in 2025 and more than 900 times since 2001. Its budget for 2021-27 is EUR 3.32 bn, about EUR 0.47 bn a year.
- **Humanitarian workforce:** about 632,000 staff work in crisis contexts, 90% of them nationals of the country (ALNAP SOHS 2022, as reported secondhand).

So even the largest disasters needed tens of thousands of military personnel at peak, not hundreds of thousands. Most disaster work can stay with the civilian Protection Service.

## 3. Police per 100,000 people (for regional police, not the Peace Force)

- A UN analysis put the median at about **300 per 100,000** in 2006. This is cited secondhand via Wikipedia, because the UN primary document could not be fetched.
- Functioning states range from about 130 to 350 per 100,000. Examples: Finland 132, India 155, Iceland 184, USA 242, Japan 255, Costa Rica 297, Panama 315, Germany 349.
- The **"UN recommended 222 per 100,000" figure is UNVERIFIED.** No UN document stating it was found, so do not cite it.
- At 300 per 100,000, the world would have about 24 million police. That is roughly 50 times the central Peace Force.

## 4. Countries without armies

- **Costa Rica:** Article 12 of the 1949 Constitution says "The Army as a permanent institution is abolished." It keeps police for public order, and any military force must be subordinate to civil power. There has been no coup since 1949, although Wikipedia notes that crediting the abolition for this is disputed.
- **Panama:** the military was abolished in 1990, and a 1994 constitutional ban followed. Security is split between the national police, SENAFRONT (borders) and SENAN (sea and air). These facts come only from search snippets and were not verified.
- **Iceland:** the only NATO member with no standing army. It was a founding member in 1949. A US defence force was stationed there from 1951 to 2006, and the Coast Guard has about 280 staff.
- The World Bank records **0% of GDP** military spending for all three countries from 2020 to 2024. It still counts 10,000 armed personnel for Costa Rica and 28,000 for Panama, because their public forces are paramilitary. **Lesson:** "no army" still means armed police and border units, plus an outside guarantee of security. Under UNIRA, One Earth would provide that guarantee.

## 5. Today's military, for comparison

- **Armed forces:** 27,406,000 people in 2020 (World Bank, MS.MIL.TOTL.P1; 2020 is the latest non-null year).
- **Military spending:** USD 2,887 bn in 2025, which is **2.5% of world GDP**, or USD 352 per person (SIPRI April 2026, re-checked against the PDF).
- **Cost per member:** world military spending divided by armed-forces personnel is about USD 105k per year.

## 6. Coups and civilian control

- **Powell & Thyne (2011, JPR) dataset:** a coup is an overt attempt by the military or other elites to unseat the executive. A coup counts as successful if its leaders hold power for at least 7 days. There were 7 coups between 2008 and 2010, and almost three dozen in that decade. Coup attempts have become less frequent since the end of the Cold War (Defense One, using the same data).
- **Belkin & Schofer (2003, JCR):** structural coup risk is driven by weak civil society, low legitimacy and a history of past coups.
- **De Bruin (2018, JCR):** counterbalancing, meaning several independent armed forces, **reduces coup success but not coup attempts.** Creating a new force **raises the odds of an attempt the following year.** For UNIRA, this means the transition is the period of highest risk, and armed regional police act as a counterweight.
- **Powell (2012, JCR):** found that coup-proofing deters coups. This was seen only in a search snippet and is unverified.

## 7. Proposed ceiling

| | Low | **Central** | High = constitutional ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personnel | 250,000 | **500,000** | 1,000,000 |
| Per 100,000 people | 3.1 | 6.1 | 12.3 |
| Share of today's armed forces | 0.9% | 1.8% | 3.6% |
| Multiple of the 2015 UN peak | 2.3x | 4.7x | 9.4x |
| Cost per member | USD 100k | USD 120k | USD 150k |
| Budget | USD 25 bn | **USD 60 bn** | USD 150 bn |
| Share of world GDP | 0.02% | **0.05%** | 0.13% (ceiling set at 0.15%) |
| Freed personnel | 27.16 m | **26.9 m** | 26.4 m |
| Freed spending (gross) | 2.48 pp | **2.45 pp** | 2.37 pp |

**How the central figure was built:**
1. Start from the 2015 UN peak of about 107,000 deployed at once.
2. Multiply by about 3 so the force can sustain that deployment through rotation, training and reserve. This multiplier is a planning assumption, not a verified figure. The result is about 320,000.
3. Add a surge reserve for 2-3 simultaneous Haiti-scale disasters (about 20,000 each), plus airlift, logistics and verification of weapons-of-mass-destruction destruction.

The total comes to about 500,000.

**Proposed constitutional wording:** "Personnel shall not exceed 0.0125% of world population, about 1 million, and the budget shall not exceed 0.15% of world output. The actual level is set by law below this ceiling."

**Caveats:**
- The freed spending is a gross figure. It does not subtract transition costs: guaranteed income and retraining for 27 million former soldiers, destruction of weapons of mass destruction, and expanding police in under-policed regions.
- The rotation multiplier and the costs per member are assumptions.

## 8. Safeguards

The full list is in `parameters.json`:
- the hard ceiling;
- no region providing more than 5% of personnel or commanders (UN peacekeeping already draws on 121 countries);
- commanders rotating every 3 years, and members never serving in their own region;
- deployment only with a Court ruling plus a double majority, with a 72-hour emergency limit;
- regional police under regional control as a counterweight;
- logistics and arms depots controlled separately from combat command (a design proposal, not tested by evidence);
- a phased build-up with independent inspection during the risky transition;
- annual public reporting.
